# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter Forty-Seven — Three Months In

He gave notice on a Wednesday.

He had been working toward the decision for two weeks — not toward the decision exactly, more toward the point where the decision had already been made and he just needed to say it out loud to the person it most affected.

Manager Tao was in when he came in Wednesday evening. This was less usual than it had been; Tao had been leaving earlier since the repair work on the back wall, perhaps because the building's issues had reminded him there were things he preferred not to know about. He was eating a meat bun from the stall down the street and reading something on his phone with the expression of a man determined to finish his bun before dealing with the world.

Lin Mushi said: "I need to give three weeks' notice."

Tao looked up. He put his phone down with the careful deliberateness of someone making room for an important conversation. He studied Lin Mushi for a moment with the gaze of an employer who had been observing an employee for eight months and had formed opinions.

"Is everything alright?" Tao asked.

"Yes. I have something else I need to do."

"Something else." Tao's voice had no particular inflection. This was not an accusation; it was the tone of someone gathering information before responding. "What kind of something else?"

"Research," Lin Mushi said. Then, because Tao's expression suggested this was insufficient: "Geological consulting, partly. I've been doing structural assessments on the side. There's demand for it."

Tao looked at him for a moment. "The crack in the back wall."

"I noticed it before the contractor did, yes."

"I always wondered about that." Tao set down his meat bun. "The contractor said you'd come in on your day off to hear the assessment."

"I wanted to verify my method was accurate."

Tao made the sound of someone who had many questions and had decided, on balance, not to ask any of them. "Three weeks," he said.

"Three weeks," Lin Mushi confirmed.

---

He had been treating the Codex as a secondary project. He had been treating it as the thing that occupied him during the dead hours of the night shift, as the thing that got the afternoon and the evenings but not the undivided whole of his waking attention. He had been treating it as a hobby because treating it as more than a hobby had felt like a claim he hadn't earned the right to make.

He had earned the right.

He had ten channels open. He had decoded three and a half Codex documents. He had mapped the Under-Web across two kilometres of Chengdu. He had performed the bilateral exchange with the substrate network and confirmed the response. He had two months of documented systematic investigation and a methodology that was working.

He was not doing this as a hobby.

He had been doing it as a vocation for ten weeks and calling it a hobby because calling it a vocation required acknowledging that his life had fundamentally changed shape, and that acknowledgment was the one thing he'd been slower to make than he'd been slow to make anything.

---

He completed his notice on the usual forms and wrote "personal research" in the field marked "reason for leaving." He signed it.

He walked home through the November night, which had gotten cold in the way Chengdu cold got — not sharp, just damp and persistent, the kind of cold that came through rather than hitting you — and thought about what it meant to have made a decision about his life.

He had savings for three months. Possibly four if he cut expenses. The geological consulting work had so far been informal — a referral from the contractor to a property developer with a problem foundation, one assessment completed, one invoice sent, payment received last week. If he treated it seriously, it would cover costs. It would have to.

He had a node at thirty percent capacity and an investigator in the city and a threshold he'd crossed once and would cross again tonight and two more channels to open before the full circuit was complete.

He had an enormous amount of work.

He thought about his mother, who would want to know why he'd quit, and what he would say, which would be something true but incomplete, as most of what he said to her was. He thought about Gao, who already knew, who had said "okay" and meant it.

He thought about Ren Wuji, who had died spending the last of four hundred years on something that had no ordinary name.

He thought: I am not doing this because I have to. I am doing it because it is the most important thing I have ever found, and it requires my full attention, and I am ready to give it.

This was not heroism. This was just a decision, made by a person who had reached the point where any other decision would have been a lie.

He went home. He opened his notebook.

Three weeks until his last shift. He had two channels remaining before the complete twelve-channel circuit, and a threshold he'd already proved he could cross, and a library below the city that was waiting for him to understand it.

He started with channel eleven.
