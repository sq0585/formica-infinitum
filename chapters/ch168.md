# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter One Hundred and Sixty-Eight — The Iron Meridian's Emergency Council

The Iron Meridian Court's Elder Council convened an emergency session four days after the document's release.

Chen Yufei was not invited — her expulsion remained formally in effect, regardless of what the cultivation world's foundations were currently doing — but Elder Zhao briefed her afterward, the same evening, with the specific thoroughness of someone who had decided that informal channels deserved the same accuracy as formal ones.

"Three senior practitioners had already independently verified the document's claims before the session began," Elder Zhao told her. "Including myself. I confirmed the meridian architecture comparison against records the Court has kept since its founding."

"And the Council's position?"

"Split. Four votes to suppress — formal denial of the document's authenticity, public statement attributing it to a fabrication intended to destabilize the cultivation world, internal directive prohibiting any practitioner from engaging with it. Two votes to acknowledge — accept the document's accuracy, begin the difficult work of reconciling the Court's practice and teaching with the corrected understanding." A pause. "Four to two. Suppression won."

Chen Yufei relayed this to Lin Mushi that evening, and he sat with it for a while before responding.

"They're going to deny something three of their own senior practitioners have privately confirmed is true," he said.

"Yes."

"That's not sustainable."

"No," Elder Zhao had told her, and Chen Yufei repeated it now. "It buys time. Denial usually does, in the short term. It doesn't resolve anything. The truth doesn't disappear because four people voted that it should be treated as if it had."

He thought about Document 7's framing of the Colony Link — how a collective's field-level state could be read at sufficient Compression. He thought about institutions as a kind of artificial collective, one that could, in principle, hold a coherent false belief the way an individual could, sustained by the specific mechanics of authority and consequence that kept dissenting individuals quiet even when they privately knew better.

"What happens to the three who verified it?" he asked.

"Elder Zhao said they're being watched. Not formally disciplined yet — the Council can't discipline people for privately confirming something true without admitting the thing they confirmed matters. But watched. Their access to certain Court resources is being quietly restricted."

He felt the familiar weight settle — the responsibility he had told Kuo he would carry, now landing on three specific people he'd never met, whose names he didn't have, who had simply done the honest thing and were now paying a quiet institutional cost for it.

"I want their names," he said. "Not to contact them directly — that might make things worse. To understand who's bearing the cost, so I can think about whether there's anything useful I can do."

Chen Yufei nodded. She would ask Elder Zhao.

"The suppression vote," he said. "What does it mean practically? Will the Iron Meridian try to prevent practitioners from reading the document?"

"Probably. Whether they can enforce it is a different question. The document is already circulating well beyond the original distribution list — Wei Canglan's situation alone has generated significant secondary spread within the Northern Peak, and informal practitioner networks move faster than institutional control measures."

He thought about the Streisand effect, a concept from his pre-cultivation reading life that suddenly felt directly relevant. Suppression, attempted publicly and unsuccessfully, often did more to spread information than simply letting it circulate would have.

"They're making a strategic error," he said. "Not a moral one necessarily — I understand why they're frightened. But a strategic one. Denial in the face of internal verification doesn't hold. It just delays the reckoning and makes the eventual reckoning harder."

"I agree," Chen Yufei said. "Elder Zhao agrees too. She voted to acknowledge. She's now one of two senior practitioners on record having done so, which puts her in a complicated position."

He thought about Elder Zhao — sixty-three years old, thirty years of careful maintenance work, the Iron Meridian array she'd run on incomplete understanding for decades. She had been one of the first people in the cultivation world to engage with him honestly, months ago, at the tea house with the circuit in the potted plant. She had asked twelve careful questions and not expressed surprise once.

Now she had voted her conscience in a four-to-two loss, and was watching three of her colleagues face quiet institutional pressure for having done the same.

"I need to think about what support looks like," he said. "Not interference. Support, for the people who chose honesty and are paying for it."

"That list is going to get longer," Chen Yufei said quietly. "Before it gets shorter."

She was right.
