# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 455 — Full Disclosure — "I Was Afraid"

He told her everything, and it took considerably longer than he expected, and considerably less courage than he'd spent the whole week dreading it would require.

He started at the beginning, because there was no other honest place to start — a convenience store, a dying man's hand closing around his, a Chitin Seed and a Codex he hadn't understood for months. He told her about the Strata, about the twenty-four hundred people now building lives inside something he'd shaped from nothing. He told her about Chen Yufei, about the Anchors, about Colony Link and Planetary Architect and, finally, carefully, about the thing that had happened eight weeks ago in a pocket dimension laboratory while she'd been calling an administrative line twice, worried, and being told, gently, that he was fine.

She sat across from him at the same kitchen table she'd fed him breakfast at for thirty years, tea going cold between them the way it always did whenever something in this family actually mattered, and she listened without interrupting, her face carrying an expression he found he couldn't fully read — not disbelief, he realized slowly. Something closer to a woman finally being handed the missing pieces of a puzzle she'd been quietly assembling on her own for longer than he'd given her credit for.

When he finished, the silence that followed lasted long enough that he started, despite everything, to brace himself.

"Some of it I'd already guessed," she said finally. "Not the scale of it. Not — stars, and civilizations, and whatever the Formicans actually are. But I've known for years you were carrying something larger than a convenience store job could explain. A mother notices these things, even when her son is very careful."

"I'm sorry," he said, and found the words arrived smaller than the feeling underneath them. "I should have told you so much sooner."

She was quiet again, studying him with the particular attention he remembered from childhood, the look she'd given him whenever he'd come home with a torn jacket and a story that didn't quite explain it. "Why didn't you?" she asked. Not accusing. Simply, genuinely, wanting to know.

He'd expected the question to be harder to answer than it turned out to be, once he actually let himself reach for the true version instead of a gentler one.

"I was afraid," he said.

"Of what?"

He looked at her — really looked, the way he'd learned to look at everything difficult over five years of practice — and found the answer waiting, already fully formed, truer than anything he'd said to anyone else in the whole of this extraordinary life.

"That you wouldn't recognize me."

She didn't respond immediately. She reached instead for her tea, long since gone cold, and drank it anyway, the way she drank tea regardless of temperature whenever she needed a moment to hold something before speaking. When she finally set the cup down, her eyes were bright in a way he hadn't seen from her since his father's funeral, years before any of this had begun.

"Lin Mushi," she said, using his full name the way she only ever did when something mattered enormously, "do you understand what you just told me? You didn't say you were afraid I'd be angry. You didn't say you were afraid I'd stop loving you, or be frightened, or think you'd become something dangerous. You said you were afraid I wouldn't recognize you."

"Yes," he said quietly.

"That is the most honest thing you have ever said to me in your entire life," she said, "and I think you should know that, whatever else comes out of tonight."

He felt something in his chest loosen that had been held tight for so long he'd stopped noticing the effort of holding it, five years of careful protection finally, quietly, setting itself down.

"I don't know what to say to that," he admitted.

"You don't have to say anything else tonight," she said, reaching across the table and taking his hand the way she had a thousand times before, through scraped knees and school troubles and every ordinary difficulty a mother spends a lifetime steadying. "I heard you. That's enough for right now."

They sat together a while longer, the kitchen's familiar warmth settling around them both, and he understood, watching her hold his hand exactly the way she always had, that whatever came next — the questions still waiting, the extension protocol still unexplained, the entire shape of what she'd now have to learn to hold — this part, at least, the truest and most frightening part, was already, gently, behind him.
