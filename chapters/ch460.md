# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 460 — What Remains the Same

The final session concluded on an ordinary Tuesday afternoon, without ceremony, in the same small monitoring room Dr. Shen Yue had occupied for three months of careful, patient observation.

Lin Mushi had expected, despite everything Meen-4 had told him, some visible marker of the change — a straighter posture, perhaps, or a particular brightness he associated, unfairly, with every dramatic transformation the stories he'd grown up on had trained him to anticipate. His mother sat up from the low recovery bench, rolled her shoulders once, and looked, in every way he could actually perceive, exactly like the woman who had walked into the Strata's residential wing three months earlier.

"Well," she said, testing her knees with a small, experimental bend, "that's certainly anticlimactic."

"How do you feel?" Dr. Shen Yue asked, running a final check across her instruments.

"Considerably better, if I'm honest." His mother straightened fully, something in the movement noticeably freer than it had been in months. "The ache in my knees is gone. Actually gone, not just quieter. And my chest doesn't feel like it's working quite so hard just to get me across a room."

"Coherence readings confirm it," Dr. Shen Yue said, satisfied. "Full recalibration achieved, no complications across the entire protocol. You responded better than the historical average throughout."

"I told you she would," Meen-4 said, entering the room with the particular quiet pride Lin Mushi had watched grow across three months of shared agricultural conversations. "Some people are simply built to receive this kind of care well. It has less to do with the protocol and more to do with the person underneath it."

His mother examined her own hands for a moment, turning them over, flexing her fingers experimentally, and then looked up at Lin Mushi with an expression he recognized immediately — the particular, dry amusement she reserved for moments when reality had failed to live up to its own hype.

"I don't look any different," she observed.

"You're not supposed to," he said. "That was the whole point. You're exactly who you were. You just have considerably more time left to be her."

She considered that for a moment, and then, apparently, arrived at her verdict.

"You could have just called more," she said.

He blinked, genuinely uncertain, for a moment, whether he'd heard her correctly. "What?"

"These past five years. All the careful protecting, all the elaborate secrecy, months spent worrying about how to tell me, weeks explaining a medical procedure that turned out to be considerably less dramatic than the buildup suggested." She fixed him with the exact look she'd given him as a teenager whenever he'd overcomplicated something simple. "You could have just called more. Visited more. None of this required forty million years of Formican civilization to solve. It required a son who picked up the phone."

He stared at her, and then, despite everything — despite five years of careful weight finally, entirely, set down — he started laughing, the sound arriving from somewhere he hadn't accessed in longer than he could remember, genuine and unguarded and slightly disbelieving.

"That's your response," he said, once he'd recovered enough to speak. "After everything. Stellar Scale, the Collective, an entire hidden civilization, decades of additional life — your actual complaint is that I didn't call enough."

"It's the truest complaint I have," she said, entirely serious despite the dryness of her delivery. "Everything else, I understand, more or less, in the way I'm always going to understand it — from the outside, patiently, learning as I go. But the calling, I understood perfectly well the entire time. You simply didn't do enough of it."

Meen-4, watching the exchange with visible delight, caught his eye briefly. "I believe," she said, "that is what your mother would call getting the last word."

"She usually does," he admitted, still smiling, and reached out to help her down from the recovery bench, feeling the particular steadiness in her grip that hadn't been there three months earlier, and the entirely unchanged, entirely familiar weight of a mother who had, once again, found exactly the right words to make an enormous moment feel, somehow, perfectly ordinary.

"Now," she said, straightening her coat with brisk satisfaction, "take me home. I have an eastern garden bed that has waited three years, and I finally have both the knees and the knowledge to actually fix it properly."
