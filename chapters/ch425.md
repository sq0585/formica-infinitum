# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 425 — The Strata City Receives Visitors

The Chinese Academy of Sciences delegation arrived on a morning Lin Mushi had spent most of the previous week quietly dreading, not because he doubted the protocol they'd built would hold, but because there was something irreducibly strange about preparing to walk three strangers through a civilization that had, until recently, existed entirely outside the world they came from.

Wei Canglan volunteered to handle the escort, which surprised Lin Mushi slightly until he understood the reasoning behind it. "You'll want to talk too much," Wei Canglan said, with the particular bluntness Lin Mushi had come to rely on. "Explain everything, justify everything, manage their reactions the whole way through. I'll just show them the place. Let it speak for itself."

The delegation was small — three researchers, carefully selected, Fang Ruxue had confirmed, for their specific expertise in geology, materials science, and biological systems rather than for any political significance. They arrived escorted through the same discreet channel the protocol had established, and Lin Mushi watched, from a careful distance, as Wei Canglan led them through the Strata's central sections with the same unhurried, understated manner he brought to everything.

Two hours, mostly silent observation. He'd expected more questions, more visible astonishment, and found himself, watching from the administrative chamber's monitoring feed, genuinely moved by how carefully restrained the delegation's reactions actually were — not indifference, he could see that clearly even through the feed, but the particular controlled composure of scientists trained, across years of careful field discipline, to observe thoroughly before allowing themselves to react.

He met them briefly, near the end of the visit, in the same communal chamber that had once held the Strata's largest, most difficult community meeting. He kept his own explanation short, deliberately, trusting the two hours of direct observation to have already communicated more than any speech he might offer.

"We have a lot of questions," the delegation's senior researcher said finally, an older woman whose expression, throughout the brief exchange, had carried something Lin Mushi read as genuine, professional curiosity held carefully in check.

"Yes," he said. "Start with the simpler ones."

She almost smiled at that, something in her composure easing slightly. "The residential pocket-dimension spaces," she said. "The temperature and humidity regulation you mentioned adjusts individually, per resident. Is that automated, or does it require active management?"

He answered it carefully, technically, the way he'd answer any genuine scientific question, and watched her take notes with the particular focused attention of someone who had, evidently, decided the simplest question was also, in its own way, the most revealing one — not the sweeping cosmic implications of pocket-dimension physics, but the practical, human detail of how a civilization actually cared for the people living inside it.

More questions followed, over the following hour, each one carefully chosen, each one answered as honestly as he could manage without violating the protocol's careful boundaries around sensitive information. He found himself, gradually, relaxing into the exchange — not the anxious, high-stakes negotiation he'd braced for, but something closer to an ordinary, if unusually strange, scientific consultation.

"Thank you for your time," the senior researcher said, as the visit finally wound toward its end, the delegation preparing to be escorted back out through the same discreet channel they'd arrived through. "I suspect this is the first of many such conversations."

"I suspect you're right," he said.

He watched them go, escorted by Wei Canglan with the same unhurried care he'd brought to the entire visit, and found himself thinking, once the chamber had emptied again, about how ordinary the whole exchange had ultimately felt, despite everything extraordinary underlying it. Three scientists, asking careful questions, taking careful notes, leaving with more questions than they'd arrived with — the same unglamorous, patient curiosity that had driven his own understanding of this world for three years, now, for the first time, meeting him from the other direction.

"How did it go," Chen Yufei asked, finding him still sitting quietly in the emptied chamber.

"Better than I expected," he admitted. "Quieter. More like scientists actually doing science than like a diplomatic crisis I needed to carefully manage."

"That's a good sign," she said. "The world doesn't need to meet us with fear, if we keep giving it room to meet us with curiosity instead."

He thought about that, about the careful, deliberate two hours of observation, the simple, practical first question about temperature regulation, and found, turning it over, that he agreed — cautiously, provisionally, but genuinely — that curiosity, given the chance, might yet prove a steadier foundation for whatever came next than fear ever could.
