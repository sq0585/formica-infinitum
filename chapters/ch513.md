# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 513 — Liang Jingzhi, Reached

He had spoken with her a thousand times through the Colony Link since the day her permanent extension had completed — brief check-ins, careful monitoring reports, the specific dry humor she brought to even the most technically demanding updates. He had trusted every one of those exchanges completely. He understood now, reaching her fully for the first time, that he had never once actually perceived her the way the rest of the Anchors had always been able to perceive one another.

He found her exactly where she had always said she'd be.

A Chengdu office, mid-afternoon, a cold cup of coffee at her elbow and a stack of geological survey data open on a second monitor she was, he noted with some fondness, not currently looking at. And, simultaneously, without any seam between the two states that his perception could find no matter how carefully he searched for one, the zone's actual center — vast, warm, permanently woven into the same structure the residual had spent months learning to reach toward.

Both were her. Neither was more true than the other.

"You found me properly this time," she said, and he felt the words arrive in both places at once, the office and the center, indistinguishable, undivided. "Everyone else just gets the report."

*Observation 513,* he wrote, though he found himself, for the length of the entry, more inclined to simply feel the moment than to analyze it. *I have read every report Liang Jingzhi has filed for the past several months. I understand now that a report, however careful, however honest, is not remotely the same thing as this. She has been telling the truth the entire time. I only now have the capacity to know it directly rather than to trust it secondhand.*

"You're really both," he said, out loud, the sound of his own voice landing strange and small against the enormity of what he was perceiving.

"I've been both for months," she said, mild, the same plain accuracy she'd given him from the very beginning of everything. "You're only now capable of noticing I wasn't exaggerating."

He laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of him, and felt it land, warm, in the office and in the center simultaneously, the coffee cup catching a fraction of light it hadn't a moment before, some infinitesimal shift in the room that he understood, without being told, was simply her, present, undiminished, exactly where she said she'd be.

"I owe you an apology," he said.

"You don't."

"I think I do. I've spent months treating what you did as something I needed to carry carefully — a weight I was responsible for, on your behalf, because you couldn't fully carry the explanation of it to me yourself. I understand now that wasn't generosity on my part. It was a failure of perception. You were never asking me to carry anything. You were just telling me the truth, and I didn't yet have the scale to actually hear it."

She was quiet a moment, the specific quiet he had learned, over months, to recognize as her considering something honestly rather than simply agreeing to be kind.

"That's fair," she said finally. "I'll accept the apology on the condition that you stop offering me any more of them. I made a decision. It was mine to make. I have never once regretted it, and I would like, at some point, for the people who love me to actually believe that instead of quietly, carefully managing it on my behalf."

"I believe it," he said, and found, saying it, that he finally, completely did.

"Good." Something in her presence warmed, both halves of her at once, the office and the center, indistinguishable. "Now. You didn't come all this way — however that phrase means anything to either of us anymore — just to apologize. What did you actually come to do?"

He told her, and felt, telling her, the particular satisfaction of a plan finally arriving at the one person capable of receiving it fully.

She listened the way she listened to everything — carefully, without interrupting, the same disciplined patience that had once, years ago, made her the kind of geologist who could sit with an unexplained seismic anomaly for months rather than force it into a premature explanation. He watched her consider the shape of what he was proposing, turning it the way she turned any data set, testing it for the places it might not hold.

"You want to combine what I'm already holding with whatever this new scale gives you," she said, when he'd finished. "Not to replace the structure. To reinforce it at the edges where my reach alone has been thinnest."

"That's the shape of it, yes."

"And you wanted to ask me directly," she said, "rather than simply do it, because you've learned, finally, the difference between those two approaches."

"I have," he admitted. "It took longer than I'd like to admit."

"It took exactly as long as it needed to," she said, gentler than her usual brisk efficiency allowed. "You were young when this started. You're not young anymore, in any sense that matters, and I don't just mean the scale. Ask the question."

"Will you let me add to what you built? Not take it from you. Add to it."

She held his gaze a long moment, both halves of her present at once, weighing the question with the same care she gave every genuine decision she had ever made.

"Yes," she said, simply, no hesitation once the answer arrived. "Obviously yes. I've been waiting months for someone to finally have the scale to ask me that properly instead of managing it for me."
