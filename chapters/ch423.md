# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 423 — Designing the Protocol

The work took three weeks, and Lin Mushi came to think of it, privately, as one of the hardest sustained projects he'd undertaken in three years — not because any single decision within it was as dramatic as surviving the Collective's review, but because there was no precedent to lean on, no notation to study, no ancient Formican structure to reverse-engineer for guidance. This was something genuinely new, built entirely from careful reasoning and hard-won trust between four people who did not always agree on the details.

Fang Ruxue brought the institutional discipline, the careful drafting, the years of experience building the Collective's own governance from nothing. Lady Shen brought a perspective none of the others fully possessed — the specific, practical understanding of how governments and institutions actually behaved when they believed something valuable was being withheld from them, honed across a lifetime spent navigating exactly that kind of political terrain. Meen-4 brought the long view, centuries of Formican political history, precedent from other zones and other disclosures the Archive's records held, however incomplete. And Lin Mushi brought the thing he'd always brought: a stubborn insistence that whatever they built had to actually be honest, not merely defensible.

They argued more than Lin Mushi expected. Lady Shen pushed, repeatedly, for a protocol that gave governments considerably less unfiltered access than Fang Ruxue believed was fair, arguing that information control, calibrated carefully, was simply how functional relationships between unequal powers were maintained without one side inevitably attempting to dominate the other.

"You're describing exactly the kind of careful concealment we just spent months arguing against, to a body that judges civilizations on their honesty," Lin Mushi said, during one particularly heated session.

"I am describing the difference between honesty and naivety," Lady Shen replied, unbowed. "You can be entirely truthful in what you disclose while still controlling the pace and manner of disclosure. That is not deception. That is competent governance. A government handed everything at once, without structure, will not respond with gratitude. It will respond with fear, and fear produces worse outcomes for everyone than careful, honest pacing ever could."

He sat with that longer than he wanted to admit, recognizing, underneath his initial resistance, the same hard logic that had shaped his own careful, staged disclosure before Dr. Shen Yue's paper had forced everyone's hand. Lady Shen was not arguing for concealment. She was arguing, in her own careful, unsentimental way, for exactly the kind of measured honesty he'd once believed in himself.

"Fine," he said finally. "Structured disclosure. Not controlled to the point of dishonesty. But paced, deliberately, so governments actually have room to absorb what they're being told instead of being overwhelmed the way the public just was."

The protocol that finally emerged, after three weeks of argument and revision, was imperfect in ways all four of them acknowledged openly. Quarterly briefings, structured similarly to the Collective's own reporting requirements, offered to any government that formally requested contact. A clear, published statement of what the Strata would and would not disclose, and why, so no government could reasonably claim they'd been promised more than they received. A standing invitation for scientific verification, supervised but genuine, so claims could be independently confirmed rather than simply taken on faith.

"This is going to need revision," Fang Ruxue said, once the final draft was complete, spread across the table alongside three weeks of crossed-out earlier attempts. "Probably within the first year. We're building this from nothing, and nothing built from nothing survives its first real test without needing adjustment."

"I know," Lin Mushi said. "I think that's all right. We don't need this to be perfect. We need it to be honest enough, and flexible enough, that we can actually fix it when it turns out to be wrong about something."

They implemented it two days later, transmitting the completed protocol to the Chinese Ministry first, and then, in the days that followed, to the other governments whose formal inquiries had continued arriving in a steady, sometimes overwhelming stream. It was not a perfect solution. It was, Lin Mushi understood, simply the best imperfect solution four genuinely thoughtful people had managed to build together, in three weeks, for a problem no one had ever solved before.

"We'll revise it as we learn," he told Meen-4, once it was finally sent. "The same way we've built everything else."

"Yes," she agreed. "I would not have expected anything else from you."
