# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter Twelve — Structural Integrity

The back wall of the store had been bothering him for three nights.

Not visually. Visually it was a convenience store wall — painted beige, scuffed at shoulder height where stock boxes brushed it, slightly discolored in one corner from a water leak that had been repaired two years ago, or so Tao had told him when he'd asked about the stain. Visually, the wall was fine.

But when he stood near the drinks case restocking at 3 AM, the depth sensation did something strange in the area of the back-left corner. It didn't intensify the way it did near the floor. It *fragmented* — as if the smooth directional signal was being interrupted by something that sat between him and whatever the signal was pointing toward. An obstruction. A gap in a material that should have been continuous.

He had been noting this for three nights. Tonight he did something about it.

---

He finished restocking the lower shelf of the drinks case, set the empty box aside, and stood up facing the wall. The store was empty. 3:22 AM. Forty minutes until the next likely customer.

He pressed his palm flat against the painted surface.

Standard wall. Cold from the night. He could feel the slight give of paint over plaster, the rigidity of the cinder block underneath.

He moved his hand left and slightly down, toward the area where the signal fragmented.

The wall felt the same.

He moved his hand again, slower this time, the way you sweep a surface looking for a seam. The depth sensation in his palm shifted — not dramatically, just a slight change in quality, as if the resistance in the wall was fractionally less than it should be for the material.

He stopped with his hand at roughly forty centimeters above the floor, a half-meter left of the corner.

He pressed.

The plaster didn't flex. Solid-feeling. But the depth sensation underneath it told a different story — something on the other side of the surface that wasn't cinder block. A different density. Or the absence of a density.

He took his hand away and looked at the spot.

A hollow. Or water damage. Or both.

He pressed again with two fingers, directly at the center of where the signal felt thinnest.

The plaster cracked.

Not dramatically. A hairline fracture, thin as a line drawn in pencil, running three centimeters diagonally from where his fingertips had rested. He pulled his hand back and stared at it.

The crack looked exactly like a crack that had been waiting to happen — the kind that appears when old water damage has compromised the substrate and the surface is one small pressure away from admitting it.

He wrote in his notebook:

> *3:26 AM. Back-left wall, 40cm above floor, 50cm from corner. Depth-signal fragmentation confirmed as structural void. Pressed with two fingers — hairline crack appeared.*
>
> *The signal was right. The wall was compromised at exactly the location it indicated.*
>
> *I knew before I knew.*

He looked at the crack. He looked at his fingers.

*I knew before I knew.*

---

He reported the crack to Manager Tao in the morning, describing it as something he'd noticed while restocking. Tao sent a message to the building's maintenance contractor. The contractor came on Thursday afternoon, three days later, looked at the crack, removed a section of plaster, and found what he called "significant moisture damage" — a hidden seep that had been eating through the interior substrate for what looked like eighteen months.

"Lucky you noticed it," the contractor said, making a note on his clipboard. "Another year and you'd have had a real problem."

Lin Mushi had not been scheduled to work the day the contractor came. He'd come in on his day off specifically to hear the assessment. He stood near the back wall and listened and did not say: *I didn't notice it. I felt it through an interface I don't fully understand yet, using a perception I'm still learning to calibrate.*

"Glad I did," he said instead.

He bought a coffee from the machine near the door and stood outside in the October afternoon and drank it slowly, watching the cars go past on Wuhou Avenue.

He had felt a structural problem through a wall before he had any visual evidence it existed.

This was either:

a) a remarkable coincidence between perceptual anomaly and actual structural failure.
b) evidence that the depth sensation was detecting something real — that it was a genuine spatial sense, not a malfunction of his own neurology.

He had eliminated (a) on the third time he'd noticed the anomaly and noted its precise location. Three nights of consistent data at the same coordinates was not coincidence. It was signal.

He finished the coffee. He tossed the cup.

He updated his notebook standing on the pavement:

> *Structural void confirmed by contractor: moisture damage, 18 months. Location matches depth-signal fragmentation exactly.*
>
> *The perception is reliable.*
>
> *Implication: I am detecting the actual physical structure of spaces. Not metaphorically. Spatially. I am reading material properties through some mechanism I cannot yet name.*
>
> *Question: what is the range? What is the resolution? What are the limits?*
>
> *Next test: can I detect something I don't already suspect is there?*

He walked home thinking about that. The trees along Wuhou Avenue had just started dropping leaves, slow and golden, the kind of October afternoon that felt unreasonably kind about what it asked you to carry.

He had a perception that worked.

Now he needed to understand what it was perceiving.
