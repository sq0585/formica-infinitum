# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 372 — What Disclosure Looks Like

"People are going to imagine a press conference," Dr. Shen Yue said, three days into planning, tapping her pen against a whiteboard that had somehow accumulated four different colors of half-erased handwriting in the space of one afternoon. "You should get ahead of that expectation now, because whatever we actually do is going to look, by comparison, almost disappointingly small."

"Small is the point," Lin Mushi said.

They had gathered in the same administrative chamber that had produced the Opening Submission — Fang Ruxue, Dr. Shen Yue, Liang Jingzhi, Meen-4 present through the Colony Link rather than in body, her attention split, as always, between a dozen things at once. The kettle had been refilled twice already. It was, Lin Mushi thought, becoming something like a war room, except the war it was preparing for was one fought entirely in the register of careful, incremental honesty.

"Walk me through the actual list," Fang Ruxue said.

Liang Jingzhi went first, because her piece was the most technical and, in some ways, the least fraught. "A follow-up paper. Full explanation of the Strata anchor anomalies — the density readings under Chengdu that started this whole thing for me, three years ago, before I understood what I was actually looking at. I can write it so it reads as an extension of legitimate geological and physics research. Which it is. I'm not going to lie in it. I'm just going to finally tell the whole truth instead of the careful half of it I've been publishing."

"That alone will draw attention," Dr. Shen Yue said. "Combined with mine, it draws considerably more."

"Yours is the framework," Lin Mushi said. It wasn't a question.

"Yes. The Veritonal Energy framework, shared formally with three additional physics institutions — not published outright, not yet, but shared the way real science gets shared before publication: peer contact, private review, invitation to replicate. It gives credible institutions time to verify before the wider world has to take our word for anything." She said it steadily, but Lin Mushi caught the small tightening around her eyes, the same one he'd seen the day she'd first shown him her original paper on subsurface density anomalies. She knew, better than anyone in the room, exactly what this step had already begun to cost her, and what it would cost more of.

"And the Circuit data," he said, turning to the third piece, the one that was his to own. "Real-time access for the Collective's review body. Not humanity's data — theirs. A separate channel, separate from anything the public sees. It's not disclosure to the world. It's transparency toward the thing that's judging us, so the Surveyor's assessment is built on our own numbers instead of secondhand inference."

"Why does that matter," Fang Ruxue asked, though her tone suggested she already suspected the answer and wanted it stated for the room.

"Because trust runs in both directions," he said. "I want the Surveyor to see we have nothing to hide from it specifically, before the rest of the world sees anything at all. If they trust our transparency with them, that becomes evidence in the review, regardless of what happens with the public disclosure track."

Meen-4's voice arrived through the Colony Link, dry as ever. "It is also, I will note, good strategy independent of sentiment. A civilization voluntarily sharing verified data is treated very differently, in review precedent, than one whose data is extracted under pressure. You are correct for more than one reason. This happens rarely enough that I wanted to acknowledge it."

"I'll take the compliment," Lin Mushi said, and meant it as a joke, and mostly succeeded.

The plan, once assembled, looked almost anticlimactic on paper — three specific, targeted actions, each aimed at a narrow, credible audience rather than the world at large. No cameras. No announcement. Just the slow, deliberate widening of a circle that had, for three years, been drawn as tightly as he could manage.

"This is going to cost someone," Dr. Shen Yue said quietly, near the end, when the others had started gathering their things. She wasn't looking at anyone specific when she said it, but Lin Mushi understood, with the particular clarity that came from having learned to read the room the way he read notation, that she meant herself, and that she had already accepted the cost before she'd said the words aloud.

"I know," he said. "I'm sorry it has to."

"Don't be," she said, standing, gathering her laptop with the brisk efficiency of someone closing a subject before it could turn into something softer than she wanted to sit with. "I decided a long time ago that I'd rather be the person who told the truth and paid for it than the one who protected a career built on half of one. You're not making that decision for me. You're just the reason it finally has a deadline."

She left before he could find the right response, which was, he suspected, exactly how she'd wanted it — the kind of exit that didn't ask to be comforted.

He stayed behind a while longer, alone with the whiteboard's four colors of handwriting, and thought about how disclosure, when it actually arrived, would look almost nothing like the world would eventually imagine it had looked. Not a curtain pulled back all at once. Just three careful, honest steps, taken by people who understood exactly what each one would cost, and chose to take it anyway.
