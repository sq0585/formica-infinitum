# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter Two — Incident Report

He slept badly, which was not unusual, and dreamed of amber, which was.

Not the color — or not only the color. The dream had texture: warm resin, ancient and preserving, surrounding him at every angle without pressure. He was falling through it, or it was falling through him. There were structures inside it, crystalline and exact, doing something that felt like language but that he couldn't read and couldn't stop trying to read. He woke at eleven-forty with the specific exhaustion of someone who had been working hard at something for eight hours and retained nothing.

He lay on his back and looked at the water stain on his ceiling.

Then he looked at his phone.

The hexagonal icon was still there. Still faint. It had not resolved itself into something normal overnight, which he had been half-hoping for and half-not.

He put the phone face-down on the mattress and went to make tea.

---

The police called at twelve-fifteen. A different officer — older, form-averse, with the manner of someone who had dealt with unexpected deaths long enough to find them routine. A few follow-up questions. Had Lin Mushi seen the man before? No. Had the man said anything? No. Was there anything else he remembered?

Lin Mushi thought about the hand movement. He said, "No."

"The deceased carried no identification," the officer said. "No phone, no wallet, no keys. Fingerprints returned no match on record." A pause. "That's unusual."

"Yes," Lin Mushi agreed.

"If you remember anything else, please contact us."

He said he would. He ended the call. He stood in his kitchen with his tea going cold and thought: a man with no identification and no fingerprint record. A man who entered a store without triggering the door sensor. A man whose fingers moved in a pattern his eyes registered but his brain could not parse.

He was assembling a list of things that did not fit. He had always been good at that.

He got his second notebook — the one from the desk, not the one under his bed; he kept two — and wrote at the top of a fresh page:

> _No ID. No fingerprints. No match on record. Entered without triggering motion sensor. Hand movement pre-death. App on phone. Dream._
>
> _Possible explanations: (1) elaborate coincidence. (2) medical episode causing perceptual distortion. (3) something I don't have a category for yet._
>
> _Working hypothesis: (3). Reason: the app is still there._

He looked at what he'd written. Then added below:

> _The dream felt like information. This is also not useful but also true._

---

He went in for his shift at ten PM.

Manager Tao was counting the till, which was unusual. Tao typically left before ten, having the healthy relationship with the night shift that consisted of never thinking about it. He looked up when Lin Mushi came through the back door, and something moved across his face that Lin Mushi couldn't immediately categorize.

"Last night," Tao said.

"Yes."

"You're alright?"

"Yes."

Tao nodded slowly, the way people nod when they want to communicate that they've acknowledged a difficulty without knowing how to address it. "If you need time—"

"I don't." Lin Mushi tied on his apron. "Was there paperwork?"

There was paperwork. The incident report form had a section for "unusual circumstances" that he left blank, which was not accurate but was the only available choice.

Tao left at ten-fifteen. The store's customers came and went — a university student, a delivery driver, a slightly drunk couple who were cheerful about it. Lin Mushi rang them through and made change and said the appropriate things and watched the clock.

At 2:09 AM, the store was empty again.

He took the phone out of his pocket.

The hexagonal icon pulsed, very slightly, when he looked at it. Not like a notification. More like the way a screen adjusts brightness when ambient light changes — automatic, responsive, without demanding attention.

He had been observing for twenty-three hours.

He opened the app.

---

The interface was not a menu.

He had been half-expecting a menu — options, settings, something to navigate. What opened instead was closer to a diagram, but diagrams implied something static and this was not static. It moved in a way almost too slow to perceive, like a clock's minute hand — motion only obvious in retrospect. The structure was hexagonal at every scale, nested geometries repeating at different sizes, amber-gold lines on a dark background that might have been meant to suggest depth.

There was one message waiting. A separate layer, a sheet of text over the diagram, in characters he almost recognized — something between notation and calligraphy, tugging at the edge of meaning without quite resolving.

He stared. His eyes ached slightly, the way they did when reading something too far away.

Then, without transition, he could read it.

Not because the characters changed. Because something in him did, subtly, the way a brain sometimes solves a puzzle between sleep and waking and presents the answer without the intermediate steps. He read:

> _You are reading this because I am dead. I apologize for the inconvenience._
>
> _My name would mean nothing to you. What matters is what I have left you, and why I had no other choice. The world you live in is a surface. Beneath it is everything that has ever mattered. I spent four hundred years learning to navigate what lies below. I had perhaps four minutes left. You were the nearest mind awake enough to receive this._
>
> _I hope you are curious. Everything depends on it._

Below the message, the diagram waited.

Lin Mushi read the message three times. He set the phone on the counter and looked at the acoustic tile ceiling and thought about what it meant to be _the nearest mind awake enough._

Four hundred years. Not possible. And yet.

He picked up the phone again. Looked at the diagram the way he looked at physics papers he didn't yet understand — not forcing comprehension, just letting his eyes rest on the structure, waiting for pattern recognition to find something to hold.

The diagram had layers. He could tell even without understanding it, the way you could tell a city had neighborhoods before you knew any of their names. Layers within layers, each geometric, each connected to the others by lines that seemed to carry information in their direction and weight.

It looked, he realized slowly, like the inside of something alive.

At 3:17 AM — exactly twenty-four hours after the man had entered — Lin Mushi became aware that the air pressure behind his eyes was slightly wrong. Not painful. Off, the way atmosphere before a migraine is off, except the migraine didn't come. Instead he had the distinct impression that the floor beneath the store was not solid.

Not frightening. More the way you might suddenly notice a floor was hollow — a change in acoustics, a different quality of vibration. The floor was still floor. But something below it was not simply more building.

He stood very still.

The impression faded after forty seconds. The floor was just floor again.

He got out his notebook.

> _2:09 AM: opened app. Interface: diagram, not menu. Moving slowly. Geometric. Hexagonal at multiple scales. One message. Read it. Or: it became readable. Not sure those are the same thing._
>
> _Message contents: the man knew he was dying. Transmitted something to me deliberately. Claims to have lived four hundred years. Claims the world has layers. Diagram may be related to 'what lies below.'_
>
> _3:17 AM: pressure shift behind eyes, 40 seconds, painless. Perception of depth beneath store floor — as if something hollow below. Gone now._
>
> _Working hypothesis update: the app is doing something to how I perceive space. This began either when I opened it or has been building since last night. The dream may have been part of this._
>
> _Question: 'the nearest mind awake enough.' Does he mean literally awake, or something else? Was it only proximity and time of night, or was there a different criterion?_
>
> _Question: four hundred years. Is this figurative. If not: what does it mean for the timeline of whatever I've been handed._
>
> _Question: 'everything depends on it.' Depends for whom. On what scale._

He capped the pen. Looked at the diagram again. Did not try to interpret it. Just looked.

When his shift ended at six AM, he walked home in the early light with his hands in his pockets and the phone warm against his thigh and the dream still somewhere behind his eyes — not remembered but present, the amber warmth of it, the sense of structures inside structures, information preserved in resin for longer than he had language for.

The man had chosen him.

He didn't know what that meant yet.

He was going to find out.

He bought a fresh notebook on the way home. Blue cover. Wrote the date on the first page and nothing else. Put it on the desk next to the phone and went to sleep.

He did not dream of amber this time.

He dreamed of depth.
