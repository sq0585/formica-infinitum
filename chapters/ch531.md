# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 531 — The Strata City — What It Is

Eight thousand residents now called the Chengdu Strata home — roughly three thousand Formican, five thousand human, a ratio that had shifted steadily over the past two years without ever once, he noted with some quiet satisfaction, tipping into the kind of imbalance either community had once quietly feared.

He remembered, distantly now, the specific anxiety of the Strata's earliest days — a handful of intentional residents, a single Celestial Compass practitioner and her elderly mother who couldn't climb stairs, the careful, uncertain question of whether a genuinely shared city between two species that had spent forty million years apart was even possible to build, let alone sustain. He had not known, back then, whether eight thousand was a number the project could survive reaching. He understood now, walking its streets on an ordinary afternoon, that it was a number the project had been quietly, patiently built to reach all along.

The city had an elected mayor now — human, a former urban planner named Su Lian who had, in her professional life before the Strata, spent two decades designing public housing for a mid-sized provincial city and had approached the position, from her very first campaign speech, with the specific unglamorous seriousness of someone who understood that governing a city was mostly a matter of drainage, schools, and honest budgeting, regardless of how extraordinary the city in question happened to be.

Meen-4 was not the mayor. He had half expected, in the earliest days of the city's formal governance, that she might eventually be pressed toward some administrative role commensurate with her age and her standing among the Formican community — and had been quietly relieved, watching her decline every such suggestion with characteristic formal clarity, when she chose instead to remain the Strata's chief archivist, a role she had, by her own careful account, held in one form or another for longer than any human institution had existed to compare it against.

*Observation 531,* he wrote. *Meen-4 has updated her records four times in the past year alone. She described this to me, without any apparent awareness of how remarkable the statement actually was, as unprecedented in forty million years. I asked her whether the pace troubled her. She said, plainly, that it was the first time in her very long life that her records had ever needed updating faster than she could comfortably keep pace with, and that she considered the difficulty, on balance, the best problem she had ever been given to solve.*

He walked the Strata's growing residential quarters that afternoon, the specific pleasure of watching a city he'd helped build simply continue, unremarkably, to be a city — children moving between human and Formican households with the easy fluency of people who had never known a world where the distinction mattered as much as it once had; a shared market where human vendors and Formican craftspeople had, over two years, developed their own particular economy of barter and mutual specialty, entirely without his intervention; a communal garden, recently expanded after considerable and genuinely heated debate over kitchen placement, that had somehow, in the end, made room for both factions' preferences without anyone actually losing the argument outright.

He passed Wei Canglan's office, its door open, a small queue of residents waiting patiently with infrastructure concerns that had once, in the Strata's earliest days, been Lin Mushi's own to personally resolve, one at a time, in the specific overwhelmed manner of someone building something he did not yet fully understand the scale of. He no longer needed to resolve any of it personally. The city had grown its own competent hands, its own careful institutions, its own capacity to solve its own small, ordinary problems without requiring him at the center of every solution.

He found, watching it, that this was exactly what he had always secretly hoped building the Strata would eventually mean.

He stopped, on his way back through the eastern quarter, at the drainage repair he'd made months earlier — the streets still dry, still unremarkable, a resident's folding chair set out in the same spot he remembered noticing on the evening he'd fixed it. He did not linger there long. There was nothing left to check. That, he understood, standing a moment in the ordinary afternoon light, was itself the entire point.

He thought about the number again, walking the rest of the way home — eight thousand, up from four thousand two hundred at the close of the last threshold, a doubling that had happened not through any single dramatic event but through two years of patient, ordinary accretion: families arriving, children born, a Formican community finally, cautiously, willing to trust that the arrangement would hold. He understood the number would keep climbing. He found he was no longer anxious about how large it might eventually become, the way he once might have been. The city had proven, thoroughly, that it knew how to grow without losing what had made it worth building in the first place.
