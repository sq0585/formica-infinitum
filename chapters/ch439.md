# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 439 — Flagging the Anomaly

The standard classifications took him the better part of three weeks to finish properly, working through each stellar type the message had specified, building the comprehension outward from the Sun's own familiar pattern to the wider variety the record described — hotter stars, cooler ones, binaries whose Veritonal fields interacted in ways that made the earlier lessons on resonance and dampening finally click into something he could apply rather than merely understand in the abstract.

He was, by the third week, reading the record almost fluently — not effortlessly, nothing about this had ever been effortless, but with the particular confident momentum of someone who has finally built enough foundation that new information lands as extension rather than confusion.

Then the classifications simply ended, and the record's structure changed.

He noticed it first as a shift in the notation's density — the careful, measured entries of the standard sections giving way, abruptly, to something more compressed, more urgent in a way that was hard to name precisely but impossible to miss once he'd seen it. Where the earlier sections had unfolded at the crew's own unhurried pace, decades of patient observation laid out methodically, this final section read like something written quickly, by people who had understood, even as they wrote it, that they might not have unlimited time left to finish.

A single coordinate anchored the entire section. Solar position 7.4-N — a designation in the crew's own reference frame, one he cross-checked twice against the installation's navigational data before trusting it, a location somewhere in the outer reaches of what he was starting to understand as the crew's own extended survey range, considerably further out than anything the standard classifications had covered.

*Observation 391: the entire final section of the archive is organized around a single flagged location. Every entry from this point forward references it directly or indirectly. This is not one anomaly among many. This is, by the record's own internal structure, the reason the archive exists in its current form at all.*

He worked through the flagged entries carefully, and found the annotations, once he'd parsed the notation's shift in register, carried a weight the earlier sections hadn't — cross-references, corrections, entries revised multiple times as if the crew kept coming back to reconsider what they'd already recorded, the specific pattern of people circling something they didn't yet fully understand and couldn't quite stop returning to.

*Priority flag, recurring throughout:* he translated, checking the phrase three separate times against the notation's grammar before trusting it. *"Requires direct investigation. Remote observation insufficient."*

He sat with that a long moment, feeling the particular quiet dread of a sentence that had, evidently, led somewhere.

The annotations didn't say what 7.4-N actually was. That was, he realized after several hours of careful cross-referencing, deliberate — the flagged entries described the anomaly's effect on surrounding measurements exhaustively, the way it distorted nearby stellar readings, the way instruments calibrated for ordinary observation returned inconsistent results the closer the crew's own remote sensors got to it, but never, in any entry he could find, a direct description of the thing itself. As if whatever it was resisted remote characterization the same way it apparently resisted everything else about being understood from a comfortable distance.

*Rule,* he wrote, and felt the specific weight of writing it. *Whatever 7.4-N is, an entire expedition of Formican researchers — people who had, by every measure available to me, spent decades successfully building one of the most patient and thorough curricula I have ever encountered — considered it significant enough to organize their final work entirely around, and cautious enough that not one of their surviving records describes it directly.*

He checked the relay chain — stable, a brief message from Chen Yufei, *how's the reading going,* nothing that suggested urgency on the terrestrial end — and found himself, for the first time since arriving at the installation, genuinely uncertain about what came next.

The message had told him to end with the anomaly. He had reached it now, technically, in the sense that the standard classifications were finished and this was, by the archive's own structure, the next thing waiting. But *end with* and *understand fully* were not, he suspected, the same instruction, and the crew's own careful annotations — priority flags, repeated revisions, an entire section organized around something they'd never once described directly — read less like an invitation to proceed and more like a door someone had left carefully, deliberately, only partway open.

He closed the notebook, checked the relay chain once more, and sat a long while in the installation's quiet cold, trying to decide exactly how much further the sequence the message had specified actually wanted him to go.
