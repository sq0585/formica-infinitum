# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter Eighty-Two — The Iron Meridian Court

She took him to see it on a Wednesday afternoon.

Not inside — she didn't take him inside. She walked him past it, on the opposite side of the street, in the manner of a person who happened to be walking on this street for reasons that had nothing to do with the building she was not pointing at.

"Third floor," she said, not looking up. "The tea house façade is real. The building above it is not."

He looked.

The building was five storeys, a 1990s construction with recent renovation — new windows, fresh paint, a lobby visible through glass. Standard Chengdu commercial building. At the third floor, where Chen Yufei had indicated, his Qi perception found something that would not have been visible to him six months ago and was now clearly wrong: the external Qi field of the building ran normally up to the second floor, then changed character above that. Not blocked, not hidden. Structured. Deliberately organized, the way a garden was organized rather than the way a wall was organized.

Someone had shaped the Qi field of the upper floors into a functional architecture.

He walked slowly.

The structure above the third floor was — he reached for accurate description — a node-tapping array. A network of Qi channels built into the building's physical materials (the walls, he suspected, specifically the load-bearing elements, which would have the most Veritonal contact with the earth below), all oriented toward and drawing from a concentrated point below the building's foundation. The same tributary of the Under-Web he'd been mapping for months, tapped at a different location.

It worked. It was undeniably functional. It was also, by the Codex's standards, approximately as efficient as drinking water through a sock.

"They've been building this for how long?" he asked.

"The current configuration: about forty years. There have been predecessor structures on this site for three hundred."

"Three hundred years of tapping the same tributary."

"They'd call it *attuning the local meridian,*" she said.

He watched the building for another few seconds. At Ratio 1 with his channels active, he could see the waste — the Qi the array was drawing but not using, dissipating into the ambient field above the building like heat from a poorly insulated structure. Probably sixty, sixty-five percent of what was being drawn was being lost before it reached the practitioners on the upper floors.

"What does the Court do with the Qi it collects?" he asked.

"Cultivation practice. Training. Some maintenance operations on the nodes they're responsible for managing." She paused. "They think of themselves as stewards."

"They're not wrong," he said. "They're just stewards of something they don't fully understand."

She was quiet for a moment.

"That's going to be a difficult thing to tell people," she said.

"I know." He had been thinking about how to tell people for two months. "What's the array's output? Do you know?"

"I don't have a number. What I know is that it powers the cultivation practice of about forty active members in Chengdu, and that the Court has been running this configuration without significant change for forty years."

He thought about the geological consulting work. The stability assessment reports. The structures he'd prevented from failing by finding what was hidden below. He thought about this building, forty years old, drawing from a tributary that had been depleted by the node's collapse and would now be drawing harder to compensate.

"The node that went dark," he said. "The one beneath the store. The tributary runs past here too."

"Yes."

"They've noticed the pressure change."

"Yes." She glanced at him, briefly. "They attributed it to seasonal variation. They're going to realize it isn't when the spring assessment comes."

He filed this. The cascade effects of the node's death were still propagating. He had caused this; he needed to understand the full extent of it.

"The array," he said. "Is it maintained? Does someone have to actively manage it?"

"A senior practitioner runs a maintenance cycle monthly. More often if there's a disruption." She paused. "Currently, Elder Zhao. She's sixty-three, very skilled, has been with the Court for thirty years."

He thought about Elder Zhao, sixty-three years old, doing monthly maintenance on an array she'd inherited and didn't know the origin of, drawing on a tributary she couldn't fully see, serving forty cultivators who practiced something real for reasons that were partially wrong.

He thought: these are not the enemy.

He wrote nothing. He kept walking.

---

Back at the tea house that had become their default meeting point, he opened the biological substrate notebook to the meridian architecture section and put it on the table between them.

"This is what the Iron Meridian technique is actually doing," he said. "At the level of mechanism."

She looked at the page. She had seen parts of it before. The full diagram was more detailed than what she'd seen.

"Walk me through it," she said.

He did.

He was precise and he was complete and he did not soften the parts that were hardest to hear — that the theory was wrong, that the mechanism had never been the metaphysical system the Court described, that the practitioners were operating correctly within a framework that was built on a misunderstanding of its own foundations.

He was also careful to be accurate about what was not wrong: the technique worked. The channels were real. The cultivation practice produced genuine results. The Iron Meridian's practitioners were not deluded — they had found a real thing and practiced it faithfully. They'd just been told the wrong story about why it worked.

She listened without interrupting.

When he finished, she looked at the diagram for a long time.

"They discovered a real thing by accident," she said, finally, "and built a mythology around why it works."

"Yes."

"The mythology is wrong."

"The metaphysics, yes. The technique is real."

She looked up from the diagram. "And you're telling me this because—"

"Because you asked for the honest accounting," he said. "And because you'll practice better when you know what you're actually doing."

She was quiet. He let her be quiet, which was the correct thing to do.

"Nine years," she said, eventually, not quite to him.

"Yes."

"And you've been doing this for six months."

"Five months when I worked out the architecture. Six now."

She made a sound that might, in different circumstances, have been a laugh. "That's irritating."

"I had the Codex," he said. "And Ren Wuji had four hundred years before me."

She looked at him with an expression that was several things at once, none of which he was going to try to name. "Show me," she said. "Not the diagram. Show me the actual mechanism. In practice."

He put the notebook away.

He spent the next two hours walking her through the Standard Protocol's Phases Four through Seven using her own established channels as the substrate — showing her, step by step, what she'd been doing and why it worked and what she'd been leaving on the table.

At the end of two hours she was silent for approximately four minutes.

Then: "My cultivation speed is going to increase significantly."

"Probably."

"The Court will notice."

"Yes."

"They'll think I have a hidden teacher."

He looked at her steadily. "You do."

She considered this. Something in her expression settled, not quite into acceptance, into something adjacent — the quality of a person who has received difficult information and has decided to receive it as a resource rather than a wound.

"Alright," she said.

That was all.

They paid their tea. They went their separate ways in the December afternoon.

Lin Mushi walked home through a city that was still the same city it had been this morning, and thought about the forty practitioners in a building above a mah-jong hall who were practicing something real without knowing what made it real, and thought about what it meant to tell people true things, and in what order, and how.
