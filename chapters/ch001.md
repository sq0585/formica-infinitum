## The Eternal Ant Legacy

### Arc I: The Night Shift

## Chapter One — The Man Who Stopped

The man came in at 3:17 AM.

Lin Mushi knew the time because he had just checked the register clock, as he did every seventeen minutes during the dead stretch between two and four. Not because he expected anything. Watching time move was better than watching it not move.

The store was empty except for him. Tuesday in October. Outside, Wuhou District held the specific silence of a city that had not stopped so much as paused — delivery trucks at five, breakfast stalls at five-thirty, but for now just cold, and the fluorescent hum, and Lin Mushi on his stool with a dog-eared printout about dark matter halo formation that he kept meaning to finish.

The door opened.

The chime did not sound.

Lin Mushi noticed the absence before he noticed the man — a small wrongness in the environment he had memorized across eight months of identical shifts. He looked up.

The man was old. Not simply old — old in the way that suggested the body had long since reached an agreement with time, complete mutual indifference. His jacket belonged to a different decade, dark fabric, a cut from photographs of the eighties. He walked from the door to the counter in twelve steps and stopped. He did not look at the shelves. He did not look at the drinks case. He looked at Lin Mushi with eyes that were brown and very calm and seemed to be involved in a private calculation.

"Is there something—" Lin Mushi started.

The man leaned forward and placed both hands on the counter.

His hands were an old man's hands, veined and deliberate. What made them interesting was what they did in the 0.4 seconds before the man stopped breathing. The fingers moved. Not randomly. A pattern, or the ghost of a pattern — Lin Mushi's eyes caught it the way peripheral vision catches motion, not quite seeing but registering that it had happened.

Lin Mushi's phone, face-up beside the register, became warm.

Then the man stopped.

Not dramatically. No gasp, no sound, no signal to the world that something had ended. His weight settled forward slightly. His hands remained on the counter. His eyes stayed open.

Lin Mushi sat with this for approximately three seconds.

Then he came around the counter and put two fingers to the man's neck, because he had taken a first aid course four years ago and this was what you did. He felt nothing. The skin was already slightly wrong under his fingers in a way he didn't have a word for yet. He said "Okay," quietly to himself, the way you use your own voice as something solid to hold onto.

He called emergency services. Gave his location, his name, a description of what had happened. Stayed on the line. Did not move the man. Stood at a slight distance and looked at the hands on the counter and thought about the pattern the fingers had made, which he could not describe and could not stop thinking about.

The paramedics arrived in eleven minutes. Police four minutes after that. A young officer with an unfortunate haircut asked questions from a form. Lin Mushi answered them accurately, except for one omission: when asked if the man had done anything unusual, Lin Mushi said, "No."

He did not do this deliberately. He simply had no language for what he had seen. The hand movement existed in his memory as a fact without a category. He filed it in the place where he kept things he didn't understand yet, which was a large and well-organized filing system.

At 4:45 AM, the store was his again. The counter had been photographed. A rectangle of missing dust marked where the man's hands had rested. Lin Mushi stood looking at it for a while.

His phone was still warm.

He picked it up.

On the home screen, between his camera app and his weather app, something had appeared that had not been there before. Not quite an icon — more a suggestion of one, hexagonal, faint gold against the dark background, visible only if you were looking for it and arguably not even then.

He looked at it for a long time.

The warmth of the phone was explicable. The app was possibly a glitch. The pattern the man's fingers had made did not look like a glitch.

He put the phone in his pocket. He made himself a cup of instant noodles from the back shelf — technically allowed, always paid for at end of shift — and sat on his stool and ate and stared at the empty counter.

He had a rule about things he didn't understand: observe first. Rush toward nothing. Write it down.

He reached under the counter for the notebook he kept there — blue cover, seven-eighths full — turned to the next blank page, and wrote:

> _3:17 AM. Old man, no ID, entered without triggering door chime. Died standing. Before — hand movement, both hands, lasted less than half a second. Pattern I can't describe. Phone warm. Unknown app: hexagonal icon, gold, faint. Did not open._
>
> _Note: the movement felt like it meant something. This is not useful information but it is true._

He capped the pen. Ate the rest of his noodles. The fluorescent light hummed.

Outside, the city had not yet decided to wake up.
