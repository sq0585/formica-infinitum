# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter Five — Sunday Call

His mother called at 10 AM sharp.

She had called at 10 AM sharp every Sunday for three years, since the month after he'd left university and moved back to the city. It was not discussed. It simply happened. He suspected she had an alarm for it.

Lin Mushi was on his second cup of tea, notebooks spread across the desk, Codex diagram open on his phone and face-down so he could think without it watching him.

"Are you sleeping?" she said instead of hello.

"Good morning," he said.

"That's a no." A brief pause, the kind that contained a thought she'd decided not to finish. "Are you eating?"

"Yes."

"What did you eat yesterday?"

He thought about the instant noodles at 3 AM and the cold rice at 6 AM. "Multiple times," he said, which was true.

"Mushi."

"I'm fine, Māma."

She made a sound that communicated, precisely, that she had filed this response under *to be revisited*. He heard her moving in the kitchen — water, the familiar tap of her cutting board. She cooked while she called him. He had only understood recently that this was how she handled the distance.

"The chrysanthemums came back," she said. "I thought they were finished. The soil in that corner is very good this year, I don't know why."

"That's good."

"Your cousin Mei is getting married. In spring."

"I know. You told me last week."

"I'm telling you again so you'll actually remember it." A pause. "You'll come."

"Yes."

"Good." The cutting board again. Something leafy. "You sound tired."

"I worked last night."

"You work every night."

"Most nights." He shifted the phone to his other ear and looked at his notebook, at the list of things he already knew, possibly. "Something happened at the store. Earlier in the week."

A beat of silence. Different from the other silences. "What kind of something?"

"A customer died," he said. "At the counter. I called emergency services. It was handled properly."

"Mushi."

"I'm alright."

"That doesn't — " She stopped. Resumed, more carefully. "Are you sure you're alright?"

He opened his mouth to say yes automatically and paused.

He was listening to her voice.

Not to the words. To the texture behind the words — the way her question arrived with a softness that had a harder thing underneath it, worry shaped like concern and concern shaped like something older. She was asking if he was alright. She was also not asking about something else, something she was holding back from saying.

She sounded tired in a way she wasn't saying.

He didn't know how he knew that. He hadn't known it on calls before — or he had, and hadn't paid attention. But now it was clear to him the way a note is clear when a tuning fork has already been struck nearby. Her voice had a frequency she was managing.

"Are *you* alright?" he said.

A brief silence. Then: "Why would you ask that?"

"You sound tired."

"I'm always tired. I'm fifty-four." But she said it lighter, as if something in her had loosened slightly at being noticed. "My back has been bothering me. It's nothing."

"Since when?"

"A month. I saw the doctor. He said stretching." A pause. "I have been stretching."

"Good."

"Don't look things up," she said, the way she always said it when she thought he was about to spiral into research. "It's a back. Old people have backs."

"You're not old."

"I'm your mother. Compared to you I am ancient." The cutting board again. "You're sure you're alright? About the customer?"

"I'm sure," he said. And then, because it was true: "I've been thinking about it. I have questions about it. But I'm not — I'm okay."

"Questions," she repeated. The word carried a particular weight in her voice, fond and slightly resigned — she had been listening to him have questions since he was seven years old and convinced there was a pattern in the flight paths of the pigeons outside their apartment window. There had been a pattern. He had been right. It had taken him two weeks and four notebooks to prove it.

"Yes," he said.

"Of course." A warm sound that wasn't quite a laugh. "When you have questions it means you're engaged. When you stop having questions is when I worry."

He didn't say anything. The depth sensation moved, very faintly, through the floor of his apartment.

"Eat properly," his mother said. "Call me if you need to talk. And come in spring for the wedding."

"I will."

"All three."

"All three," he agreed.

She hung up at 10:38 AM. He held the phone for a moment after, not looking at the Codex diagram.

He had noticed something in her voice. Not guessed at — noticed. The way he noticed the absence of the door chime, the way he noticed the pattern in a man's fingers, the way he noticed depth in a floor that looked flat. As if his perception had been tuned, or was in the process of being tuned, to a frequency that picked up information he hadn't been able to receive before.

He uncapped his pen.

> *10:38 AM. Sunday call. Mother: tired in a way she wasn't saying. Back pain, one month, doctor-confirmed minor. She didn't mention it until I asked.*
>
> *What I noticed: sub-verbal information. Not what she said. How the words arrived.*
>
> *This is new. Or: this is not new, but the clarity of it is new.*
>
> *Question: is the Codex changing how I perceive people as well as spaces? The depth sensation is spatial. What happened on the call felt like the same phenomenon, different domain.*
>
> *The man said four hundred years. He spent four hundred years learning to navigate what lies below. He also — from the message — knew he was dying while he was dying, and did something with four minutes left, and chose based on a criterion I don't fully understand.*
>
> *He was very calm. He was not afraid.*
>
> *Note: something to think about later. Not now.*

He closed the notebook. He looked at the Codex diagram for a long time.

The day was gray outside, Chengdu's particular gray, the kind that was neither threatening nor warm, just present. A pigeon landed on the power line outside his window and sat there in the manner of a pigeon that had no particular reason to be anywhere else.

He thought about his mother's voice.

He thought about the things that existed below the surface of what people said — the frequencies, the textures, all the information carried in a word that the word itself didn't contain.

He thought: *the world you live in is a surface. Beneath it is everything that has ever mattered.*

Maybe Ren Wuji had not been speaking only about the floor.

He reached for the phone. Opened the Codex. Did not select any nodes.

Just looked.

The diagram moved in its slow, barely-perceptible way, amber lines drawing their patient geometry, and somewhere beneath the building something answered it, quiet and deep and waiting to be understood.

He picked up the pen.

> *Rule: don't rush.*
>
> *Corollary: don't slow down, either. If something is observable, observe it.*

He turned to the next blank page, and began.
