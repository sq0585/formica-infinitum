# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter Twenty-Six — A Colleague's Question

Xiao Dong had the quality, common in people who worked early morning shifts, of being comprehensively unbothered by things that would concern people who slept regular hours.

He was twenty-four, studying accounting at a university he described as "fine," working the 6 AM–2 PM shift to pay for an apartment he described as "tolerable," and possessed an unshakeable philosophical position about convenience store work that could be summarized as: the job was simple, the customers were manageable, and he got to listen to music while stocking shelves. He did not appear to find anything complicated about this.

Lin Mushi respected him for it.

---

On the morning after the architectural perception breakthrough, Xiao Dong arrived at 5:58 AM to find Lin Mushi standing in the center of the store looking at the floor.

"You're doing the floor thing again," Xiao Dong said, unwinding his scarf.

Lin Mushi looked up. "What floor thing?"

"You stand there and look at the floor." Xiao Dong tied on his apron with the practiced speed of someone who had done it eight hundred times. "Started about a month ago. Sometimes you also look at the corners."

"I find the floor interesting."

Xiao Dong considered this. "Okay," he said, in the tone of a man who had decided this was not his business and was comfortable with that decision. He began counting the till.

Lin Mushi collected his bag. "I ordered more of the mandarin orange drinks," he said. "Stock should come Thursday."

"Great. They always run out on Thursdays."

"I know."

Xiao Dong looked up from the till. "Do you have a thing for the store? Like a, I don't know. Ownership feeling."

"I just pay attention," Lin Mushi said.

"You really do." Xiao Dong went back to the till. "Tao said you noticed the wall before the contractor did. And the drinks ordering, and the thing with the door sensor. You notice stuff."

"The door sensor stops working when the humidity changes," Lin Mushi said. "It's the adhesive on the contact strip."

"See, that's what I mean." Xiao Dong finished the count and wrote the number on the reconciliation sheet. "Most people just say the door's broken. You know why it's broken."

Lin Mushi put on his jacket.

"The floor has good bones," he said.

Xiao Dong laughed. Not a polite laugh — a genuine one, surprised out of him. "Good bones," he repeated. "Okay. Good bones. See you Thursday."

---

Lin Mushi walked home in the early light, thinking about that.

*The floor has good bones* was, in a specific and literal sense, the most accurate thing he had said to another person in the past month. The floor did have good bones. The bones were forty meters deep minimum and had vaulted ceilings and hexagonal tile work.

He could not say this.

He was accumulating a category of things he could not say, which was an unfamiliar position. He had always been reasonably direct — not because he had any particular commitment to radical honesty, but because he found the maintenance of false impressions tedious and the maintenance of accurate ones effortless. Saying what you knew was simpler than managing what you were pretending not to know.

The gap, right now, was very large.

He had a city under the floor of a convenience store where he worked a night shift and his colleague thought he paid good attention.

He bought breakfast from the stall on the corner — a jianbing, egg and scallion, the man making it in forty seconds with the accuracy of high repetition — and ate it walking home.

He thought: Xiao Dong is a decent person doing his job well, living a coherent life, completely unaware that the store he works in sits over something that might change everything.

He thought: most people are like this. Including me, four weeks ago.

He thought: this is not a reason for contempt. This is a reason for care.

He finished the jianbing. He went upstairs. He opened the notation notebook.

The floor has good bones.

Today he was going to find the grammatical layer that unlocked the next Codex document, because the document described something the second message called the *entry protocol*, and he needed to know what that meant before he attempted anything that involved going through a threshold in a wall.
