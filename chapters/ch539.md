# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 539 — The Last Sunday Call

His mother picked up on the second ring, the way she always did, as though she'd been sitting beside the phone waiting rather than simply going about an ordinary Sunday morning the way he suspected she actually had.

"Mushi."

"Mother."

"You're eating?" she asked, the same three questions she had asked him every Sunday for six years, unwavering in their order, unwavering in the specific plain concern underneath them.

"Fine," he said. "Properly, the way you told me to."

"Sleeping?"

"Adequate."

"Work?"

"Ongoing," he said, and felt her accept the word the way she always accepted it now — not a dismissal, not a deflection, simply the honest shorthand two people had settled into over years of a conversation that had learned, patiently, exactly how much detail either of them actually needed.

"Good," she said, satisfied, and moved, without further preamble, to the part of the call he suspected she genuinely looked forward to most. "The tomatoes came in. Best I've ever grown, Mushi. I mean that. Forty years of gardening, and this year's crop is the best of my life."

"What changed?"

"Soil amendments," she said, and he could hear, even through the phone, the specific pride of someone discussing a subject she had become genuinely, thoroughly expert in. "Meen-4 suggested something last spring — a mineral balance she said she'd learned from very old agricultural records, long before either of us were born. I was skeptical. I tried it anyway. I don't understand half of what she explained to me about why it works. I understand entirely that it worked."

"I'm glad."

"She's mentioned something else now," his mother continued. "A planting cycle timed to something she calls Qi-resonance — old Formican agricultural tradition, apparently, tied to lunar and seasonal patterns in a way considerably more precise than anything the almanac ever taught me. I'm considering trying it for next season."

"That's a good idea," he said, and meant it entirely.

"I thought you'd say that." There was warmth in it, unhurried, the specific fondness of a woman who had spent six years watching her son become something she still did not fully understand and had never once, across any of it, stopped simply being his mother about. "You always think trying things is a good idea. I used to worry that instinct would get you into trouble."

"Did it?"

"Considerably," she said, and he could hear her smiling. "I've decided I don't mind. You've mostly turned the trouble into something worth having."

They talked for forty minutes about nothing that mattered and everything that did — her garden, a neighbor's grandchild she was fond of, a minor disagreement with the community association about fence heights that she recounted with the specific relish of someone who enjoyed a good, low-stakes argument. He told her, in turn, about Su Lian's founding statement, about Dr. Shen Yue's Nobel, about a drainage problem he'd fixed without telling anyone how. She listened to all of it the same unhurried way she listened to everything, asking questions when she was curious, letting the rest simply wash past her without requiring full comprehension.

"You sound settled," she said, near the end of the call, the specific plain observation she offered him most Sundays now, in one form or another.

"I am."

"Good," she said. "Then keep being settled. Call me next week."

"I will."

He held the quiet after she'd hung up a moment longer than usual, feeling the specific, ordinary warmth of forty minutes spent on nothing that mattered and everything that did, and understood, sitting with it, that this — not the twelve cities, not the Nobel Prize, not even the healed wound at the edge of everything — was the exact, irreplaceable center everything else had always been built to protect.

He thought, sitting with the phone still warm in his hand, of every Sunday call across six years — the anxious early ones, when he'd been careful, deliberate, choosing each word to avoid frightening a woman who had no framework yet for what her son had quietly become; the harder ones, during the disclosure, when she'd read the news and asked, plainly, whether the strange things happening in the world were his doing, and he'd told her the truth for the first time without softening it; and now this, easy, unhurried, forty minutes about tomatoes and fence heights and nothing that either of them needed explained.

He understood, holding the memory of all of it together, that this progression — from careful concealment to plain, comfortable honesty — was itself one of the arc's quietest achievements, unremarked by any Collective transmission or any formal review, and mattering, to him, considerably more than most of what had.

He set the phone down, and sat a while longer in the ordinary afternoon light, entirely content.
