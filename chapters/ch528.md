# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 528 — What Doesn't Change

The council meeting ran forty minutes past its scheduled close, and he found himself, sitting through the final stretch of it, genuinely, specifically annoyed — not at the scale of anything cosmic, not at some enormous unresolved question the Collective's silence had left hanging, but at the particular, ordinary tedium of a zoning subcommittee unable to agree on where to place a second communal kitchen in the Strata's growing residential quarter.

He noted the annoyance with something close to fondness.

*Observation 528,* he wrote, later, once the meeting had finally, mercifully concluded. *I can perceive the entire stellar neighborhood without effort. I spent forty minutes today irritated by a zoning disagreement about kitchen placement. I want to record this without embellishment, because I think it matters more than almost anything else I have written this week: the capacity for genuine annoyance at small, ordinary things has not diminished at all. If anything, I suspect it has become more precious to me, not less, the larger everything else around it has grown.*

Wei Canglan's formal objections continued to make him laugh, even when — especially when — he understood, technically, that he shouldn't. The man had filed one just that morning regarding the expedition timeline for a piece of infrastructure work Lin Mushi privately considered entirely ready, and had done so with such precise, deadpan thoroughness that Lin Mushi had read the whole three paragraphs twice, once for the content and once simply to enjoy the specific rhythm of Wei Canglan's institutional prose.

He missed Chen Yufei, some days, in a way he had learned, across the better part of a year, to recognize as settled rather than sharp — not the raw ache of the relationship's ending, which had long since folded into something gentler, but a quieter, more permanent kind of missing, the specific fondness a person carries for a version of a relationship that was real and good and simply became something else, without either of them treating the becoming as a loss worth mourning rather than a shape worth honoring.

He was glad, every single day, in a way that had never once dulled with repetition, that his mother's joints no longer hurt her. He checked on this specific fact more often than any other single detail of the vast structure he now maintained — not because it required checking, the extension had held steady for years now, but because the checking itself had become a small daily ritual he found he did not want to give up, a private, deliberate act of gratitude aimed at exactly one person, out of everyone alive, who mattered to him in precisely this particular way.

He still filled notebooks.

He was on number forty-four now, the pages accumulating in the same careful, methodical hand that had first learned to write *Observation 1* at a small table in a night-shift convenience store, and he found, opening the current one to record the day's small irritations and larger accomplishments side by side, that the habit had never once felt like something he was doing because a dying man had asked him to, all those years ago. It had simply become, somewhere along the way, who he was.

He wrote the kitchen zoning dispute down first, in some detail, with genuine complaint.

He wrote the drainage fix underneath it, briefly, without much comment at all.

And beneath both, last, the same small private note he had started leaving himself most evenings now, unremarkable, unhurried, entirely his own: *Called Mother. She's fine. I'm fine. Tomorrow, more of the same, and I find I am glad of it.*

He closed the notebook, and went to sleep, the way he did most nights now — as himself, entirely, everywhere, and, above all, exactly here.

He woke, the next morning, to an ordinary alarm he no longer strictly needed and had never once considered giving up, and lay a moment in the quiet grey light before the day's work began, letting himself simply notice, the way he tried to notice most mornings now, the specific, accumulated strangeness of the life he had ended up living.

He had once worked a night shift because the pay was slightly better and the silence suited him. He now held perception across a solar system and a permanent, careful stake in the healing of a forty-million-year-old wound. Both facts were true. Neither one, he had learned across six years of careful, patient practice, cancelled the other out.

He got up. He made tea, properly, the way his mother had once told him to finish things properly, and drank it slowly, unhurried, before checking a single connection or reading a single report. The Strata's ordinary morning noise reached him through the window — a delivery cart, a child's laughter, the specific unremarkable music of a city simply continuing to be itself.

He was, by any measure that actually mattered, exactly where he wanted to be.

He picked up notebook forty-four, turned to a fresh page, and began, the way he began most days now, with nothing more dramatic than an honest accounting of what the day ahead actually held.
