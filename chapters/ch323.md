# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter Three Hundred and Twenty-Three — Dr. Shen Yue's Paper, Submitted

She sent the message at 11:47 in the evening, a single line that carried, despite its brevity, the weight of months of careful work finally set in motion.

*Submitted. Journal of Cosmological Physics. It's out of my hands now.*

He found her in the laboratory the next morning, the eight stations around her humming with their usual quiet activity, Dr. Shen Yue herself sitting at her own station with a stillness that suggested she hadn't fully returned to ordinary work yet.

"How does it feel?" he asked.

"Strange," she admitted, turning from her instruments to face him fully. "I've submitted papers before. Dozens of them, over the years. This is the first one where I genuinely don't know what happens next, in any direction — acceptance, rejection, the specific shape either outcome might eventually take once other people start reading it."

"What do you expect?"

"Rejection," she said, without hesitation. "Not because the science is wrong. Because the implications are large enough that reviewers will default to skepticism rather than genuine engagement, at least initially. That's not a flaw in the peer review process. It's the process working exactly as it should — extraordinary claims should face extraordinary scrutiny before the field accepts them."

"You sound remarkably calm about being rejected."

"I've had time to prepare for it." She turned back to her instruments, though her attention remained on the conversation. "I've thought about the timescale carefully, more than once. Two years, maybe five, before the readings are taken seriously enough that other groups start independently attempting to replicate them. Longer still before anyone connects the replicated data to what it's actually measuring, rather than simply cataloguing it as an unexplained anomaly."

---

"That's a long time to wait," he said.

"It's the honest timescale. I'd rather tell you the honest one than a comfortable one that turns out to be wrong." She met his eyes directly, something steady and unflinching in the look, the same quality he'd come to rely on in her since the day she'd first walked into the Strata and immediately reached for her notebook. "I've thought about this a great deal, in the months since I showed you the draft. I don't think the timeline is something either of us can meaningfully accelerate. Science moves at the pace evidence accumulates, and evidence accumulates at the pace instruments and researchers actually collect it. I could publish something more dramatic, make bolder claims, and it would simply be dismissed faster and more completely."

"So we wait."

"We wait, and in the meantime, we prepare for what waiting actually means — for you, specifically. Because I don't think the waiting is neutral for you the way it's neutral for me." She set down the instrument she'd been holding, giving him her full attention now. "I'm a physicist submitting an unusual paper. Worst case, my career takes a reputational hit and I spend a few uncomfortable years defending careful, correct work against skeptical colleagues. That's a real cost, but it's a bounded one."

"And for me?"

"For you, this is the first thread in something considerably larger — the first genuine, publicly documented crack in the wall between your world and the one everyone else lives in. It won't feel large today, or even next year. But it's real, and I'd rather you understand that clearly now, at the beginning, than be surprised by it once the pace finally picks up."

---

He sat with her assessment for a long moment, understanding, not for the first time, exactly why he trusted her judgment as completely as he did — not because she softened difficult truths, but because she delivered them with the same careful, rigorous precision she brought to everything else, trusting him to handle the weight of accuracy rather than needing comfort in its place.

"Thank you for submitting it," he said. "And thank you for being honest about what it actually means, rather than just the easier version."

"You've earned honest assessments," she said, something almost gentle entering her usually clinical tone. "I don't think I'd be doing either of us any favors pretending this is smaller than it is."

He left the laboratory that afternoon with the specific, grounded weight of the conversation settling into him — not urgent, not yet actionable in any concrete way, but real, patient, the first thread of something he understood, with increasing clarity, he was going to need to prepare for carefully in the months and years ahead.

He wrote a brief note in his journal that evening, the entry short but deliberate.

> *The paper is submitted. Dr. Shen Yue expects rejection first, then years of slow, patient accumulation before it matters publicly. She's right that this isn't neutral for me. I don't know yet what to do with that. I know I need to start thinking about it now, rather than waiting for the pace to force my hand later.*
