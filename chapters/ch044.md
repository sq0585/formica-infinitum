# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter Forty-Four — Qigong

He found the group on a Wednesday, by accident.

He had been walking the longer route home from the night shift — the route that took him through the park, which he was using for Qi observation practice, testing his range against the ambient field under different atmospheric conditions — when he saw them in the grey pre-dawn light: eight people moving through a sequence of positions near the pond, slow and precise, an elderly man at the front calling the transitions in a voice too low for Lin Mushi to hear the words.

He stood at the path's edge and watched.

He had read about qigong in the context of traditional energy cultivation. He had catalogued it alongside the meridian texts: an empirical practice that had preserved something real in wrong language. He had not observed it live. He had not, it occurred to him now, considered what it would feel like to watch someone who didn't know what they were doing performing something that worked anyway.

---

The Qi the group was moving was visible.

Not strongly — they were working with atmospheric flow, not deep channeling, the kind of practice that drew from the ambient filament network at the surface level rather than from any deep source. But the movement was real. As the group breathed and shifted weight and extended their arms in the prescribed positions, the atmospheric Qi around them responded: small eddies of the filament network's surface layer bending toward and away from the group's collective field, drawn and released by the coordinated breath-and-motion cycle.

They were doing something the Standard Protocol would classify as Phase Four exercises — the foundational breath regulation, but applied externally rather than internally, shaping ambient Qi through the medium of the body's movement rather than through direct channel engagement.

The technology was different. The underlying phenomenon was identical.

He watched for twenty minutes. Then, on a decision he made before he was fully aware of having made it, he went and stood at the back of the group.

The elderly man at the front did not comment. There was a quality of settled equanimity to the group that suggested new arrivals were not unusual.

He followed the sequence as best he could, watching the person in front of him for the positions he didn't know.

---

He attended for a week.

Every morning, 5:40 to 6:30 AM, the park beside the pond, eight positions in sequence, the elderly man calling transitions and occasionally adjusting someone's posture with a touch that Lin Mushi could now perceive as remarkably precise — the man's fingertips finding the exact point where the channel ran and pressing there to redirect what was blocked.

The man was a trained practitioner. Low-level by what Lin Mushi was becoming capable of assessing, but genuine — his channels were open, his energy disciplined, the practice that had built them ancient and well-structured. He called it Tai Chi Chuan rather than qigong and made no claims about what it did. He simply did it, every morning, and expected others to do it with him.

On the fourth morning, Lin Mushi mapped the correspondence between the sequence's eighth position — arms extended in a specific angle, weight forward, breath held for three counts — and Protocol Phase Six, the resonance field attunement exercise from Document 2.

The angle was not quite right in the sequence. Off by perhaps fifteen degrees from the Protocol's specification. But the intent was correct, and the structural principle — extend the arms along the line of the resonance field's direction of flow, hold the breath to increase the field's sensitivity, release to allow integration — was present.

Someone had found this, centuries ago, without the theoretical framework. They had found it by attention and repetition and the careful preservation of what worked, and they had passed it down in a form that was fifteen degrees off from optimal but still functionally valuable.

He thought about the meridian map. Real but inexact. He thought about the biochemist's paper: describing the substrate without knowing why it was there. He thought about the qigong sequence: preserving the practice without understanding the mechanism.

Fragments. Everywhere, fragments.

The Formicans had possessed the complete picture. That picture had retreated with them below the ground. What remained was a world of people working with pieces of something they didn't know was whole.

He wrote that evening:

> *Qigong observation, one week. Key finding: traditional practice preserved fragments of Formican cultivation theory, transmitted through bodily memory across centuries. The technique works. The explanation is wrong. The body remembered what the intellect forgot.*
>
> *The elderly instructor: channels open, practice genuine, understanding empirical. He knows what his body does. He doesn't know why.*
>
> *Position 8 / Protocol Phase 6 correspondence: confirmed. Angle deviation: ~15 degrees. Possible cause: original transmission error, or deliberate modification for a population without channel-open prerequisites, or simple drift over generations.*
>
> *The world is full of people who found a real thing and didn't know what they'd found.*
>
> *I need to decide what I owe them.*

He put the pen down.

Channel seven had stabilized. He was ready for eight.

He stopped going to the qigong group. He had learned what he needed to learn.

He went home and opened the eighth channel, which took forty minutes, and which gave him the ability to perceive structural resonance in large-scale geology — the faint harmonic the bedrock made against the deeper filament flows, a texture to the ground beneath the city that he was only now, with eight channels, sensitive enough to detect.

Chengdu sat on bedrock that sang very quietly, in a frequency nothing human had ever named.

He named it. He added it to the index.

He kept going.
