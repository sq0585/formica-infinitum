# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 401 — Aftermath — Morning

He called his mother at seven, which was, by her standards, a perfectly reasonable hour, and by his own exhausted, sleepless standards felt like the culmination of the longest four hours of his entire life.

"You're up early," she said, by way of greeting, the familiar sound of her kitchen already active behind her voice — the specific clatter of a pot being set on the stove, the low hum of the radio she kept on for company more than for the news it reported.

"Couldn't sleep," he admitted, which was true enough not to require further explanation.

"Something on your mind?"

He considered, for a moment, how to answer that. Three years of careful omission sat behind the question, the particular practiced habit of protecting her from weights she had never asked to carry, weights that had, until a few hours ago, included the literal possibility of an ending neither of them would have seen coming in time to say goodbye.

"Something I've been working on went well," he said finally, choosing the sentence with the same care he'd have given a piece of formal notation. Not the whole truth. But not a lie, either — simply the truest thing he could offer without unraveling three years of protection in a single phone call before he'd even had coffee.

"I'm glad," she said, and he could hear, in the two words, that she meant them without needing more detail than he'd given her — the particular trust she'd extended him for years now, the considered trust of a woman who had raised him and knew his character even when she didn't know the specifics of what that character was currently carrying. "Are you eating breakfast?"

"Working on it," he said, glancing at the plain rice and pickled vegetables he'd assembled without much thought, more out of habit than appetite.

"Working on it isn't eating," she said, with the exact dry practicality he'd come to rely on more than he ever told her directly. "Eat properly. You sound thin even over the phone, which shouldn't be possible, but somehow always is with you."

"I'll eat properly."

"Good." A pause, the sound of something sizzling faintly in a pan behind her. "Are you coming by this week? I'm making the braised pork you like, the one that takes all day, so you'll need to actually plan for it rather than showing up unannounced expecting me to have made it just because you walked through the door."

"I'll plan for it," he said, and found, saying it, that the small domestic promise carried more weight this particular morning than it would have on any ordinary day — a future he could plan for, casually, without the private, unspoken fear he'd carried for months that planning too far ahead was its own kind of foolishness.

They talked for twenty minutes, about nothing important — a neighbor's new dog that barked too much, the price of vegetables at the market she still insisted on walking to herself despite his repeated offers to have things delivered, a television drama she was following with more investment than she'd ever admit to directly. Ordinary conversation, unremarkable in every way, and precisely, he understood, the thing the entire review had ultimately been fought to protect.

"You sound better than when you called," she observed, near the end. "Whatever it was that had you up all night. Sounds like it worked out."

"It did," he said. "It really did."

"Good," she said again, simply, and he could hear, underneath the simplicity, something that told him she understood more than she let on — not the details, never the details, but the shape of relief in his voice, the particular lightness of someone who had been carrying something heavy and had finally, that morning, been allowed to set it down.

"I love you," he said, before she could move on to the next ordinary thing.

"I know," she said, unfazed, the way she always was when he said it — not because it didn't matter to her, he'd learned over years, but because she'd never needed the words as proof of something she already knew completely. "Eat your breakfast. And bring an appetite Thursday. This pork isn't going to eat itself."

He hung up, and sat for a moment in the quiet morning light, the notebook closed on the table beside him, and let himself feel, fully and without reservation for the first time in months, the particular ordinary happiness of a life that got to keep continuing, twenty unremarkable minutes at a time.
