# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter One Hundred and Eighty-Three — What the Assembly Decides

Five hours produced no formal agreement, and Lin Mushi had expected this — twelve people from five different traditions, each carrying their own institutional history and their own wounds from the past several weeks, were not going to converge on a complete structural proposal in a single evening.

What five hours did produce was something he considered, on reflection, more valuable than premature agreement would have been.

They had argued. Wei Jianming had pushed back hard against any structure that resembled hierarchy at all, his Iron Meridian experience having left him deeply suspicious of any arrangement where one person's judgment could override another's. Elder Zhao had argued, with equal conviction, that some structure was necessary — that pure flat egalitarianism among people with wildly different levels of experience and capability tended, in practice, to let the loudest or most confident voices dominate regardless of whether they were the most correct. The Verdant Stream elder had wanted something closer to his own tradition's loose, regional, low-hierarchy network. Wei Canglan, quieter than the others, had mostly listened, occasionally offering a precise observation that reframed whatever argument was currently circling.

No agreement emerged. But by the fourth hour, something else had: a shared communication channel, established collectively rather than imposed by Lin Mushi, through which the twelve of them — and whoever else eventually joined — could exchange information, ask questions, and coordinate without requiring any single authority to approve the exchange.

It was modest. It was, by the standards of what the Courts had built over centuries, almost nothing.

It was also, Lin Mushi understood, the first piece of infrastructure for whatever came next, built by the people who would actually use it rather than handed down from above.

He had said almost nothing during the substantive argument. He took notes — the same notebook habit that had carried him through fifteen months of solitary documentation, now turned toward documenting a collective process he was deliberately not steering.

Near the end, Wei Jianming turned to him directly. "You've been quiet all evening. You called this assembly. What do you actually want the outcome to be?"

Lin Mushi considered the question carefully before answering.

"I want this group, and whoever joins it later, to build something I don't get to define alone," he said. "I have opinions — I think some structure is better than none, the way Elder Zhao argued. I think hierarchy based on demonstrated capability rather than birth or institutional position is more honest than what we've inherited. But I don't think my opinion should simply become the answer because I happened to be the one who found the archive first."

"Why not?" Wei Jianming pressed. "You have more information than any of us. You're closer to the source than anyone alive."

"Because the thing I'm trying to avoid building," Lin Mushi said, "is exactly the problem we're correcting — an authority that holds its position because of who has access to the truth, rather than an authority that's accountable to people who can verify the truth independently. If I simply decide the structure unilaterally, I become a sixth Elder Council with better information. That's not actually different in kind from what's broken now. It's just better-informed."

The room was quiet for a moment.

"That's a harder standard to hold yourself to than just leading," Elder Zhao said.

"I know," he said. "I think it's the right one anyway."

He did not lead the discussion. He took notes.

When the twelve filed out that night, tired and unresolved but with a shared channel and the beginning of something neither defined nor abandoned, he stood alone in the outer hall for a while afterward.

He had declined a leadership role he could very easily have seized.

He thought, standing there in the amber quiet, that this was probably one of the most important decisions of the entire fifteen months — not because it was dramatic, but because it was the quietest possible refusal of exactly the kind of power the document's release had, without his fully intending it, made available to him.

He wrote, before he left: *Building a network, not an empire. I need to keep checking this distinction, because it will be tempting to blur, especially when the network is slow and an empire would be faster.*
