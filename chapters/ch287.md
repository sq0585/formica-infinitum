# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter Two Hundred and Eighty-Seven — The First Residents

Meen-4's answer to the residential wing had taken four days, which by her standards counted as swift, and had arrived not as an argument but as a single condition delivered with her usual economy: *the arrangement will be logged as provisional. I will not describe permanent human residency as established fact until I have observed it function.*

He'd agreed without hesitation, understanding the condition for what it was — not resistance, exactly, but the particular caution of someone who had watched civilizations rise and dissolve inside these same walls and had learned not to name a thing finished before it had proven itself.

The first application came two days after that, through Fang Ruxue, who brought it to him with the same unhurried precision she brought to everything, a printed page in one hand as though the Strata still ran on paperwork.

"Mrs. Tan," Fang Ruxue said, setting the page down on his workbench. "Celestial Compass tradition, third generation, has been part of the Collective for four months. She's asking about one of the residential rooms — not for herself. For her mother."

He read the application, which was less an application than three careful, handwritten paragraphs explaining a situation with the specific restraint of someone unused to asking for things. Mrs. Tan's mother was eighty-one. She lived alone in a fourth-floor walk-up in the old city, the stairs increasingly beyond her, her daughter's visits increasingly consumed by the logistics of simply getting to her rather than the actual visiting. Mrs. Tan wasn't asking for cultivation access. She wasn't asking for anything the Strata's mysteries usually attracted people toward. She was asking whether a room with no stairs might exist somewhere her mother could be warm.

"She specifically doesn't want anything unusual done to the room," Fang Ruxue added. "I made sure to clarify that before bringing this to you. No cultivation enhancement. She was very firm that her mother wouldn't want to feel like an experiment."

"That's fair," he said, and meant it.

---

He met them three days later, at the residential wing's threshold, Mrs. Tan supporting her mother's arm with the specific patience of someone who had done this exact motion ten thousand times and would do it ten thousand more without complaint.

The elder Mrs. Tan — he never did learn to call her anything but that, the honorific settling naturally into place within the first minute of meeting her — looked at the corridor's manufactured daylight with open, undisguised suspicion.

"This isn't a real window," she said.

"No," he admitted. "It isn't."

"But it feels like one."

"That was the intention."

She considered this with the flat, assessing skepticism of someone who had outlived several decades of people trying to sell her things, then walked, slowly, into the room he'd built, her daughter hovering half a step behind.

She stood in the middle of it for a long moment, taking in the bed, the chair by the false window, the small kitchen alcove Fang Ruxue had specifically requested be included so her mother could make her own tea without depending on anyone.

"It's cold," she said finally.

He felt the observation land with more weight than its plain content suggested — not a complaint about the room's temperature, which he'd calibrated carefully, but something closer to a test, the kind elderly people sometimes ran on new spaces to see whether anyone was actually listening.

"I can adjust that," he said, and did, feeling for the fold's edges the way he'd learned to over the past two days, warming the air by careful degrees until the room's temperature settled into something he judged comfortable.

She stood with her eyes closed for a moment, feeling the shift.

"Better," she said. Then, unprompted, with the particular bluntness of the very old: "You did that because I said something, not because you measured it first."

"Yes."

"Most people building something like this would have measured first. Decided what was correct and installed it before I ever complained." She opened her eyes and looked at him directly, something sharp and evaluating in the look. "You waited to be told."

"I didn't know what you'd need until you told me."

"Hm." She turned back to the room, apparently satisfied with something in the exchange he hadn't fully understood the shape of. "I was cold in my apartment for six years. Nobody asked. They just told me to wear another sweater."

He didn't have a response to that beyond the small, quiet resolve that formed somewhere in his chest, and so he said nothing, and let the room's new warmth speak for itself.

---

She moved in the following week — no ceremony, no announcement beyond Fang Ruxue quietly updating the Collective's residency log, the Strata's first intentional human resident settling into a room that had been built, in its final and most important detail, around one offhand complaint from a woman who'd spent six winters being told to wear another sweater.

He mentioned it to Chen Yufei that evening, the two of them sharing a late dinner at the small table that had become theirs by unspoken habit.

"You built her a home because she was cold," Chen Yufei said, something warm in her voice.

"I built her a home. Being cold is just the part I happened to notice first."

"That's usually how it works," she said. "You notice the small true thing, and it turns out to contain everything else."

He thought about that for the rest of the evening — the residential wing standing quiet and occupied for the first time in the Strata's long, strange history, not because anyone had come chasing power or knowledge or the impossible things this world could offer, but because an old woman had been cold, and someone had finally been close enough to notice.
