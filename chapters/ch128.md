# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter One Hundred and Twenty-Eight — The Wedding

He took the train to Leshan on a Friday.

His mother met him at the station. She looked at him the way she always looked at him when she had full information after a period of remote monitoring — the same assessing warmth, the same recalibration — and then hugged him with more force than usual.

"You're thin again," she said.

"I eat properly."

"You look like you've been thinking very hard for a very long time."

"That's accurate." He picked up his bag. "The tomatoes?"

"Decided against. Too much work for the drainage situation." She walked beside him toward the taxi stand. "The wedding is tomorrow evening. Tonight your aunt is hosting dinner. Which will be large and loud."

He thought about thirty people in a room. He thought about the Colony Link management protocols he'd been practicing for two weeks. He thought about selective attention framing — the technique of holding the ambient field at low resolution while keeping the specific-attention capability available for focused engagement.

He had tested it in the noodle shop last Tuesday. Thirty-eight people. Two and a half hours. He had been tired afterward but functional, which was significantly better than the previous test.

A family dinner of twenty people should be manageable.

"Tell me about the food situation," he said, because the wedding's catering crisis had apparently been resolved but not without drama and his mother had been tracking the drama closely.

She told him. He listened properly.

---

The dinner was held in his aunt's apartment, which was larger than it appeared from outside, and full of people who had known each other for varying numbers of decades and had achieved the specific comfort level of families who argued about important things and agreed on everything important.

Twenty-two people by his count. The Colony Link management protocol running quietly in the background.

He found his cousin Mei in the kitchen, where she was doing what people who had just navigated a catering crisis did: making sure the food was right, personally.

"Lin Mushi," she said, which was how his family used full names — warmly, not formally.

"Congratulations," he said.

She looked at him. She had always been direct. "You look strange."

"In what way?"

"Like you've grown several years older since the last time I saw you. But not in a bad way. In the way of—" She searched for it. "In the way of someone who has been doing something very difficult that they believe in."

His Colony Link perception, low-resolution ambient mode, picked up her Qi field's quality: genuine curiosity, affection, a slight satisfaction at having gotten the description right. She was a good observer.

"That's close," he said.

"Good. I'm glad you have something." She handed him a bowl of something that smelled of star anise and three years of his childhood. "Eat. We'll talk later if you want."

He ate. He talked to his aunt about her garden, which was a different but equally serious garden than his mother's. He talked to his uncle about the provincial infrastructure project his uncle had been managing for two years and which had recently encountered a geological challenge that his uncle, not knowing who he was talking to, described in terms that Lin Mushi could assess immediately and with considerably more precision than the engineering report apparently had.

He did not offer the assessment. He noted it. He thought about the geological consulting work and whether there was a useful connection to make here.

He would think about it later.

His mother watched him from across the room with the expression of someone who was seeing something she hadn't predicted and was pleased by it. He caught her watching. She raised her cup of tea. He raised his.

---

At the wedding the next evening, he sat beside Gao, who had traveled from Chengdu because Gao had met Mei once at a university function and had liked her enough to accept the invitation.

"How many people are here?" Gao said, surveying the hall.

"A hundred and forty, approximately."

"And you're—" Gao glanced at him.

"Fine. Management protocols." He paused. "It's like driving. I'm not ignoring the road. I'm not actively reading every detail. I'm monitoring."

Gao looked at him. "Can you drive while you're doing it?"

"I can attend a wedding. Which is about as complex as driving."

The wedding was beautiful in the specific way of weddings that had been planned with genuine care — the details right, the people in the right places, the ceremony short enough to be moving rather than enduring. His cousin's face when she arrived at the front of the room was the expression of someone who had spent three weeks managing catering crises and was now, finally, at the thing that all of that had been for.

He perceived, at low resolution, the room's aggregate emotional field.

It was warm. It was genuinely, collectively warm — the specific quality of people who were happy for someone they loved and who were, in this particular hour, focused entirely on that happiness rather than on their own concerns.

A Colony Link field reading, at low resolution, of a room full of people who were simply glad.

He had been treating the Colony Stage perception as a professional tool, a source of information about cultivation practitioners and their intentions and about the Formican community's collective state. He had not expected to find it here.

He looked at his cousin at the front of the room, her face entirely present.

He looked at his mother across the room, who was watching her niece with an expression he could now read clearly: pride, and a specific wistfulness, and beneath the wistfulness something simpler and warmer that didn't have a word.

He noted, in the notebook he'd brought because he always brought a notebook, but very briefly, in small characters:

> *The Colony Link is not only a tool. It is a different way of being in the same room as the people who matter to you.*

He closed the notebook.

He attended the rest of the wedding without writing anything.
