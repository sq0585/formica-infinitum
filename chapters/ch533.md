# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 533 — Axis-7 and Wei Canglan

He found Axis-7 in the agricultural district, at micro-scale, a location and a scale so far removed from her usual bearing that he nearly failed to recognize her presence at first — the ancient, careful gravity he'd learned to feel from her across years folded down into something small, precise, entirely absorbed in the specific molecular texture of a root system she was, by every appearance, arguing with.

"You haven't attended a council meeting in six months," he said, once he'd made his own presence known.

"I have been informed," she said, without looking up from the root structure she was examining, "that this is considered notable. I confess I had not been tracking the interval closely." She indicated the small experimental plot around her, cultivation-enhanced growing techniques laid out in careful, methodical rows, human and Formican farmers working alongside one another with the easy fluency of a partnership considerably older than its actual two years. "I have found this work more immediately satisfying than governance. I did not anticipate that outcome when I began."

"What changed?"

"Nothing changed," she said, and there was something almost like amusement in it. "I simply discovered, rather late in an extremely long life, that I have opinions about soil nitrogen cycling, and that having opinions about something this small and this concrete is considerably more restful than having opinions about the fate of civilizations. I recommend it, when you eventually find yourself with the leisure to try it."

He laughed, and left her to her argument with the root system, understanding, walking away, that this was exactly the kind of ending Axis-7 deserved — not resolution exactly, she remained, he suspected, permanently uncertain about a great many larger questions, but peace, found in a plot of dirt she was allowed to be stubborn about.

He found Wei Canglan later that same afternoon, in a teaching hall he'd built himself, six years into a discipline he had first begun learning as Lin Mushi's most junior and most bitterly displaced student.

Wei Canglan had reached Dimensional Weaver stage. He had reached it, characteristically, through his own derivation of the technique rather than Lin Mushi's direct instruction, a slower path than the one Lin Mushi had walked himself, and, as Wei Canglan had once observed years earlier with characteristic dry precision, a better one — better tested, better documented, better suited to being taught to someone else without requiring the specific, unrepeatable circumstances of a dying man's final gift.

He was teaching now, and he was, by every account Lin Mushi had gathered from his students, considerably harder on them than Lin Mushi had ever been on him.

"You're demanding," Lin Mushi observed, watching him correct a student's dimensional fold with precise, unforgiving thoroughness.

"I am precise," Wei Canglan corrected. "There is a difference, and my students understand it, even when the correction is uncomfortable to receive." He glanced toward the student in question, who had already returned to the exercise, chastened but unresentful. "They thank me for it, generally. Not in the moment. Afterward, once the correction has proven itself worth having received."

"Do you enjoy it?"

Wei Canglan considered the question with his usual seriousness. "I enjoy watching someone become capable of something they doubted they could manage," he said finally. "I did not expect to enjoy that as much as I do. I suspect it says something about what I actually wanted, underneath everything else I lost when I chose to side against Elder Kuo. I did not merely want to survive the choice. I wanted, eventually, to be useful because of it."

"You are," Lin Mushi said. "Considerably more than useful."

Wei Canglan gave him the same small precise nod he gave every closed item on any agenda he considered genuinely resolved, and turned back to his students without further comment, exactly as satisfied, Lin Mushi understood, as a man like him was ever likely to visibly appear.

He walked back through the Strata that evening thinking of both of them together — Axis-7, forty million years old, finding something genuinely restful in an argument about nitrogen cycling; Wei Canglan, who had lost everything institutional in a single afternoon of principled defiance and had spent six years quietly rebuilding, on his own precise terms, an institution worth belonging to. Neither of their stories had required his direct intervention to reach this point. He understood, walking, that this was itself a kind of achievement he hadn't fully anticipated when he'd first begun building the Strata — not merely a city that could function without him, but individual lives, once tangled inextricably with his own choices, that had grown into something fully, independently theirs.

He thought, briefly, of how differently both of their stories might have ended, in a version of events where he'd made different choices in Arc III and Arc IV — Wei Canglan never given the space to rebuild, Axis-7 never given reason to trust that Earth's ascent might resolve into something worth staying to witness. He did not dwell on the counterfactual long. It served, mostly, to remind him how much of what he now took for granted had never once been guaranteed.
