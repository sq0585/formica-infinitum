# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter Three Hundred and Twenty-Nine — The History Project

The project convened for the first time in the laboratory's largest shared space, nine participants gathered around a wide table spread with translated fragments, cross-references, and the general, sprawling architecture of forty million years of Formican record-keeping — six human researchers Fang Ruxue had carefully selected for their range of academic backgrounds, and three Formicans, Meen-4 among them, present alongside two archivists from other Strata locations Lin Mushi hadn't previously met.

He attended the first session as an observer rather than a direct participant, curious to see how the collaboration would actually unfold once set in motion.

The early hours moved slowly, methodically, the human researchers working through basic orientation to the Archive's notation conventions while the Formican participants offered patient, careful clarification. It was well into the second hour before anyone stumbled onto something that made the room's careful pace shift.

"This section," one of the human researchers said, a historian named Dr. Peng who specialized, Lin Mushi had learned, in institutional collapse across several human civilizations. "The Amber Lattice School's destruction. The record describes it as disciplinary action."

*Correct terminology, as recorded,* one of the visiting archivists confirmed.

"Disciplinary action against whom?" Dr. Peng asked. "The phrase implies a target. A school isn't disciplined the way a location is destroyed by an external threat. Disciplinary action is what you do to a member of your own institution who has violated its standards."

The room went quiet, and Lin Mushi felt something shift in the air, a specific tension he recognized as the particular stillness that settled over a room right before an uncomfortable realization arrived.

*The phrasing is accurate to the original notation,* Meen-4 signaled, after a pause long enough to carry its own weight. *I had not previously examined the implication as closely as you are examining it now.*

"So the Amber Lattice School wasn't destroyed by an enemy," Dr. Peng continued, working through the logic carefully. "It was destroyed by its own civilization. As punishment. For something."

*That is a plausible reading of the terminology,* Meen-4 signaled, something careful and controlled in the cadence that Lin Mushi, watching closely, recognized as considerably more effort than her usual measured signals required.

---

The session continued for another hour, the group working carefully through every reference to the destruction they could locate, the emerging picture growing clearer and more uncomfortable with each cross-reference — an internal action, deliberate, sanctioned by whatever governing body the Isolationists had represented at the time, directed at an institution that had, apparently, failed some standard significant enough to warrant its complete dissolution.

Lin Mushi found his own attention narrowing, increasingly, on a single unanswered question sitting beneath everything the group was carefully assembling.

Failed to prevent what?

He didn't voice the question aloud, sensing, correctly, that the room wasn't yet ready to arrive there, and that pushing toward it before the evidence naturally led there risked closing off exactly the kind of patient, collaborative discovery Meen-4 had wanted this project to provide.

The session ended for the day with the question still unresolved, the group agreeing to continue the following week, and Lin Mushi found Meen-4 afterward, alone in the administrative chamber, her stillness carrying a weight considerably heavier than the afternoon's careful academic pace had shown.

"You knew this was coming," he said gently. "The disciplinary action phrasing. You've read it a thousand times before today."

*I have read it many times,* she signaled. *I had not, until today, allowed myself to examine what the phrasing actually implies as closely as Dr. Peng examined it within the first hour of the project's existence.*

"Why not?"

She was quiet for a long moment, the particular stillness he'd learned to recognize as her deciding how much of something difficult she was willing to say aloud.

*Because I suspect I already know, at some level, what the record will eventually reveal once this project continues its work,* she signaled finally. *And I have spent a very long time not looking at it directly.*

He didn't press further, understanding, sitting with the weight of her admission, that whatever she was circling was considerably more personal than academic curiosity — and that the careful, patient work of the history project she'd proposed was going to lead, before long, somewhere she'd apparently been avoiding for longer than he could easily calculate.

He left her to her stillness, and walked back toward the residential wing carrying the quiet, mounting certainty that the next session wasn't going to stay comfortable for very much longer.
