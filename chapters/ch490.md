# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 490 — Chen Yufei's Visit

She came to him formally this time, not the ordinary drift of an evening together but a deliberate visit, arriving at the pocket dimension laboratory with the particular purposeful quiet he'd learned, across five years, to recognize as her version of announcing that something mattered.

"I wanted to talk," she said, settling into the chair across from him, "before whatever comes next actually arrives. I have a feeling we're getting close to it, even if neither of us has said so directly yet."

"We are," he admitted. "The dissolution zone repair is holding steady. Veth-Karun's account gave me the mechanism I needed. I think Formless Scale is closer than either of us has wanted to say out loud."

"That's what I thought." She studied him for a long moment, something searching in her expression that he recognized from the earliest, most uncertain days of their relationship — the specific attention of someone trying to see past the surface of an answer to whatever actually sat underneath it. "I want to ask you something, and I want an honest answer, not a comfortable one."

"Always."

"What's your anchor now?" she asked. "Not the Protocol — I understand the mechanism, Meen-4's explained it thoroughly. I mean the actual thing. After everything — Stellar Scale, your mother, Veth-Karun's letter, three hundred and twenty-three people trusting you with something this enormous — what's actually holding you steady, when you reach for it?"

He sat with the question, understanding it deserved more than a quick, reflexive answer, and let himself actually search for the honest shape of it rather than reaching for whatever sounded appropriately meaningful.

"Five people," he said finally. "You. Liang Jingzhi. Dr. Shen Yue. Wei Canglan. Meen-4. That hasn't changed, even with three hundred and twenty-three more people now extended alongside all of us. They're the network. You five are still the actual foundation everything else stands on."

Chen Yufei was quiet a moment, something moving across her expression. "Liang Jingzhi gave more than the rest of us," she said. "Permanently. I think about that more than I've told anyone."

"I think about it every day," he admitted. "I don't know how to hold what she gave without it changing the weight of everything else. I'm still learning to."

"That's four names and Meen-4," she said, something gently teasing entering her tone despite the conversation's evident weight. "You said five people and something else. I heard it in how you paused."

He smiled slightly, caught, and let himself finish the thought properly. "And one moment," he said. "The specific moment in the kitchen, five years ago, where a dying man's hand closed around mine and I understood, before I understood anything else about any of this, that I was about to become responsible for something considerably larger than myself. Everything since has been built on top of that single moment. Five people hold me steady now. But that moment is still the actual root all of it grows from."

She was quiet for a while, absorbing the answer with the same careful attention she'd asked the question with, and he watched something settle in her expression — not surprise, he thought, but a kind of confirmation, as if she'd suspected the answer already and had simply needed to hear him actually say it.

"That's enough," she said finally. "I wanted to make sure, before whatever comes next, that the answer was still something real. Not a slogan. Not something you'd say because it sounds right in a moment like this. I think five people and one moment is real."

"It is," he said quietly. "It's the realest thing I know."

She nodded, the particular, settled nod of someone who had asked exactly the question she needed to ask and received exactly the answer she needed to hear, and reached across the small distance between them to take his hand — not romantically, he understood clearly, the specific shape of their relationship having settled, years ago, into something steadier and, in its own way, considerably more durable than romance alone might have been. Simply present. Simply real.

"Whatever comes next," she said, "I'm still here for it. However large it turns out to be."

"I know," he said, and found, holding her hand in the laboratory's warm, familiar light, that he had never doubted it for a moment.

They sat together a while longer, not needing to fill the silence with anything further, the same comfortable quiet that had carried them through every difficult threshold of the past five years. He thought, watching the evening light shift slowly across the room, of how much this relationship had changed shape since it began — from the frightened, uncertain alliance of two people surviving something neither of them understood, through romance, and past it, into something that had never once needed a single word to describe it fully.

"Do you ever wonder what we'd have become," she asked eventually, "if none of this had happened? If Ren Wuji had never walked into your store that night?"

"Sometimes," he admitted. "Not often. I think whoever that version of me would have been, he wouldn't have known what he was missing. That's the strange mercy of it, I suppose — you can't grieve a life you never got the chance to imagine."

"I don't think I'd trade this one," she said quietly. "For all it's cost either of us."

"Neither would I," he said, and meant it entirely.
