# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter Two Hundred and Forty — Fang Ruxue's Collective

Twenty-three members, from six traditions, gathered in a rented university seminar room for the Cultivation Research Collective's first formal session — a number Lin Mushi found himself quietly cataloguing as he found his seat near the back, watching the room fill with people who, a year ago, would have had no institutional reason to sit in the same building together.

Fang Ruxue chaired from the front with the unhurried precision of someone who had spent thirty-one years running departmental meetings and understood, with the patience of long practice, exactly how to keep two dozen opinionated cultivators from talking over each other.

"We'll begin with introductions," she said, "brief ones, tradition and area of interest only. We have substantial work ahead of us and a limited room booking."

The introductions ran the breadth of what the Courts' fracture had actually produced — three Celestial Compass scholars beyond Fang Ruxue herself, two quiet Iron Meridian practitioners who introduced themselves without mentioning their Court affiliation until directly asked, a River Jade observer who spoke for exactly the length of time required and not a second longer, several independent cultivators Lin Mushi recognized from the early Assembly, Wei Canglan representing what Fang Ruxue diplomatically termed "unaffiliated practitioners with direct Archive access."

Lady Shen sat near the front, introduced simply as herself, no Court title attached.

When the round reached Lin Mushi, he kept his own introduction as brief as everyone else's. "Archive interpreter," he said. "Translation verification, primarily."

Fang Ruxue moved the meeting forward without dwelling on it, exactly as he'd hoped she would.

---

The first project, once introductions concluded, was more ambitious than he'd expected her to propose on day one.

"Standardizing the interpretation of Formican cultivation theory across traditions," she said, projecting a careful outline onto the room's modest screen. "Not erasing each tradition's distinct practice — the variations developed over centuries have genuine value, refinements suited to different temperaments and different bodies. But replacing the five competing, contradictory founding mythologies with a single, accurate, shared understanding of what we're all actually working with underneath the variation."

A hand went up — one of the Iron Meridian practitioners, careful but direct. "That's a request to formally abandon two thousand years of doctrinal language. Some traditions will resist that more than others."

"They will," Fang Ruxue agreed, without softening the acknowledgment. "This isn't a project that happens quickly, or without resistance, or without some traditions choosing not to participate at all. I'm not proposing we force anyone's hand. I'm proposing we build the accurate framework carefully, make it freely available, and let its usefulness speak for itself over time, the way good scholarship eventually does."

The discussion that followed ran for nearly two hours — careful, occasionally sharp, never quite descending into the kind of territorial defensiveness Lin Mushi had half expected from a room containing representatives of institutions that had spent centuries treating each other as rivals. Fang Ruxue guided it with the patient, unhurried authority of someone who had clearly run a hundred meetings exactly like this one, redirecting tangents, drawing out the quieter members, never once raising her voice above the room's natural volume.

By the session's end, the framework's first working group had been assigned — three members, including Lin Mushi himself, tasked with producing a draft comparison of all five traditions' founding techniques against the Archive's original notation, due in six weeks.

He gathered his notes afterward, watching the room slowly empty, the strange, hopeful texture of two dozen people from institutions that used to define themselves against each other now filing out together, already talking among themselves about working group logistics.

Fang Ruxue found him near the door.

"Twenty-three members," she said. "I'd have settled for twelve."

"You got nearly double that."

"I got nearly double that because the world is in enough flux right now that people are willing to consider something genuinely new." She looked back at the emptying room, something quiet and satisfied in her expression. "That won't last forever. We should build as much of this as we can while the willingness is still here."

He nodded, understanding the urgency underneath her careful academic patience, and followed her out into the evening, the Collective's first real session behind them, the much longer work of building something that actually lasted still entirely ahead.
