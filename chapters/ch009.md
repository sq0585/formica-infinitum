# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter Nine — Auntie Zhang

She arrived at 4:07 AM, which was slightly later than usual, and bought her warm soy milk from the machine near the door, and stood at the counter while she opened it, which was not her habit — usually she took it and left — so Lin Mushi looked up from his notebook.

"You're reading again," she said.

"Thinking," he said.

Auntie Zhang was sixty-eight years old and had the insomniac's talent for treating 4 AM like a perfectly normal hour to be somewhere. She lived in the apartment block two streets over and had been coming to this store every other night for as long as Lin Mushi had worked here, which meant she was part of the scenery in the way that the fluorescent hum was part of the scenery — noticed only in its absence.

She was wearing her winter coat, the padded one, slightly too large for her, and a knitted hat she had probably owned since her forties. She held her soy milk with both hands the way someone holds something warm when they're cold.

"My father had a shop," she said, apparently continuing a conversation they hadn't been having. This was a thing she did. "Not here. The old part, near Kuanzhai Alley, before they renovated everything."

"What kind of shop?"

"Repair shop. Watches, clocks. Small things." She took a sip. "The air in that building was different."

Lin Mushi looked up from his notebook.

"Different how?"

She considered this with the seriousness of someone who had been asked a question they'd thought about before but never had occasion to answer. "Warmer," she said finally. "Not temperature warm. Just — warmer. The way your mother's kitchen feels warm even in winter. The things he fixed in that shop worked better than things he fixed anywhere else. He used to say the building had good bones."

"Good bones," Lin Mushi repeated.

"He was not a superstitious man," Auntie Zhang said, as if this were important to establish. "He liked facts. He kept records. He wrote down every repair, how long it took, what failed." She paused. "But he always said that building had something in the air. That things came together properly there."

Lin Mushi set his pen down.

The depth sensation ran north and slightly down. He had been tracking it for eleven days. He had a heading but not a destination, a phenomenon but not an explanation. He had a dead man's message and a diagram he could feel more than read.

And here was a woman in a winter coat telling him about air that was different in certain places, in a watch-repair shop her father had kept records of.

"What happened to the building?" he asked.

"Demolished," she said simply. "Oh, twenty years ago. My father was upset, but not in the way he would have been about a place he'd only been attached to for reasons of money. He stood across the street on the day they tore it down and watched the whole thing. He told me after: 'Something was in there that shouldn't have been in the rubble.'" She finished her soy milk. "I don't know what he meant. I've thought about it sometimes."

She folded the carton neatly along its original crease lines, a habitual precision, and placed it in the recycling.

"My daughter thinks I should sleep more," she said, in a different tone entirely, the tone of the conversation she'd apparently come here to have. "She's probably right. But when I sleep too long, I think about things I don't want to think about, and when I'm awake at four in the morning I think about things my father said about a building that doesn't exist anymore, and somehow that is better."

"It sounds better," Lin Mushi said.

She smiled at him — a small, recognizing smile, the kind shared between people who understand that certain hours have their own logic. "You're a good listener," she said. "Not everyone your age knows how to listen."

"I take notes," he said.

"That's not the same thing."

He thought about that after she left. The door chimed this time, in the normal way, and the store was his again, and the fluorescent light hummed.

He opened his notebook to a fresh page and did not write anything about Qi or the Codex or directional depth or battery anomalies. He wrote:

> _Auntie Zhang's father: watch repair shop, Kuanzhai Alley area (pre-renovation). Air felt different. Warm. Things worked better inside it. Repairs turned out well. Father called it 'good bones.' Demolished ~20 years ago._
>
> _He stood across the street and watched. Said afterward: 'Something was in there that shouldn't have been in the rubble.'_
>
> _He was not superstitious. He kept records._
>
> _This is just an old woman's memory of her father. It is also the third data point for the idea that certain locations have a consistent anomalous property that affects whatever happens inside them._
>
> _She doesn't know what it means. She just remembers it._

He capped the pen. Then he uncapped it again.

He had been avoiding a word for eleven days, and he noticed himself avoiding it now, the same way he avoided naming the depth sensation as anything but "the depth sensation" — because naming a thing prematurely was a way of pretending you understood it before you did. But avoiding the word had a cost too. He had been writing _warmth_, _energy_, _the phenomenon_, _whatever this is_, a different evasion each time, and the variety was itself a kind of imprecision. A working term wasn't a conclusion. It was a label on a box, so you could find the box again.

He thought about Auntie Zhang's father, a man who kept records and was not superstitious, reaching for _good bones_ because that was the vocabulary he had — not because it was correct, but because it was the nearest real word for an experience nobody had given him formal language for. The qigong groups in the park down the street did the same thing every morning, calling whatever moved through their bodies during the slow forms by a word that was already three thousand years old before anyone had a mechanism to back it up. The word turned up in ordinary speech everywhere, once he started noticing: _shengqi_ — to get angry, literally to produce qi. _Qise_ — complexion, literally the color of one's qi. Buried deep enough in idiom that it had stopped sounding like a claim about anything.

It was not a precise word. That, he decided, was exactly why it was the right one to start with. A precise word would commit him to a theory before he had one. _Qi_ committed him to nothing except agreeing that something was there.

He wrote at the top of a fresh page:

> _Working term, as of tonight: Qi. Not a conclusion — a placeholder borrowed from existing vocabulary (qigong, traditional medicine, common idiom), because it already names "energy a body or place can have" without specifying a mechanism, which is exactly the gap I'm working in. Will replace if something more precise turns up. Until then: consistency matters more than originality._

He put the pen down and sat with the city's quiet for a while.

He thought: I am not the first person to notice this.

I am possibly just the first person in a long time to have the equipment to study it.

He turned the page and kept writing.
