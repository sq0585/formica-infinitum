# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 383 — What Happens to Lady Shen

She withdrew from the Orthodoxy's engagement three days later, and Lin Mushi learned about it the way he learned most things Lady Shen did — indirectly, after the fact, through Meen-4, who had picked up the change in formal Collective standing before anyone had bothered to announce it to him directly.

"She has removed herself as the Orthodoxy's observer of record," Meen-4 told him. "Formally. It is a matter of standing filed with the review body, not a private decision she could simply reverse in her own head. It is done."

"Because I asked her to?"

"You didn't ask her to," Meen-4 said. "I would have told you if you had, and I don't believe it would have worked regardless. She does not withdraw from things because someone demands it. She withdraws when she has finished saying what she came to say and has been genuinely heard. That is a different mechanism, and a slower one, but in her case it appears to be the only one that holds."

He thought about that, and about the conversation in the low chitin-ribbed chamber, and understood, slowly, that Meen-4 was right — he had not commanded anything. He had simply listened, and judged the two halves of what she'd told him honestly, without collapsing either one into the other, and that, apparently, had been enough.

He found her a week later, not because he sought her out but because she appeared, as she always did, at a moment of her own choosing — this time in the Strata's outer garden terrace, one of the newer pocket-dimension spaces he'd built for residents who missed sunlight that wasn't simulated. She was sitting on a low bench, watching light filter through leaves that existed nowhere except inside a fold of dimensional space he had personally shaped.

"I'm not returning to the Orthodoxy," she said, before he'd sat down. "I want you to hear that from me directly, not through Meen-4's channels."

"I'd heard already. I wanted to hear the reason from you too."

"The reason is simple. I said what I came to say. You heard it, and judged it honestly, without pretending my fear was baseless or my method was forgivable. That is more than I expected, and more, frankly, than I believe I deserved. There is nothing left for me to accomplish through them that continued engagement would improve." She turned to look at him directly. "I am not seeking your forgiveness, Lin Mushi. I want to be clear about that. I am not seeking anyone's absolution for a choice I made with my eyes open, believing it was necessary. I am simply done making it."

"Where does that leave you," he asked. "In the review. In — everything."

"Undecided, for now. I am not aligned with the Orthodoxy any longer. I am also not, I think, fully aligned with you — not in the sense of unconditional support, which I don't believe either of us would find useful even if I offered it." She looked back toward the filtered light. "I would like to remain in the review process. As an observer. Neutral standing, if the Surveyor will permit it, and I believe it will, given the precedent I've already set by attending as the Orthodoxy's representative."

"Watching. Not helping either side."

"Watching," she confirmed. "Because I still believe what I told you — that the risk I described is real, even if I no longer believe the Orthodoxy's remedy is the correct answer to it. I would rather be the person who continues watching honestly than the person who either abandons the concern entirely or returns to trying to solve it through people who want you erased."

He sat with her in the quiet for a while, the two of them watching light move through leaves neither of them had grown, only shaped, and found, to his own mild surprise, that the anger he'd expected to still be carrying had thinned into something more complicated — not trust exactly, not yet, but a kind of respect that trust might, eventually, be built out of.

"You're the most complicated person I know," he said finally, and meant it as neither compliment nor accusation, simply as the truest thing he could offer.

"I have been told that before," she said, something almost like amusement in her voice. "Usually as an insult. I find I don't entirely mind it, coming from you."

They sat together a while longer, saying nothing else, and he understood, watching her in the filtered light, that whatever she became to him now — ally, observer, something in between that had no clean name — she had earned, through three years of incomplete truths finally made whole, the right to occupy that uncertain space rather than be forced into a simpler one.
