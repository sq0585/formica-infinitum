# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter Two Hundred and Seventy-One — The Stone Hall's Surrender

The application arrived through formal channels, which surprised Fang Ruxue more than the application's actual content did.

"Stone Hall Sect," she said, reading through the submission with the careful attention she brought to anything procedurally unusual. "Formally applying for Collective membership. Full institutional dissolution, members transitioning under our governance framework, the same structure Lady Shen used."

Lin Mushi considered this, the particular irony of it settling slowly. Stone Hall had been, by every account he'd gathered across the past year, the least sophisticated and most reflexively hostile of the remaining Courts — the institution most willing to use force, least equipped to wield it cleverly, the loud, predictable objector to every development that had reshaped the cultivation world around them.

"What changed?" he asked.

"According to their formal statement, considerable attrition over the past several months. They never had Iron Meridian's institutional depth or Northern Peak's political sophistication. Watching three other Courts either dissolve or transform while they remained isolated and increasingly irrelevant apparently produced exactly the kind of pressure that eventually breaks even the most stubborn holdouts."

"Do you trust the application?"

Fang Ruxue considered the question seriously, the particular careful weighing she brought to anything that required judging genuine change against strategic convenience.

"I want to verify it carefully before recommending full acceptance," she said. "Stone Hall's history includes some of the conflict's more reckless conduct — not Gou Ping's calculated violence, but something closer to undisciplined aggression, poorly considered and poorly executed. I'd want assurance that joining the Collective represents genuine reconsideration rather than simply the last viable option left to an institution that's run out of every other choice."

"That's a fair caution."

They reviewed the application together over the following week, the Collective's governance committee conducting the same careful process they'd applied to every prior transition — individual interviews with senior Stone Hall practitioners, a review of past conduct, a clear-eyed assessment of whether the institution's leadership genuinely understood what they were asking to join rather than simply seeking shelter from continued isolation.

The interviews were, by Fang Ruxue's account, more genuinely uncomfortable than any prior review process had been.

"Their senior elder admitted, directly, that Stone Hall never had the scholarly sophistication to engage with the Codex on its own terms," she told Lin Mushi afterward. "He said, and I'm quoting him closely: 'We were never the clever Court. We were the strong one. Strength stopped being the deciding factor in this conflict somewhere around the time you built a device a child could use to do what took our masters forty years to learn.' That's an uncomfortable kind of honesty. I respect it more than I expected to."

The committee approved the application with conditions: a probationary period, close governance oversight, mandatory participation in the same comparative scholarship working groups that had reshaped every other surviving tradition's relationship to its own foundational texts.

Stone Hall accepted every condition without negotiation.

---

Lin Mushi sat with the news that evening, the particular shape of what it meant settling slowly into something he understood as genuinely significant.

Five Courts, two thousand years of independent institutional tradition, were now, functionally, gone. Not destroyed in any single dramatic confrontation, not erased through force or conquest, but absorbed — slowly, messily, each one arriving at dissolution through its own particular combination of pressure, reconsideration, and the simple accumulating weight of a world that had changed faster than any of them could adapt to on their old terms.

He thought about Elder Kuo, the man who'd once offered him recognition in exchange for silence about past surveillance, now reportedly working as an ordinary teacher in a small mountain community somewhere outside the Collective's main structure, having lost the institution his entire identity had been built around and finding, slowly, something smaller and more honest to replace it.

He thought about the particular cost of every transition like this — real people, real careers, real identities built across years or decades, all of it requiring genuine reconstruction rather than simple celebration.

"It's not a victory exactly," he told Chen Yufei, when she found him still sitting with the news. "I don't think 'victory' is the right word for watching institutions two thousand years old quietly come apart, even institutions that deserved to."

"What's the right word?"

He considered this for a long moment.

"Transition," he said finally. "Difficult, costly, necessary transition. The Courts were wrong about almost everything important. They were also real institutions full of real people who'd built genuine lives around them. Both things are true. I don't think I get to celebrate the first part without holding the weight of the second."

Chen Yufei sat with him in the quiet that followed, neither of them rushing to fill it, the particular gravity of watching a two-thousand-year-old world finally, fully give way to something new settling into the evening around them.
