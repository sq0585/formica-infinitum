# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter Two Hundred and Seven — Lady Shen Alone

She arrived without weapons, which he could verify from the Circuit network's reading of her Qi signature before she'd even reached the threshold, and without escort, which he could verify by the simple absence of any second signature within half a kilometer.

She crossed into the outer hall the way a person crosses into a room they have already decided will change them, regardless of what they find there — not cautiously, not eagerly, but with the deliberate, controlled pace of someone managing her own reaction in advance.

"No demonstration today," he said, by way of greeting. "Just the hall, as it is."

"That's the demonstration." Her eyes moved across the scale of the space, the shelving running past the point where ordinary architecture gave out, the amber light with no visible source. "You didn't curate this. You're letting me see it the way you see it on an ordinary day."

"Yes."

She walked the length of the nearest shelving row, her steps unhurried, her hands folded behind her back in a posture he recognized from every formal Court interaction he'd had with her — except this time there was no audience to perform it for. She was simply, he understood slowly, the kind of person who held herself that way even alone.

"What do you see?" he asked, when she reached the end of the row.

She didn't answer immediately. She turned back to face him, and for the first time since he'd known her, something in her expression slipped past the careful political composure he'd come to expect.

"It's the most important thing I have ever seen," she said. "And the Northern Peak Court has no legitimate claim to any part of it."

He hadn't expected the honesty to arrive that quickly, or that completely.

"Say more," he said.

"Thirty years," she said. "Thirty years building Northern Peak's authority on cultivation techniques we believed we'd developed ourselves, refined ourselves, earned through generations of disciplined practice. And it turns out we were copying homework we didn't know we were copying, badly, from a source we never had access to and couldn't have understood if we had." She gestured at the hall around them, a single controlled motion. "This makes everything I've spent my career protecting look like theft I didn't know I was committing."

"That's not entirely fair to you."

"It's entirely accurate, which matters more to me than fair." She met his eyes directly. "I'm not interested in being comforted, Lin Mushi. I'm interested in understanding what happens next, and what role — if any — remains for someone who built her life on a structure that turns out to be standing on borrowed ground."

He considered the question seriously, the way he tried to consider most things she said now that she'd stopped performing for him.

"That depends on what you actually want," he said. "Not what Northern Peak wants. What you want."

"I want the Court to remain relevant in whatever comes after this," she said, without hesitation, as though the answer had been sitting fully formed for longer than the question had existed. "Not dominant. I've stopped believing dominance was ever honestly earned. Relevant. I spent thirty years building institutional stability, and I am not willing to simply watch it dissolve into nothing because the foundation turned out to be a misunderstanding rather than a lie."

"That's an honest answer."

"It's the only kind I have left," she said. "I've discovered I'm not particularly good at the other kind, once the option's been taken away from me."

He looked at her for a long moment — the careful posture, the controlled voice, the genuine uncertainty underneath both that she clearly hated having to show him.

"There may be a way to do that," he said. "Build something instead of defending something. I don't know yet what it looks like. But it would make you relevant to something that actually lasts, instead of something that was always going to fail once enough people learned the truth."

She didn't answer right away. She looked past him, toward the amber distance the hall fell away into, and for a moment she looked less like the most dangerous person who had ever tried to take something from him and more like a woman doing arithmetic on thirty years of her own life.

"I'll need to think about what that means," she said finally. "I'm not someone who agrees to things before I understand their shape."

"I wouldn't trust you if you did."

Something close to a real smile crossed her face, brief and unguarded, gone almost before he registered it.

"No," she said. "I don't suppose you would."
