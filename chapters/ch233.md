# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter Two Hundred and Thirty-Three — Finding Liang Jingzhi

She wasn't difficult to find. That was, in its own way, part of what unsettled him about the situation — a twenty-six-year-old PhD candidate with a public university profile, a departmental page listing her research interests and contact information, a modest academic presence that anyone with an internet connection and a few minutes could assemble into a reasonably complete picture.

He read everything publicly available about her before he decided anything else.

Liang Jingzhi. Born in Chongqing, undergraduate degree from Sichuan University, now three years into a doctoral program focused on regional seismology and subsurface mapping. Her departmental bio described her research as *investigating anomalous density signatures in tectonically stable zones* — careful, hedged language that matched the tone of the paper itself, the writing of someone who reported only what her data actually supported.

He found a recorded conference presentation from the previous year, fifteen minutes of footage, and watched it twice.

She spoke clearly, without unnecessary hedging once she was past the careful academic throat-clearing of an introduction, the kind of confidence that came from genuinely understanding her own data rather than performing confidence she didn't have. She fielded a skeptical question from an older professor in the audience without defensiveness, simply walking him through the methodology again, patient and precise, until his skepticism visibly eased.

He sat with the footage paused on her face, mid-explanation, and thought again about the cost of what he was considering.

He printed her paper. He annotated it carefully, in the margins, with the actual explanations — not the full truth, not yet, but enough correction to the geological framework she'd been working within that anyone who understood what he'd written would recognize immediately that her density variations weren't geological at all.

Then he sat with the annotated paper for a long time, turning the decision over from every angle he could find.

---

Chen Yufei, when he showed her the annotated pages, didn't answer immediately either.

"You're more careful with this than you were with me," she said finally.

"I am. I think about it differently now than I did two years ago." He looked at the printed pages, his own handwriting crowding the margins of someone else's careful, honest work. "With you, I corrected your meditation framework because it was sitting right in front of me and the correction seemed obviously useful. I didn't think hard enough, at the time, about what knowing the truth would actually cost you — the attention it would draw, the choices it would force."

"And now?"

"Now I think about it before I act, instead of after." He met her eyes. "She's twenty-six. She has a career she's built carefully, a research path she clearly cares about, and a discovery that's either going to fade into academic obscurity the way most papers do, or attract exactly the kind of attention that could put her in real danger if the wrong people decide her data is worth more than her safety."

"You're asking whether you have the right to make that choice for her."

"I'm asking whether contacting her at all is fair to her, when she has no way to consent to what she'd be agreeing to before she already knows enough to understand the choice."

Chen Yufei considered this for a long moment, the same careful weighing she brought to most genuinely difficult questions.

"You can't protect her from her own discovery by staying silent," she said finally. "Her paper is already published. The data's already public. If you don't reach her first, someone else eventually will — and you have no guarantee that someone else approaches her with anything resembling the caution you're agonizing over right now."

He'd already arrived at roughly the same conclusion, turning it over alone for the better part of two days. Hearing her say it plainly settled something that had been sitting unresolved in his chest since he'd first read the paper.

"I'll reach out," he said. "Carefully. I'll tell her enough to be honest about who I am and why I'm contacting her, without handing her everything at once before she's decided whether she wants to know it."

"That's about as fair as this situation allows for."

He nodded, and began composing the message — not through any official channel, but a simple, direct email to the address listed on her departmental page, identifying himself honestly as someone with information relevant to her recent paper, and asking, plainly, whether she'd be willing to meet.

He sent it before he could talk himself into further delay, and sat afterward with the particular weight of having set something in motion that he could no longer fully control the shape of.
