# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 487 — Wei Canglan's Question

Wei Canglan raised it during an otherwise routine administrative briefing, delivered with the same careful, direct honesty he brought to every objection he'd ever filed across five years of steady, essential partnership.

"I have a question I've been sitting with for two weeks," he said, closing the folder he'd been working through. "I want to ask it properly, not as criticism exactly, but because I don't think anyone else is going to ask it, and I think it deserves asking."

"Go ahead," Lin Mushi said, recognizing, from the particular careful weight of the preamble, that whatever followed mattered considerably more than the ordinary briefing had suggested.

"Do they actually know?" Wei Canglan said. "The three hundred and twenty-three. Not the technical mechanism — Dr. Shen Yue's screening covers that thoroughly, I've seen the materials. I mean the actual thing they're agreeing to. Sustained presence inside something this vast, this abstract, for an unknown duration, possibly years. Can any of them genuinely consent to that, or is informed consent simply not a coherent concept at this scale?"

Lin Mushi sat with the question, recognizing its shape immediately — the same old question, applied now to something considerably larger than anything it had previously been asked about. "You asked this before," he said slowly. "About the disclosure protocol. About the governments we were bringing into contact with all of this."

"I did. And I'll be honest — I think you've gotten considerably better at this since then. The screening process, the orientation previews, the explicit permission to withdraw without shame — all of that is more careful than anything we built during the disclosure. I want that acknowledged plainly, because it's true and it matters." He paused, choosing his next words with visible care. "But I don't think better process fully answers the underlying question. Some things might simply be too large for genuine informed consent to apply to cleanly, regardless of how carefully you build the process around them. And I worry, sometimes, that you've become skilled enough at building careful process that it's harder for anyone — including you — to notice when the process is doing more work than actual understanding ever could."

Lin Mushi didn't answer immediately, letting the challenge settle rather than reaching for a comfortable rebuttal. It was, he recognized, exactly the kind of question that deserved sitting with rather than resolving quickly.

"I don't have a clean answer," he admitted finally. "I want to be honest about that, rather than pretending I've already settled something this difficult. I think you're right that scale changes what consent can actually mean. A person can understand, intellectually, that they're agreeing to sustained presence inside a dissolution zone. I don't know if intellectual understanding and genuine, felt comprehension are the same thing, not at this size. Liang Jingzhi herself told me the preview sessions only partially prepared her for what sustained contact actually felt like."

"So what do we do with that?"

"I think," Lin Mushi said slowly, working the answer out as he spoke it, "we do what we've always done when the honest answer is uncertain. We build the most careful process available, we stay genuinely open to withdrawal at every stage, and we don't pretend the process has fully solved a problem it might not be possible to fully solve. I'd rather operate honestly inside that uncertainty than convince myself I've resolved something I actually haven't."

Wei Canglan considered that, something easing slightly in his posture — not full satisfaction, Lin Mushi noted, but the particular respect he reserved for honesty that didn't try to paper over genuine difficulty.

"That's a better answer than I expected," he said. "I still don't think it's a complete one."

"I don't think it is either," Lin Mushi admitted. "I think that's probably the most honest thing I can say about it."

"Keep asking me this question," he added, after a moment. "Not just about the 323. About whatever comes next, whatever scale I end up building consent processes around in the future. I think I need someone who keeps checking whether my process has actually gotten better, or whether it's just gotten better at feeling like it has."

"I intend to," Wei Canglan said, with the particular dry certainty that had, over five years, become one of the Strata's most reliable constants. "It's rather become my favorite part of the job."

He nodded, and found, sitting with the unresolved weight of the question afterward, that he trusted it considerably more than he would have trusted an easy answer.
