# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter Two Hundred and Ninety-Eight — Wei Canglan's Breakthrough

The breakthrough arrived, as most of the meaningful ones did, sideways — not during the deliberate work Wei Canglan had been grinding through for three exhausting weeks, but in the middle of an entirely unrelated conversation about something else.

Lin Mushi was helping him move a crate of reclaimed piping toward the workshop district's east wing, the two of them talking about nothing in particular — the carpenter and technician's competing joints, now a month into their trial and both, apparently, holding up reasonably well — when Wei Canglan mentioned, almost as an aside, that he'd started dreaming about the compression ratio problem, the geometry surfacing in his sleep the way stubborn problems sometimes did.

"What does it look like, in the dreams?" Lin Mushi asked, mostly making conversation, not expecting the question to matter.

"Wrong," Wei Canglan said. "Every time. I dream I've solved it and then I wake up and the solution doesn't survive contact with actually being awake. Last night I dreamed the fourth fold wasn't beneath the third at all. It was beside it. Which doesn't make sense, obviously, given how the first three ratios stack."

Lin Mushi set his end of the crate down, something in the offhand description snagging against a thought he hadn't fully finished having yet.

"Say that again."

"Which part?"

"Beside it. Not beneath it."

Wei Canglan set his own end down slowly, his attention sharpening with the specific speed Lin Mushi had come to associate with him recognizing, before he'd fully understood why, that something important had just been said. "It's just a dream image. It doesn't mean anything."

"Maybe not. But you told me, three weeks ago, that the fourth ratio keeps drifting about two-thirds through the fold. What if it's not drifting because the geometry is unstable. What if it's drifting because you're forcing a stacked structure onto something that actually wants to sit alongside the third ratio instead of underneath it."

Wei Canglan went very still, the crate forgotten entirely now, his eyes fixed somewhere past Lin Mushi's shoulder at nothing in particular — the specific stillness of someone doing rapid, silent calculation behind an otherwise unreadable expression.

"That would explain the drift," he said slowly. "If I stopped trying to stack it and instead treated the fourth fold as parallel — sharing the third ratio's boundary rather than extending past it —"

He didn't finish the sentence. He was already moving, abandoning the crate entirely and heading back toward his workspace at something close to a run, leaving Lin Mushi standing alone in the corridor with a faint, satisfied smile he didn't bother hiding.

---

He found Wei Canglan four hours later, well past the point where the crate should have been fully unloaded, hunched over a fresh set of diagrams with an expression Lin Mushi hadn't seen on him in three exhausting weeks — not the tight, frustrated focus of someone grinding against a wall, but the open, absorbed concentration of someone finally moving.

"It worked," Wei Canglan said, without looking up, before Lin Mushi had said anything at all.

"Show me."

Wei Canglan turned the diagram around, the geometry considerably cleaner than anything Lin Mushi had seen from him over the past three weeks — the fourth compression fold sitting exactly where the offhand dream image had suggested, parallel rather than stacked, sharing a boundary with the third ratio instead of collapsing beneath it.

"It held," Wei Canglan said. "I ran it eleven times. Clean geometry every time, no drift, no collapse."

"That's not a small solution."

"It's not a solution I found through effort," Wei Canglan said, something almost bewildered in his voice. "I spent three weeks doing everything correctly and got nowhere. Then you asked me about a dream, offhandedly, in the middle of moving a crate, and the entire problem reorganized itself in about four seconds."

"That happens sometimes. Effort builds the ground. The answer doesn't always arrive through the same door the effort was digging."

Wei Canglan sat back from the diagram, some of the exhaustion of the past three weeks visibly draining out of him now that it no longer had a problem to hold itself against. "I want to say something, and I want you to actually hear it rather than deflecting it."

"Go ahead."

"You didn't solve this for me. You asked one question, at exactly the right moment, without trying to solve it yourself." He met Lin Mushi's eyes directly. "That's a specific kind of teaching. I've had teachers who solved problems for their students to demonstrate mastery. I've had teachers who withheld help entirely, believing struggle alone built character. You do neither. You wait until the exact right question is useful, and then you ask it, and then you step back and let the person actually finish the work themselves."

Lin Mushi felt the observation land with more weight than a simpler compliment would have carried — not flattery, but the specific, careful accuracy of someone who'd spent months studying how he worked and had finally articulated something he hadn't fully named for himself.

"I had a good teacher," he said simply. "I'm mostly copying what worked on me."

"Ren Wuji."

"Mostly Ren Wuji. Some Meen-4. Some just watching what actually helps versus what only looks like helping."

Wei Canglan nodded slowly, returning his attention to the clean, finally-correct geometry in front of him, something settled and steady in his posture that hadn't been there three weeks earlier.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I think you're better at it than either of them."

Lin Mushi didn't have an answer to that, and found, standing there in the workshop's fading afternoon light, that he didn't especially need one.
