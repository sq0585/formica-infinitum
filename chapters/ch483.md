# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 483 — What Liang Jingzhi Experiences

He found her in the geology department's small shared office, the same unglamorous room she'd worked from since long before any of this began, a cup of tea cooling beside her keyboard exactly the way it always did when she was absorbed in something that mattered. It had been eleven days since the node settled into place. He'd given her that long on purpose, understanding that whatever answer she gave him now would be truer than anything she could have offered in the first raw week of it.

"I wanted to check on you properly," he said, settling into the chair across from her desk. "Not through Meen-4's monitoring. In person."

"I appreciate that," she said, closing the laptop she'd been reviewing coherence readings on. "Though I should warn you, the monitoring data and the in-person version are going to say something similar. I've had eleven days to find better words for it than the ones I gave Meen-4's official report, if you'd like to hear them."

"Please."

She considered the question a moment, the particular careful precision she brought to everything, weighing her own experience the way she'd once weighed geological samples, patiently, before offering a conclusion she trusted.

"I am here," she said finally, "sitting in this office, with tea going cold beside my keyboard, in a building I've worked in for years. That part hasn't changed. I still notice when the heating's too warm. I still get hungry around noon."

"And the other part?"

"The other part is different now," she said, "from what it was even six weeks ago, when I was just one contact point among three hundred and twenty-three. I am also, permanently, a fraction of myself standing at the center of forty million years of absence in the Void Lattice's memory, and I am making it present again. Not remembering it — the zone doesn't hold memory the way a person holds memory. Presence, absence, presence again, held now by something in me that doesn't come back when I stop paying attention to it, the way the general network's contact points can rest between shifts. This part doesn't rest. It's simply there now, the way my own hand is there whether or not I'm looking at it."

He sat with that, feeling the weight of it settle heavier than it ever had in any of their previous conversations about this. "Is it painful?"

"No," she said, and he could tell, from the specific certainty of her answer, that she'd already asked herself the question rigorously, more than once, before he'd arrived to ask it for her. "That surprised me less this time than it did the first time you asked, months ago, when this was still temporary. It isn't painful. It is permanent, and very large, and those are different things from pain. Pain would suggest something is wrong. This isn't wrong. It's simply a fact about what I am now, the way it's a fact that I have two hands instead of one — not a wound, just an addition I didn't have before and will have for the rest of my life."

"How do you hold both at once? The office, the tea, and — all of that, permanently?"

She smiled slightly, though something in it carried more weight than the dry warmth he remembered from before. "The same way you taught all of us to hold everything else, I suspect, except I don't get to set it down at the end of the day the way the rest of the network does. I can still feel my tea getting cold on my desk. I can still feel forty million years of absence becoming present again through the part of me that's standing at the zone's own center now, permanently, whether I'm thinking about it or not. Both things are true at the same time, every single day, for however long the rest of my life turns out to be. I don't think holding them together requires choosing one as more real than the other. I think it requires exactly what the Anchor Protocol was always built for — staying located, staying specific, while something considerably larger has become a permanent part of the same attention."

He looked at her for a long moment, understanding, with a specific and considerable weight, exactly what she'd actually given — not a temporary contribution among many, but an irreversible addition to who she now was, offered because it was, in the truest sense, an extension of exactly the person she'd already spent her whole life being.

"You didn't have to be the one," he said quietly. "I want you to know I understand that. There were other ways to build this."

"I know there were," she said. "I chose this one anyway, and I would choose it again, and I need you to actually believe that rather than simply accept it out of guilt. I am not diminished, Lin Mushi. I am not a sacrifice you allowed to happen. I am a geologist who spent her whole career wanting to be present for something enormous and unwitnessed, and I finally got to be exactly that. Permanently. On purpose."

He felt something in his chest tighten and ease at once, the particular, complicated weight of gratitude that had no comfortable place to settle. "Thank you," he said. "For being the one holding that specific piece of it. For the rest of your life."

"Thank you for building something worth holding it for," she said, and reached for her tea, finding it, as always, gone cold, and drinking it anyway — the same steady woman she had always been, permanently, now, considerably more than she had been before.
