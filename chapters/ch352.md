# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter Three Hundred and Fifty-Two — The Mother's Call

The call came at its usual Sunday hour, the small, steady ritual he'd maintained across two years of increasingly impossible circumstances, his mother's number appearing on his phone with the same unremarkable regularity it always had, as though the world hadn't spent the past several months quietly reorganizing itself around review clocks and cosmological structures and an AI system now folded permanently into his own mind.

"Mushi," she said, when he picked up, her voice carrying the same warm, practical directness it always had. "You sound tired."

He almost laughed, the observation landing with a specific, grounding accuracy that cut cleanly through everything else currently occupying his attention. "I am tired. It's been a demanding few months."

"Demanding how? You're always vague about the specifics. I've stopped expecting details, but I haven't stopped noticing when you sound like this."

He considered, briefly, how to answer honestly without answering fully — the same careful balance he'd maintained for two years now, protecting her from a truth she couldn't safely carry while refusing to simply lie to her outright. "I've taken on a lot of responsibility. Managing something considerably larger than what I was managing even six months ago. Some of it's gone well. Some of it's been harder than I expected."

"Are you eating properly?"

The question arrived with such characteristic, unhesitating practicality that he found himself, despite everything, genuinely smiling. "I'm eating fine, Ma."

"You say that every time, and every time I have to decide whether to believe you."

"I promise. Chen Yufei makes sure of it, if my own discipline slips."

"Good. I like her. You should tell her I said that, next time you see her." A pause, the particular quality of silence that meant she was working up to something. "Are you managing all of it, though? Whatever it actually is you won't tell me the specifics of. Not just eating. Managing."

---

He sat with the question longer than he usually let himself sit with anything she asked, the specific, uncomplicated love in it landing with more weight than its plain phrasing suggested — not curiosity about the details, which she'd long since learned not to expect, but a mother's simple, direct check on whether her son was actually holding up under whatever weight he was carrying.

"I think I am," he said honestly. "Some days better than others. This week's been one of the harder ones — a big decision, something I had to accept full responsibility for, in a way that felt significant even beyond the practical details."

"Did you make the right decision?"

"I think so. I'm fairly confident I did. But confident isn't the same as certain, and I think that's been sitting with me more than the decision itself."

"That sounds like most decisions worth making," she said, something warm and wise entering her voice, the particular quality of a lifetime's accumulated practical experience distilled into a single, simple observation. "The easy decisions rarely need this much thought afterward. If you're still sitting with it, it was probably worth the weight."

He felt something loosen in his chest at that, the specific, uncomplicated comfort of being cared for by someone who had no idea what she was actually reassuring him about, and somehow, entirely without meaning to, had said exactly the right thing anyway.

---

"How's your own week been?" he asked, deliberately shifting the conversation's weight toward her, wanting the ordinary rhythm of their usual call to reassert itself.

"Ordinary," she said, and told him about it anyway, with the same unhurried, practical detail she always brought — a dispute with a neighbor over shared garden space, finally resolved; a recipe she'd been perfecting for weeks, the balance still not quite right; a friend's granddaughter's upcoming wedding, the specific, small dramas of an ordinary life continuing steadily, unbothered, entirely separate from everything he currently carried.

He listened, fully present in a way he'd learned, across months of Chen Yufei's patient teaching about the pauses, to make deliberate rather than automatic — no Colony Link drift, no Amber-0 flag pulling his attention elsewhere, just his mother's voice, warm and ordinary and entirely, wonderfully unremarkable.

"I should let you go," she said, eventually, the call reaching its natural close the way it always did. "You sound like you need sleep more than you need to keep talking to your mother."

"I always need to talk to my mother," he said, meaning it more fully than the casual phrasing suggested.

"Good answer." He could hear the smile in her voice. "Eat properly. Sleep when you can. And whatever this big decision was — I trust you made it carefully. You always do."

He sat with the phone in his hand for a long moment after she'd hung up, the ordinary, grounding weight of her voice still settling into him, and understood, sitting there in the quiet of his own room, that this small, steady Sunday ritual — a mother checking whether her son was eating, entirely unaware of Amber-0 or Void Lattices or eighteen-month review clocks — remained, after everything this impossible world had handed him, one of the most important anchors he had.
