# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 371 — The Disclosure Decision — Made

He did not decide it in a single dramatic moment. That was, he thought afterward, probably the most honest thing he could say about it — that the decision arrived the way most of his real decisions arrived, by accumulation, until one morning he woke up and found it had already been made somewhere underneath his conscious attention, and all that remained was to write it down.

The morning it surfaced, he was sitting at his mother's kitchen table, which was not where he did his most important thinking by design, but had become, over three years, the place where his most important thinking tended to finish. She was cutting vegetables at the counter with her back to him, humming something tuneless and content, and he was supposed to be helping and was instead staring at the wall, turning over three hundred and forty pages of Opening Submission and a warning from Lady Shen and a lesson from Meen-4 about a genocide-shaped fear that was, underneath its cruelty, not entirely wrong.

*Cut faster,* his mother said, without turning around, *or I'm taking the knife back.*

He cut faster. And somewhere in the small, ordinary rhythm of it — the knife against the board, the particular smell of ginger, his mother's humming — the shape of the decision finally came clear enough to write down.

He wrote it that night, in the notebook, in the same plain handwriting he'd used three years ago to record the first uncertain observations of a Codex he didn't understand.

*The Collective's review is partly a judgment of what Earth is. What Earth is cannot be represented accurately by secrecy. If I want them to see us clearly, we must be visible. Not to the Surveyor alone. To the world that's being reviewed. It cannot go on knowing less about itself than the thing judging it knows.*

He read it back twice. It felt, on the page, smaller than the decision actually was — a handful of sentences standing in for a choice that would touch every institution, every government, every person who had spent three years living beside something extraordinary without knowing its name.

He called Fang Ruxue first, because she would need the most lead time, and because she deserved to hear it from him rather than discover it unfolding.

"You're sure," she said, after he'd read her the notebook entry over a connection that crackled faintly with distance.

"No," he admitted. "I'm not sure it's the right decision. I'm sure it's the honest one, and I've run out of confidence that those are different things I get to choose between separately."

A long pause on her end. He could hear, faintly, the sound of her own kettle behind her voice, the particular domestic static of someone standing in a kitchen much like his mother's, doing something ordinary while something enormous settled into place.

"Then we'll need a plan," she said finally. "Not a leak. Not a scramble. Controlled. Staged. You've thought this far — have you thought further?"

"Some. Not enough yet."

"Then that's tomorrow's problem. Tonight, just — " she stopped, and when she continued her voice had softened into something less administrative. "Tonight, just know that I think you're doing the harder thing on purpose, and I think that's usually the sign you're doing the right one. Sleep, if you can."

He didn't sleep much. But he lay in the dark afterward with the notebook closed on the table beside him and felt, for the first time in weeks, something that wasn't quite peace but was adjacent to it — the particular stillness that came, he was learning, not from certainty, but from having finally stopped circling a question and simply answered it.

Three years ago he had been a convenience store clerk who noticed things other people missed. He was still, underneath everything the years had built on top of him, exactly that person — someone who believed, stubbornly and without much evidence that the belief was strategically wise, that the truth handled carefully was almost always safer in the long run than a lie maintained forever.

He thought of Ren Wuji, briefly, the way he did at moments like this — not grief exactly anymore, three years had worn grief down into something gentler, more like company. *I hope this is what you would have done,* he thought, not really to anyone, and then, because it felt true and he had learned to trust the things that felt true at three in the morning: *I hope it doesn't matter whether it is. I think it's what I have to do regardless.*

He turned off the light, and somewhere at the edge of his awareness, distributed thin across a planet that did not yet know how thoroughly it was about to be asked to see itself, the Colony Link hummed on, patient, waiting for morning.
