# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 454 — Deciding to Tell Her

He didn't decide it all at once, in the end, despite how badly some part of him wanted the decision to arrive the way his breakthroughs usually did — a single clean threshold, crossed and then behind him. It arrived instead the way most of the decisions that had actually mattered in his life had arrived: slowly, in pieces, until one evening he noticed the pieces had already assembled themselves into something whole, and all that remained was admitting it out loud.

He sat in the pocket dimension laboratory with the notebook open, the same one he'd carried, in one form or another, since a dying man's hand had first closed around his in a convenience store five years ago, and wrote the decision plainly, because plain was the only way he trusted himself to write something this significant without dressing it up into more than it needed to be.

*Observation 442: I am going to tell her everything.*

*Not because the Collective requires disclosure to her specifically — they don't, and I checked, more than once, in the quiet hope that some external obligation might make this decision for me and spare me the harder work of choosing it myself. Not because the extension protocol technically demands it — Meen-4 was careful to note that consent could, in principle, be obtained through a narrower, more limited explanation, if I wanted to protect her from the fuller truth even now.*

*I am telling her because she raised me. Because for thirty years before any of this began, she got up before dawn to open a small apartment's worth of ordinary love around a boy who had no idea, yet, how large his life was eventually going to become. Because every Sunday call for five years has been, in its own quiet way, a small dishonesty dressed as kindness — protecting her from a truth I decided, unilaterally, she didn't need or couldn't handle, without ever once actually asking her.*

*I am telling her because I do not want to ask her to accept something enormous from a version of her son she has never actually been allowed to fully know. That would not be consent. That would be asking her to trust a stranger wearing her son's voice.*

He read the entry back twice, and found, the second time through, that his hands had gone slightly unsteady, the particular tremor he associated less with fear of the unknown and more with the specific vertigo of a decision that could not, once acted on, ever be quietly undone.

He thought of every reason he'd used, across five years, to justify the careful omission — she's happier not knowing, she's safer not knowing, the world isn't ready and neither is she, I'm protecting her the way she once protected me from things too large for a child to carry. He turned each reason over now, honestly, and found each one, examined directly, revealed the same uncomfortable shape underneath its kindness.

Fear. Not fear for her. Fear of the moment where she finally saw the whole of what he'd become, and decided, for reasons entirely her own to hold, that she didn't recognize the man standing in front of her anymore.

*That fear is mine to carry,* he wrote, finally, underlining it with more pressure than the sentence strictly required. *It is not a good enough reason to keep denying her the choice that's rightfully hers. Whatever she decides — about the extension, about me, about any of it — she deserves to decide it as a fully informed adult, not as someone I've spent five years quietly managing for her own comfort.*

He closed the notebook and sat for a long while in the laboratory's warm, familiar light, the decision settled now in a way that felt less like relief and more like the specific, steady weight of finally standing at the edge of something he could no longer step back from.

He called her the next morning, before he could let one more day of careful omission accumulate on top of the thirty years already spent.

"Mā," he said, when she answered, using the word he hadn't reached for informally in longer than he could remember, "can I come by tonight? There's something I need to tell you. All of it, this time. I should have told you a long time ago."

There was a pause on the line, brief, and then her voice, steady in the particular way it had always been steady for every difficult thing life had ever handed either of them.

"I'll put the kettle on," she said. "Come whenever you're ready. I'm not going anywhere."

He held the phone a moment after she'd hung up, and found, standing there, that he was more frightened of the evening ahead than he had been of almost anything the past five years had shown him — and more certain, at the same time, that it was finally, honestly, time.
