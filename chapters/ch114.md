# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter One Hundred and Fourteen — The First Lesson

She came back the next day.

He had arrived at the archive at his usual time, found his usual section of the shelves, begun his usual work. He was aware of her presence fifteen minutes after arriving — the specific Qi-field quality he'd mapped over three weeks of her watching him, now familiar enough that he clocked it as *she is here* without having to locate her specifically.

Today she did not go to the east wall.

She sat across from him.

She watched him work for thirty minutes.

Then she put her forelimb on the table between them — a gesture he had observed the archive worker use when giving him the notebook, a gesture he had come to associate with *I have something for you* — and emitted a communication register signal.

He caught eighty percent. He was getting faster at this.

The signal: *Show me what you have derived for Scale Walking Ratio 2 technique.*

He looked at his notebooks. He found the section — the formal derivation he'd built from the Standard Protocol's geometry and the Scale Walking manual's practical framework, the personal reconstruction of the technique from first principles. He had done this the way he did all technical work: not copying the instructions but deriving from them, making sure he understood why each step was what it was, building the understanding independently enough that he could explain it without the source material.

He turned the notebook to face her.

She looked at it.

She read it for approximately twenty minutes without moving. He went back to his translation work. A Formican reading his notebooks at the table across from him was — he noted — something he had become comfortable with faster than he would have predicted.

When she looked up, the signal she sent was longer than anything she'd said to him yet.

He caught sixty percent, which was not enough to parse the details.

He picked up his pen. In the communication register — he was now writing notation rather than speaking it for most of his Strata exchanges, the written form being easier for him to control precisely — he wrote: *Can you slow the technical portion?*

She regarded him for a moment.

Then she repeated the technical portion of the signal at approximately half speed.

He caught eighty-five percent.

She had found his three errors. She was explaining why they were errors. Not in abstract terms — in specific geometric terms, showing him where in his derivation the geometry diverged from the correct architecture and what the consequences of the divergence would be at Ratio 4, where the pressure on the interface geometry was significantly higher than at Ratio 2.

Error one: he had derived the compression geometry's primary axis from the body's anatomical centerline. The correct primary axis was the Chitin Seed's integration spine, which was related to but not identical to the anatomical centerline. The divergence was small enough at Ratio 2 to be negligible. At Ratio 4, it would create interface instability in the peripheral channels.

Error two: his re-expansion technique had a timing asymmetry. The compression could be held stably, but the re-expansion timing he'd derived was expanding the outer channels before the core, which was backwards. At Ratio 2 this produced the slight jolt he'd been experiencing on exits. At Ratio 4, it would produce a re-expansion failure.

Error three: he had not accounted for the Colony Link's interaction with the compression geometry. At Ratio 3 and above, the Colony Link field became a structural element of the interface rather than a perceptual output. He had been developing Colony Link as a perception capability. He needed to develop it as a structural support capability.

He wrote each correction in his notebook as she explained it.

When she finished, he read his notes back to her in the communication register to confirm his comprehension.

She sent back a confirmation signal that he read as *correct.*

She did not explain why she was helping.

He asked, in the register: *Why are you telling me this?*

She looked at him for a moment. Then: *The errors will cause problems at Ratio 4.*

He waited, because this was not a complete answer.

She did not offer a more complete one.

She stood.

She left.

He sat in the archive with his corrected derivation and his three new annotations and the specific quality of gratitude that arrived when someone with better knowledge than you used it on your behalf without obligation.

He had been thinking of the Formicans as the people whose civilization he was studying. They were that. They were also people — which was a thing he had understood abstractly and was now understanding practically, because a person had sat across from him and chosen to spend two hours on his errors.

She could have not told him.

She had told him.

He wrote at the bottom of the page:

> *Note: find out her name/designation. Not because I need it, but because she has been present in my work for three weeks and I would like to know what to call her in my notes.*

He turned back to the translation document.

He had sixty-two pages drafted. He needed sixty-five before it was ready.

Three more pages.

He worked until the pages were done.
