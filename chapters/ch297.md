# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter Two Hundred and Ninety-Seven — Fang Ruxue's Framework

Fang Ruxue brought the document to him rolled into a tube, which struck him as unnecessarily formal until he unrolled it and understood the scale of what she'd actually drafted — not a memo, not a proposal, but something closer to a constitution, dense with sections and subsections, the specific thoroughness of someone who had spent thirty-one years watching institutions fail through vagueness and had decided, this time, not to repeat the mistake.

"Governance framework," she said, settling into the chair across from him with the unhurried precision that had become, over the past year, one of the Strata's most reliable constants. "For the resident community, specifically. Dispute resolution, resource allocation, workspace assignment. I've kept it deliberately narrow — I'm not attempting to govern the Strata's cultivation or Archive functions. Just the parts that are becoming, unmistakably, a human and Formican community living and working together."

He scanned the document, recognizing within the first page the same careful precision she'd brought to the Cultivation Research Collective's founding structure — clear escalation paths, defined authority, nothing left ambiguous enough to become a future argument.

"You've built dispute resolution around a rotating panel," he said. "Not a single arbiter."

"Deliberately. A single arbiter concentrates authority in one person, which works until that person is unavailable, or biased, or simply wrong about something specific. A rotating panel of three — one human resident, one Formican resident, one neutral party drawn from outside the immediate dispute — distributes the judgment enough that no single mistake becomes structurally catastrophic."

"Who selects the neutral party?"

"Currently, I do. I'd like that to change within the year, once the community's large enough to select its own arbitration pool. For now, someone needs to hold that function while the structure's still finding its feet, and I'd rather it be someone with no material stake in most disputes than someone who has one."

He set the document down, looking at her directly. "You're proposing yourself as the Strata's administrator."

"I'm proposing myself as the Strata's administrator for exactly as long as it takes to build a structure that doesn't require me specifically." Something dry entered her voice. "I spent thirty-one years watching university departments collapse under chairs who couldn't imagine stepping down. I have no intention of becoming what I spent three decades criticizing."

---

He read the framework in full that evening, alone, the specific care of the document revealing itself more fully the deeper he went — provisions for workspace disputes modeled loosely on the carpenter-and-technician friction he'd watched resolve itself the week before, resource allocation rules built around transparency rather than authority, a deliberate structural humility running through every section that assumed, correctly, that the framework itself would need revision as the community grew.

He found Fang Ruxue the following morning to tell her so.

"I want to back this publicly," he said. "Not as something I'm imposing. As something I'm supporting, with you as the acknowledged authority over how it's administered."

She studied him for a moment, the particular assessing look she gave anything she suspected of being more complicated than it first appeared. "You could simply become the arbiter yourself. Most communities would defer to you regardless of what any document said."

"I know. That's exactly why I don't want to."

"Explain."

He considered how to say it honestly. "Every structure I've built in this world so far has had me at the center of it, by necessity — there wasn't anyone else positioned to hold it. That's not true anymore. You've built something better than anything I'd build if I tried to do this myself, because you're not distracted by everything else I'm carrying, and because you actually understand governance in a way I've only ever approximated." He paused. "If I become the arbiter, this community learns to defer to me instead of to the structure. That's fragile. Everything depends on my continued presence and my continued good judgment, forever. I'd rather build something that survives me being distracted, or wrong, or simply unavailable."

Something shifted in Fang Ruxue's expression — not quite surprise, but the recognition of having been understood more precisely than she'd expected to be.

"That's a more mature answer than I expected," she said.

"I've had good examples," he said. "Watching how carefully you built the Collective's structure taught me most of what I know about this."

She didn't respond to the compliment directly, occupying herself instead with rolling the framework document back into its tube with the same brisk efficiency she brought to everything, though something in the set of her shoulders had eased, fractionally, into something that looked, if he was reading it correctly, like quiet pride.

"I'll present it to the resident council next week," she said. "With your public backing, if you're certain."

"I'm certain."

"Good." She stood, tube in hand, already halfway toward the door and the next item on whatever list governed her day. "Then let's build something that doesn't need either of us, eventually. That's the actual test of whether we did this correctly."

He watched her go, the framework already shaping something he suspected, though he couldn't yet say precisely how, would matter considerably more in the months ahead than its careful, unglamorous language currently let on.
