# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter One Hundred and Seventy-Two — Wei Canglan's Defection

Wei Canglan arrived at Chen Yufei's apartment on a Sunday evening, with a single bag and an expression that gave away nothing.

He had been staying at a friend's place since the proceedings concluded — Chen Yufei learned this only when he mentioned, almost in passing, that the friend's lease had a no-extended-guests clause and he didn't want to cause complications. He said it the way someone describes a minor logistical detail rather than the loss of the last place he had to stay.

Chen Yufei let him in without ceremony. She had been expecting this, in the sense that she'd told Lin Mushi two days ago that he might come, but the actual arrival still required the specific gentleness she'd developed over the past several months — the same gentleness she'd needed from Lin Mushi during her own expulsion, now offered forward to someone else.

"You can stay here," she said, before he'd finished setting the bag down. "As long as you need."

"I don't want to impose—"

"You're not imposing. You're someone who made a difficult, correct decision and is paying a cost for it that I understand from direct experience. Sit down. I'll make tea."

Lin Mushi arrived an hour later, having been told by Chen Yufei that Wei Canglan was there and might appreciate company, or might appreciate solitude, and that he should come and figure out which.

He found Wei Canglan sitting very still at the small kitchen table, a cup of tea in front of him that had clearly gone cold without being touched.

Lin Mushi sat down across from him. He did not say *this was the right thing to do.* He had decided, walking over, that this was exactly the wrong thing to say — true, perhaps, but useless and slightly obscene in the face of what Wei Canglan was actually sitting with.

"What do you need right now?" he asked instead.

Wei Canglan looked at him for a long moment.

"Somewhere to sleep," he said. "Something to eat. And no one telling me it was worth it yet."

Lin Mushi nodded.

"I can do the first two," he said. "I can definitely manage the third."

Something in Wei Canglan's face shifted — not quite relief, something closer to the specific relaxation of someone who has braced for a particular kind of pressure and found it absent.

Lin Mushi got up and went to the small kitchen. He found instant noodles in the cabinet — Chen Yufei kept some for late nights at the archive — and made two bowls, badly, with more attention to speed than craft.

He set one in front of Wei Canglan.

They ate in silence.

It was not an uncomfortable silence. It was the specific kind of silence that two people share when neither needs the other to perform anything — not gratitude, not encouragement, not philosophical framing. Just noodles, and the simple fact of being fed, and the absence of anyone insisting that the pain currently present needed to be reframed as meaningful before it was allowed to simply be pain.

When they finished, Wei Canglan said, without looking up from the empty bowl: "My parents are renting a single room now. My mother got a job for the first time in twenty years. She's fifty-three."

Lin Mushi didn't say anything. He let it sit.

"My sister found out it was because of me," Wei Canglan continued. "She hasn't said much about it. I don't know if that means she's angry or just doesn't know what to say."

"Probably both," Lin Mushi said. "Those aren't mutually exclusive."

Wei Canglan looked up at him.

"You're not going to tell me I did the right thing," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I think you did," Lin Mushi said. "But I think you knowing that, or not knowing that, isn't actually the thing you need from me right now. You asked for noodles and no speeches. I'm trying to give you that."

Wei Canglan was quiet for a long moment.

"Thank you," he said.

It was the most honest exchange either of them had had in days, and it had taken almost nothing — two bowls of badly made instant noodles, and the simple discipline of not saying more than was needed.

Wei Canglan stayed that night, and the next, and the relationship that began over cold tea and instant noodles became, over the following months, one of the most important in both their lives.

But that was still ahead.

Tonight was just noodles, and silence, and the absence of speeches.
