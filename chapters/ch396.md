# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 396 — Liang Jingzhi's Paper — Aftermath

Liang Jingzhi found him in the Circuit monitoring room with a printed copy of a citation report clutched in one hand like she wasn't entirely sure it was real, and something in her expression — half delight, half a kind of stunned disbelief — made him set down the diagnostics tablet immediately.

"It's the most downloaded physics paper of the year," she said, before he'd even asked. "Not subfield. Not regional. The year. Across every major repository that tracks it."

He took the printout from her and scanned the numbers, feeling a small, genuine warmth spread through the exhaustion of the past weeks. "That's not a small thing."

"It's not a small thing," she agreed, sitting down heavily across from him, the printout still crumpled slightly in her grip. "Three separate research groups have already begun partial replication attempts on the subsurface density work. Not full replication — the Strata anchor anomalies require access to data they don't have, obviously — but the parts they can independently verify, they're verifying. Geologists I respected as an undergraduate, whose papers I used to cite with something close to reverence, are now citing mine."

"How does it feel?"

She was quiet a moment, turning the question over with the same careful precision she brought to any measurement. "Strange," she admitted finally. "I spent three years publishing careful half-truths, dancing around the actual explanation because I knew the actual explanation would sound insane without the context I couldn't share. And now the full truth is out, and instead of being dismissed as insane, it's being treated as the most exciting geological discovery in a decade. I don't know how to feel about being right for reasons I couldn't tell anyone until now."

He understood that feeling more than he let on immediately — the particular vertigo of finally being believed, after years of necessary secrecy had trained you to expect disbelief as the default response to anything true.

"The uproar," he said, "is it what you expected?"

"Bigger. The subsurface density structures are generating actual arguments at conferences now — not polite disagreement, real arguments, the kind that used to be reserved for questions people actually cared about the answers to. Three different research groups have proposed three different geological explanations, none of which is correct, because none of them have the one piece of context that would make the pattern make sense." She almost laughed, something dry and slightly disbelieving in it. "No one has said 'cultivation infrastructure' yet. Not once. It's remarkable, honestly, how far careful people will go to avoid the explanation that's actually sitting right in front of them, if the alternative explanations still feel more comfortable to imagine."

"Do you think someone will get there eventually? Without us telling them directly?"

"Eventually, probably. Someone persistent enough, or willing enough to consider an answer that sounds absurd on its face. But not soon, I think. People trust their training more than they trust their curiosity, most of the time. It takes a specific kind of person to actually chase an anomaly all the way down to an answer their whole education taught them to dismiss."

He thought, inevitably, of himself three years earlier — a convenience store clerk with no formal training at all, chasing a stubborn notation symbol for eleven minutes because he genuinely could not stand to leave a question unanswered. Perhaps that had been the advantage all along, he thought. Not intelligence. Not resources. Simply an unwillingness to let a strange thing stay unexplained.

"Are you glad you published it," he asked. "Fully, this time. No half-truths."

"Yes," she said, without hesitation. "Even with everything it's already cost, and everything it might still cost. I spent three years being careful in a way that felt, increasingly, like a kind of lying by omission to a field I actually respected. This is uncomfortable, and it's chaotic, and half of academic geology currently thinks I've stumbled onto something extraordinary for reasons they haven't figured out yet. But it's honest. I'd rather live inside honest chaos than comfortable, careful silence."

He nodded, recognizing in her words an echo of something Dr. Shen Yue had said weeks earlier, in a university office half-packed into boxes — the same hard, deliberate choice, arrived at through a different path but landing in the same place. Truth, chosen with open eyes, regardless of what it cost.

"The Strata owes you something for this," he said. "I don't know exactly what yet. But you took a risk most people wouldn't have, and it's part of why the review is going as well as it currently is."

"You can thank me," she said, standing, tucking the crumpled printout carefully into her bag like something worth keeping, "by making sure it was worth it. Win the review, Lin Mushi. Then all of this chaos gets to mean something instead of just being chaos."

"I'm working on it," he said, and meant it, watching her go with the particular quiet gratitude he'd learned, over years, to feel for the people who chose the harder honest thing alongside him without ever being asked to.
