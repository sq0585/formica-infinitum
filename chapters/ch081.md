## The Eternal Ant Legacy

### Arc II: The City Below

## Chapter Eighty-One — The Exchange

They met at a tea house near the Jinjiang River on a Tuesday afternoon, which was neutral ground in the way that neither of them had history with it.

The tea house had wooden tables that absorbed sound and a playlist that cycled through instrumental guqin without looping in any detectable pattern and windows that looked out onto the river embankment where people walked dogs and pushed strollers and moved through their Tuesday afternoons in the manner of people who had no idea what was happening in the building behind them or forty meters below it.

Lin Mushi arrived first, which was his preference in any meeting where information would be exchanged. He ordered tea. He opened his notebook to the page he'd prepared.

Chen Yufei arrived four minutes later and sat down across from him with the focused attention of someone who had been thinking about this conversation since she'd walked out of the Strata at 4 AM and had arrived with several things she intended to say.

"The five Echo Courts," he said, before she could begin.

She blinked. "That's what you want to start with."

"You know things I need. I know things you want. This is the exchange you said you wanted. I'd like to start with the organizational structure because I've been working without it."

She looked at him for a moment. Then she put both hands around her tea cup and began.

---

The Iron Meridian Court: oldest of the five, founded approximately eight hundred years ago, headquartered in Chengdu, specializing in what they called _internal pathway cultivation_ — the meridian system. Approximately three hundred active members globally. Known for technical precision and institutional conservatism.

The Northern Peak Court: headquartered in Lhasa, specializing in _high-altitude Qi absorption_ — what the Codex would classify as atmospheric layer cultivation, though no one in the Court knew to call it that. Approximately two hundred members. Known for political aggression and resource acquisition.

The Celestial Compass School: headquartered in Xi'an, specializing in astronomical Qi mapping — they tracked the Veritonal network's cosmological-scale patterns, which they interpreted as celestial influence. Their instruments were the most sophisticated of any Court's, their theory the most partially correct. Approximately one hundred fifty members.

The Verdant Stream Collective: headquartered in Yunnan, specializing in ecological Qi cultivation — drawn from living systems, forests, water cycles. The most fragmented organizationally, the most ethically developed, the least politically engaged. Approximately four hundred members widely dispersed.

The Void Mirror Sect: the smallest, oldest in terms of doctrine, headquartered in Ningbo. They practiced what they described as _stillness cultivation_ — which Lin Mushi suspected, from the Codex, was a garbled version of Compression-stage practice. Approximately eighty members. Known for being impenetrable and occasionally terrifying.

Lin Mushi wrote all of this without looking up. When she finished, he reviewed the page, added three cross-references to Codex notation clusters he'd already decoded, and looked up.

"Which Courts are currently coordinating?" he asked.

She considered. "The Northern Peak and Iron Meridian have an intelligence-sharing agreement that's been active for approximately forty years. The Celestial Compass School talks to everyone but is formally affiliated with none. The Verdant Stream considers coordination with the Courts a compromise of principle. The Void Mirror Sect acknowledges the existence of the other Courts approximately once per decade."

"Northern Peak sent the investigator. Your Court received the inquiry?"

"We were copied, not consulted." A pause. "The Northern Peak Court does not ask for opinions on acquisitions."

He noted the word _acquisitions_ and did not comment on it yet.

"The investigator," he said. "Elder Kuo Tianming. His authorization was for enhanced acquisition protocol. What does that mean specifically?"

Chen Yufei's tea cup went down with a small deliberate sound. "Where did you get the authorization language?"

"He requested backup and the authorization simultaneously. The backup request was picked up by my monitoring." He did not explain what monitoring meant in this context. "The authorization language was in the request. I read it."

She looked at him for a long moment with an expression that was recalibrating something.

"Enhanced acquisition," she said, "means they've classified you as a resource with strategic value. They're authorized to offer terms, to compel if terms are refused, and to contain if compulsion is refused." She said this flatly, the way you say something when the plainness of the saying is the courtesy. "The third step has a specific protocol. It involves preventing the subject from operating independently."

"Detention."

"Their word for it is _secure cultivation environment._"

He wrote: _secure cultivation environment = detention._ He underlined it.

"What would prevent them from proceeding to step three?"

"A prior claim by another Court." She looked at him steadily. "Or a demonstrated relationship with a Court member that predates the investigation."

He looked back at her.

"You've thought about this," he said.

"I've had three days."

"And your conclusion."

She turned her tea cup around in her hands — once, slowly, the gesture of someone who'd already made a decision and was now informing themselves of it. "My conclusion is that I would like to know what the notation says before Elder Kuo's backup arrives. And that the most efficient way to ensure I get to know what the notation says is to be the person standing between you and anyone who wants to take you somewhere I can't follow."

Lin Mushi looked at his notebook page, at the five Courts and their specializations and the cross-references to Codex notation he'd made in the margins.

"That is a pragmatic position," he said.

"I'm a pragmatic person."

"What are your terms?"

"Full access to whatever documentation you're willing to share from the library. Advance notice of anything you plan to release publicly. And—" she paused — "an honest accounting of the Formican framework's relationship to Iron Meridian cultivation. Not what you're willing to tell me. What's actually true."

He thought about this. He had been planning to give her the honest accounting regardless, because dishonesty here would compound into exactly the kind of misalignment that caused preventable problems later. The fact that she'd asked for it specifically was simply evidence that she already knew this too.

"Accepted," he said.

"Your terms," she said.

"Interpretations, not raw files, until I've assessed what's safe to share and with whom. Prior notice before any contact I make with the Courts. And—" he looked at her directly — "the same honesty in the other direction. What you know about the Courts' methods, resources, and intentions. Without editing for what you think I should know."

She held his gaze for a moment.

"Accepted," she said.

They drank their tea. The river outside moved at its usual pace, indifferent and continuous, full of whatever rivers were full of in December.

"The Northern Peak's backup," Lin Mushi said. "How long?"

"The authorization was filed six days ago. Standard transit and deployment: ten to fourteen days total. You have a week, probably less."

"Then we have things to do," he said.

He turned to the next blank page of his notebook.

He asked the first question of what would turn out to be a four-hour briefing, and Chen Yufei answered it, and the arc's real work began.
