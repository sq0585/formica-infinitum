# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 390 — The Community Meeting

The Strata's largest communal chamber had never held all of its residents at once. It had not been built to, originally — a gathering space sized for festivals and shared meals, not for the entire weight of a civilization's population standing shoulder to shoulder, waiting to hear whether their lives were about to end.

Lin Mushi stood at the front of it that evening, no podium, no formal arrangement, just himself and Fang Ruxue and Meen-4 present in body for the occasion, and roughly eleven hundred people filling a space that groaned faintly, at the edges, with a crowd it had never actually been tested to hold.

He did not open with reassurance.

"Six of you were contacted directly by an institution called the Containment Orthodoxy," he said, his voice carrying easily through the chamber's acoustics, engineered years ago for exactly this kind of clarity. "Offered relocation, safety, in exchange for testimony against me. I want to start by telling you that offer was real, that the danger behind it is real, and that I am not going to spend tonight convincing you it isn't."

The chamber was silent in the particular way a crowd goes silent when it realizes the person speaking is not going to soften anything.

He told them everything. The Surveyor's arrival. The formal review, the accounting, the presentation he'd made in their defense. The Containment Orthodoxy's counter-submission, its cold and technically correct argument that competence itself, regardless of intention, constituted an unacceptable risk. He told them about the sterilization threat in plain language, without euphemism, because he had decided, walking into the chamber, that people facing the possibility of their own ending deserved to hear it named honestly rather than wrapped in something gentler.

He told them, too, about the Surveyor's hesitation, the unprecedented four-day pause, the technical question about Void Lattice restoration that had felt, for the first time, like a door opening rather than closing. He told them about the vote the Collective had held on whether to even consider Earth's case seriously, and the substantial majority that had said yes.

"I am not going to promise you a specific result," he said, near the end, looking out over a crowd he could feel, faintly, through the Colony Link — a thousand overlapping currents of fear and hope and stubborn, unglamorous resolve. "I don't have that promise to give. What I can tell you is what I am doing about it, every day, with everything I have. And I can tell you that the choice to stay or go is genuinely yours. I built this place so people could choose to live here. I am not going to stop meaning that now, when the choice has gotten harder."

The questions began almost immediately, and they were not gentle.

*Why didn't we know how bad this could get, before we moved our families here?* — from a man near the back, his voice tight with something between fear and genuine anger. Lin Mushi answered honestly: because the review's full stakes had not been fully clear even to him, this early, and because he had, in hindsight, underestimated how much of the danger deserved to be shared before it arrived rather than after.

*If they win, does it happen fast, or do we know it's coming?* — from a woman near the front, holding a child who was clearly too young to understand the question but old enough to sense the fear in the room around her. He didn't have a full answer, and said so, and watched her nod slowly, absorbing an honest uncertainty instead of a false comfort.

*Is the woman who almost gave them what they wanted still here?* — a harder question, aimed obliquely at Lady Shen without naming her, and he answered that one carefully too, without excusing what had happened and without pretending the situation was simpler than it was.

For four hours, the questions kept coming, heated and scared and honest in exactly the way Fang Ruxue had warned him they might be, and he stayed and answered every one he could, and admitted plainly when he couldn't.

By the end, the anger in the room had not disappeared. But something else had entered alongside it — a kind of clear-eyed resolve he recognized, because he'd felt it himself in the days after Ren Wuji died, the particular steadiness that came not from certainty but from finally understanding exactly what you were facing, together, instead of alone.

He left the chamber that night more exhausted than he'd been in weeks, and more certain than he'd been in weeks that whatever the review decided, the Strata itself — the people, not the structure — had already decided something important about what kind of community it wanted to be, faced with the worst news anyone had ever given them all at once.
