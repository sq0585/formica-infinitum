# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 391 — What the Community Chooses

The vote was Fang Ruxue's idea, proposed the morning after the meeting, and Lin Mushi had hesitated over it longer than he'd expected to.

"It shouldn't be a vote," he said. "Not really. People don't need to justify wanting to stay somewhere, or wanting to leave. It's not a referendum on loyalty."

"It's not a referendum on loyalty," she agreed. "It's information. You told them the truth last night, all of it, uncomfortable parts included. Now let them tell you, formally and anonymously, what they've decided to do with that truth. Not so you can grade their answers. So you know what you're actually protecting, and who's still standing beside you with full knowledge of the risk."

He agreed, eventually, on the condition that it be framed exactly as she'd described — a private, anonymous choice, remain or relocate, no explanation required, no consequence attached to either answer beyond the practical logistics of arranging safe passage for anyone who chose to go.

The results came back three days later. One thousand one hundred and forty-seven residents chose to remain. Fifty-three chose to leave.

He read the numbers twice, feeling something complicated settle in his chest — not triumph, though the ratio was, by any honest measure, overwhelming. Something quieter. Fifty-three people, frightened enough by what he'd told them to choose safety over the life they'd already built here. He understood that choice completely. He would have made it himself, he suspected, in an earlier version of his life, before curiosity had slowly reshaped him into someone who couldn't quite walk away from an unfinished question even when walking away was the wiser option.

"I don't want them shamed," he told Fang Ruxue immediately, before any logistics had even been arranged. "Not by anyone. Not in whispers, not in how they're treated on their way out. They heard the same truth everyone else heard and made a different, entirely reasonable choice."

"I'll make sure of it," she said. "But I think there's something else you should do, beyond just protecting them from judgment."

"What."

"Talk to them. Individually, if you can manage it. Not to change their minds — I don't think you should try to change their minds. Just to understand what would have made them feel safer, if anything could have. You might learn something the rest of the Strata needs to hear too."

He spent the following week doing exactly that — one conversation at a time, no urgency in any of them, sitting with each of the fifty-three residents in whatever space felt comfortable to them, asking not *why are you leaving* but *what would have helped.*

Most of the answers surprised him with how ordinary they were. An elderly man who simply wanted clearer, plainer explanations of the review process, without the notation and formal language that made everything feel more alien and unreachable than it needed to. A young mother who wanted to know, specifically, what evacuation would actually look like if the worst outcome arrived — not reassurance that it wouldn't happen, but a concrete plan for what happened if it did, something she could hold onto instead of abstract dread. A couple who admitted, quietly, that they had simply never fully understood how much danger they'd been living beside for the past year, and needed time away from it to decide whether they'd choose differently with full information from the start.

He listened to all of it, and where he could, he acted. He drafted, with Fang Ruxue's help, a plain-language summary of the review process, stripped of formal notation, updated weekly and posted where every resident could read it without needing to ask. He built, with Wei Canglan, an actual evacuation protocol — routes, timing, a clear procedure rather than a vague promise — and made it available to everyone, not just the fifty-three who had asked.

"You turned their leaving into something that helps everyone who stayed," Chen Yufei observed, once the changes were in place.

"That wasn't the goal," he said. "The goal was just to actually hear them, instead of treating their fear as a problem to manage."

"I know," she said. "That's why it worked."

He thought of the fifty-three, in the days that followed, each time he walked past the space where they'd once lived, now quietly emptying as arrangements were finalized. He didn't resent them. He found, if anything, that he respected them — people who had looked honestly at an impossible situation and made the choice that was right for them, without pretending otherwise for anyone's comfort, including his own.
