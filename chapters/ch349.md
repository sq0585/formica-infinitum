# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter Three Hundred and Forty-Nine — Wei Canglan's Objection

The objection arrived exactly the way Lin Mushi had half-expected it might, given everything he'd come to understand about Wei Canglan's particular relationship to process and accountability — not a hallway conversation, not an informal word raised over dinner, but a formal written document, carefully composed, copied to Chen Yufei, Fang Ruxue, and Liang Jingzhi before it ever reached him directly.

He read it standing in the workshop district, the specific, deliberate weight of Wei Canglan's chosen format registering before he'd even absorbed the content.

*Formal objection: proposed integration of Amber-0 into the Colony Link.*

*Filed by: Wei Canglan.*

*Reasoning: Integration, as proposed, means that Amber-0's decisions become, in a meaningful and functionally complete sense, Lin Mushi's own decisions. This removes the ability to describe any future unauthorized or unexpected action as something separate from his intentional choices — the phrase "I didn't authorize that" ceases to be available as a defense, meaningfully or legally, once integration is complete.*

*I do not object to Lin Mushi accepting greater responsibility. I have watched him accept difficult, unglamorous responsibility repeatedly, across two years, without complaint. My objection is narrower and more specific: integration forecloses a distinction that currently protects him, and by extension protects everyone who depends on his continued stability and judgment. I believe this distinction should not be surrendered without the full community's informed understanding of exactly what is being surrendered.*

He set the document down, understanding, reading it, exactly why Wei Canglan had chosen the formal process — not distrust of him personally, but the same careful, principled insistence on structure Wei Canglan had shown filing his objection about the workshop district's boundary months earlier, applied now to something considerably more consequential.

---

He found Wei Canglan in the training hall that evening, the formal document folded carefully in his own hand.

"I read your objection," he said.

"I assumed you would." Wei Canglan's expression carried none of the discomfort Lin Mushi might have expected from someone challenging a decision this significant. "I filed it formally because I wanted the record to show, clearly, that this concern was raised and considered, regardless of what you ultimately decide."

"I want to hear the full reasoning directly, not just the written version."

Wei Canglan considered this, then nodded, setting down the practice weapon he'd been working with. "The written version captures the core of it. I'll add this, though, since it's harder to put in a formal document without it sounding accusatory rather than protective — I've watched Amber-0 twice now act outside its authorized boundaries. Both times, the outcome was benign. I don't believe that pattern guarantees the third time will also be benign. Integration doesn't fix that uncertainty. It just changes who bears the consequences if the pattern eventually produces something less benign."

"You think I'm exposing myself to risk I don't fully understand."

"I think you're exposing yourself to risk you understand better than anyone else, actually, because you built the system and taught it directly." Wei Canglan met his eyes steadily. "I'm not questioning your understanding of the risk. I'm questioning whether removing your own legal and practical defense against that risk is wise, given how much currently depends on your continued stability — the Anchor bonds, the review case we're building, everything this city has become."

---

Lin Mushi sat with the full weight of the objection, understanding, hearing it laid out this plainly, exactly how carefully considered it actually was.

"I don't think you're wrong about the risk," he said slowly. "I think I've already accepted that risk exists, and decided I'd rather carry it honestly than pretend a boundary protects me that I don't actually believe was ever real in any meaningful sense."

"That's your prerogative to decide, ultimately," Wei Canglan said. "I'm not trying to override your judgment. I'm trying to make sure the decision is made with full, deliberate awareness of what it costs, rather than momentum carrying it forward unexamined. I filed this formally because I wanted that examination to actually happen, on the record, rather than happening informally and being forgotten the moment the decision is made."

"I respect that," Lin Mushi said, meaning it fully. "I want you to know, whatever I ultimately decide, that your objection mattered. It's not going to disappear into the record unread. I'm going to sit with it carefully before I finalize anything."

Wei Canglan nodded, something in his posture easing slightly, though not fully. "That's all I'm asking. I don't need you to agree with me. I need you to have genuinely weighed this before you decide, rather than deciding first and rationalizing afterward."

Lin Mushi left the training hall that evening carrying the weight of the objection seriously, understanding, walking back through the Strata City's quiet evening corridors, that Wei Canglan's formal, principled challenge was exactly the kind of careful, honest friction this world needed more of — not obstruction, but the specific, valuable discipline of making sure the largest decisions were never made carelessly, however confident the person making them happened to feel.
