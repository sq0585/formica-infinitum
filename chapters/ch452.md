# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 452 — The Question He Can't Avoid

He spent three days turning the question over before he let himself talk about it with anyone, which was, he recognized even as he was doing it, its own small evidence of exactly how much the question actually cost him.

The mechanism itself wasn't in doubt. He'd watched Fang Ruxue undergo the extension protocol two arcs ago, understood by now, thoroughly, what it involved and what it didn't — not immortality, not transformation into something unrecognizable, simply a meaningful, well-understood extension of healthy, capable years, offered to a body that had earned the extension through nothing more than the specific misfortune of aging inside a world that now had the capability to offer alternatives.

His mother qualified for it as easily as Fang Ruxue had. Easier, in some ways — her condition was milder, her body otherwise sound, and Meen-4's protocol, from everything he understood of its mechanics, worked most reliably on exactly this kind of case: ordinary decline, caught early, addressed before it became anything more urgent.

*Observation 438,* he wrote, sitting alone in the pocket dimension laboratory with the notebook open and the pen, for once, moving slower than his thoughts. *I have the capability to offer her this. I do not currently have any legitimate way to offer it honestly.*

The problem wasn't the offer itself. The problem was everything the offer required her to already understand before she could meaningfully consent to it.

He could not explain the extension protocol without explaining Formican biology. He could not explain Formican biology without explaining the Codex, the Strata, the Colony Link, five years of a life she had watched from the outside without ever once being given the full shape of what she was watching. He could not ask her to accept something this permanent, this fundamental, from a version of her son she did not yet fully know — not the public version the disclosure protocol had introduced to governments and journalists in careful, managed doses, but the actual, complete truth he had spent five years deciding, quietly, that she was better off without.

*Rule,* he wrote, and pressed the pen hard enough into the page that he noticed the specific physical effort of it. *Informed consent requires actual information. I cannot give her a meaningful choice about the extension without first giving her a meaningful understanding of what she would actually be consenting to become part of. There is no version of this where I can protect her from the truth and also ask her to trust me with something this large.*

He found Chen Yufei later that evening, in the small kitchen above the noodle shop, and told her the shape of the problem without yet deciding what to do about it.

"You've been protecting her for five years," Chen Yufei said, listening carefully, the way she always listened to things that actually mattered. "What's different now?"

"Before, protecting her cost her nothing she could measure. She got to keep an ordinary life, an ordinary understanding of her son, and I got to carry the weight of the difference alone. That felt, for a long time, like the kinder trade." He turned his tea slowly in his hands, not yet drinking it. "Now protecting her costs her specifically — costs her the choice of more time, more years, simply because I've decided the truth is too large a thing to hand her. That's not protection anymore. That's just fear, wearing protection's clothes."

Chen Yufei was quiet for a moment, considering. "Do you think she can handle it? The whole truth, I mean. Not the managed version the world's been getting."

"I think," he said slowly, working the answer out as he spoke it, "that I've spent five years underestimating exactly how much this specific woman can handle, because handling it would have meant I had to actually say the words out loud, and some part of me has been afraid of what happens after I do."

"Afraid of what, specifically?"

He sat with the question longer than he expected to need, and found, when the answer finally arrived, that it was smaller and truer than anything he'd have guessed a week ago.

"Afraid she won't recognize me," he said quietly. "Afraid that once she knows everything, the version of her son who calls every Sunday and asks about her garden will feel, to her, like it was always partly a lie."

Chen Yufei reached across the small table and took his hand, the same steady warmth she'd offered him through every difficult threshold of the past five years.

"I don't think that's how mothers work," she said. "But I also don't think I get to answer that question for you. I think there's only one person who actually can."

He nodded, and sat with the weight of it a while longer, the tea gone cold between them, the question no closer to answered than it had been three days ago — only clearer now, in its actual shape, for having finally said it out loud to someone.

Before he asked anything of his mother, though, he understood there was a smaller, more practical question he owed himself an honest answer to first: whether what he was even considering offering her was as sound, as carefully understood, as he currently believed it to be. That was a conversation for Meen-4, not for tonight, and not, he suspected, one he could keep putting off much longer either.
