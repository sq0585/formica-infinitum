# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter Two Hundred and Fourteen — What Maintenance Requires

The maintenance protocol, once he found it properly catalogued in the Archive's infrastructure sections, was more demanding than he'd hoped and less mysterious than he'd feared.

A qualified practitioner needed to reach Compression stage — stable, not necessarily advanced, but stable enough to hold a consistent interface to the Veritonal substrate for the duration of a resonance adjustment — and perform a sequence of corrections at each node, roughly every three to four weeks, to keep accumulated pressure distributed rather than pooling.

He read through the full protocol twice, cross-referencing it against what he already understood of Compression mechanics, and found nothing in the technique itself that struck him as inaccessible. The difficulty wasn't the work. The difficulty was the arithmetic.

Western Chengdu's node cluster alone contained forty-one nodes requiring rotation.

He pulled up the Courts' historical staffing records — fragments of it, anyway, gathered through Chen Yufei's network and a few documents Fang Ruxue had shared from her own institutional access — and found the number that made the scale of the problem fully concrete.

Two hundred qualified practitioners, across all five Courts combined, had handled the city's full Under-Web maintenance burden before the fracture. Forty-one nodes in western Chengdu represented a fraction of the total network, and even that fraction had apparently required a meaningful slice of those two hundred working in careful rotation.

He counted his own available people honestly, refusing to round generously in either direction.

Himself. Chen Yufei, stable at Compression but still recovering full capacity. Wei Canglan, newly trained, technically qualified but inexperienced enough that Lin Mushi wasn't certain how reliable his work would be under real conditions yet. Two of the independent cultivators from the original assembly. That was five, and he was being generous calling Wei Canglan ready.

Fewer than ten, against a workload built for two hundred.

He sat with the gap for a long time before he wrote anything down, turning the numbers over from different angles, looking for an assumption he could safely loosen without compromising the actual safety of the work.

> *Forty-one nodes, western cluster alone. Each requiring resonance correction every 3–4 weeks. Two hundred Court practitioners used to cover the full city's network. I have fewer than five reliable people and a problem that doesn't care how reasonable my excuses are.*
>
> *This is what the Courts were actually for. Not just hierarchy, not just authority — real, unglamorous, necessary labor that kept something dangerous from becoming something catastrophic. I disrupted the institution without having anything ready to replace this specific function, and now the gap is mine whether I planned for it or not.*
>
> *I can't cover two hundred practitioners' worth of work with five people. I need to either reduce the scope of what needs covering, or find more hands, or both.*

He closed the notebook and sat with the discomfort of the conclusion for a moment longer than he usually allowed himself.

This wasn't a problem he could solve through better technique or more careful study. It was a problem of scale, and scale required people — people willing to learn a discipline that offered no glory, no advancement, nothing but the quiet, recurring obligation of keeping something invisible from going wrong.

He thought of Fang Ruxue's three Celestial Compass students. Of the Assembly members who'd joined the Strata community over the past months, most of them still finding their footing. Of Lady Shen, two days into a conversation about building something that lasted, who had thirty years of exactly the kind of institutional thinking this problem actually needed.

It would mean asking more of people who had already given up a great deal to be here.

He picked up his phone and started composing the message anyway, because the alternative — pretending the gap would close itself, or that he could cover it alone through sheer effort — wasn't a real alternative at all. It was just a slower version of the failure he was trying to prevent.
