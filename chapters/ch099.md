# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter Ninety-Nine — Formican Library — Outer Hall

He spent three days reading what he could of the predecessor's node repair documentation.

Not three continuous days — he still had geological consulting contracts, a second meeting with Elder Zhao, and daily practice that he'd learned not to skip even under time pressure. But the documentation occupied the center of his attention in the way that the most important available material always did: returning to it between other things, processing it while eating, reorganizing its implications in his notebooks at midnight.

What the predecessor had worked out:

Node repair was not a matter of energy input. He had already intuited this from the directed release experiment on the plant — the Qi released functioned as a catalyst for the node's own recovery processes rather than simply supplementing its energy. The predecessor's ninety years of practical documentation confirmed this and refined it considerably.

The correct mechanism: a very specific frequency of directed release, matched to the node's natural Qi signature, applied at the node's primary inflow point rather than at its center. The effect was to re-establish the node's own organizational coherence — its ability to maintain structure and flow — rather than to replenish its content. A depleted node that had lost coherence couldn't hold energy anyway; the energy would just flow through and disperse. Restoring coherence came first. Energy recovery followed on its own.

He tested this on the depleted tributary that ran past his apartment building — not the dead node, which was too far gone for his current capability level, but a secondary tributary that the node's death had left slightly decoherent, running rough in a way his perception now had enough resolution to identify.

He applied the predecessor's protocol.

The tributary smoothed.

Not immediately — the predecessor's documentation specified a forty-eight to seventy-two hour response time for significant damage. But at twenty-four hours the tributary's signature had the quality of beginning to self-organize again, the way a disturbed beehive settled back into working pattern over time.

He wrote:

> *Node/tributary repair protocol: validated. Predecessor's documentation accurate. Mechanism: restore coherence first (frequency-matched directed release at primary inflow); energy recovery follows organically. Response time: 24–72 hours depending on damage severity.*
>
> *The dead node under the 7-Eleven: too far gone for this protocol at my current level. The coherence loss is too complete. The predecessor's documentation includes a note that fully dead nodes require Ratio 3+ to restore — the coherence work at that level of damage requires more Compression depth than the standard repair protocol. I cannot do this yet.*
>
> *What I can do: prevent further damage to secondary tributaries. Begin repair of partially depleted nodes elsewhere in the district.*
>
> *This is the correct order: stop causing harm, then repair what can be repaired, then address the full damage.*

He looked at this last line.

He had been thinking about it since the node's death. The guilt he'd noted, the responsibility he'd named. He had not been able to act on it until now because he hadn't had the mechanism. He had the mechanism now.

He spent the next three days walking the district's Under-Web with the Chitin Circuit in his bag and the predecessor's protocol in his notebook, finding partially depleted tributaries and beginning the repair process on each one.

It was unglamorous work.

It was also exactly the right work.

---

The outer hall of the Amber Lattice Library looked different on visit sixteen than it had on visit eleven.

He wasn't sure what had changed in him, or whether anything had changed in the hall — the preserved containers on the shelves, the sealed dimensional locks, the careful darkness interrupted only by the faint glow of the inscribed material. What he was aware of was that he had spent three days reading what someone else had built in two hundred years, and standing in the outer hall now had a new quality.

He was not the first person to stand here.

He was the first person to stand here in two hundred years, by the Keeper's record.

He stood in the outer hall and looked at the containers on the shelves with their sealed locks and the inscription patterns he could now read at seventy-two percent fluency — higher than a week ago, the Strata visits accelerating his comprehension as immersion always did — and felt something he didn't immediately try to name.

He tried to name it anyway, later, in the notebook:

> *Standing in the outer hall on visit 16. What was different: the sense that the hall knew something. Not the hall itself — the built context, the decisions that had been made in designing this archive and maintaining it for a period of time I can't accurately calculate. The decision to preserve things. The decision to arrange them. The decision to make them accessible to someone who wasn't born yet when the decisions were made.*
>
> *The hall is a kind of letter. Not addressed to anyone specific. Addressed to whoever came through the door.*
>
> *I came through the door.*

He opened his notebook to the predecessor's register translation section and kept working.

The locks on the containers required Ratio 1.8 and full communication register fluency. He had one but not the other. He needed eighty percent register fluency before the locks would yield fully. He was at seventy-two. A few more weeks.

Then he'd be able to read what the previous Carrier had left specifically for the next one.

He looked forward to this with the specific quality of anticipation he reserved for things that were going to be genuinely important.

He kept working.
