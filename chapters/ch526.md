# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 526 — Liang Jingzhi — Changed

He visited her properly a few days later — not through the Colony Link, not through the combined structure they'd built together at the dissolution zone's edge, but in the ordinary way, walking into the Chengdu office in the early afternoon the way he might once, years ago, have walked into any colleague's workspace to see how they were actually doing.

She was exactly where he expected her to be, and exactly as she had described herself so many times he had, he realized with some private embarrassment, half stopped picturing it accurately: a desk cluttered with survey printouts she still preferred in physical form despite every digital alternative available to her, a coffee cup that had gone cold sometime before he arrived, a window that looked out over an ordinary Chengdu street doing ordinary Chengdu things.

And, simultaneously, permanently, unmistakably, present at stellar scale inside a healing wound she had given six months of herself to.

"You're staring," she said, without looking up from the printout in front of her.

"I'm looking properly," he said. "There's a difference. I've been told."

She smiled at that, faintly, and set the printout down.

"How are you," he asked, and meant it the specific, careful way he had learned to mean the question over the past several months — not a formality, not a prelude to the actual conversation, but the actual conversation itself.

"Fine," she said.

He waited, the way he had learned to wait with her, understanding that her answers arrived complete rather than needing to be drawn out, and that the waiting itself was a kind of respect rather than a lack of trust.

"I mean it," she said, after a moment, meeting his gaze directly. "I know 'fine' sounds like the answer someone gives when they don't want to discuss something further. It isn't that, with me. It's the accurate answer. I am, by every measure that matters to me, fine. I still drink too much coffee. I still argue with reviewers about sediment dating methodology in papers that will take eighteen months to publish. I am, in every ordinary sense a person can be measured, exactly the geologist I was six years ago, doing exactly the work I always wanted to do, and I happen to also be permanently woven into the healing structure of a forty-million-year-old wound. Both are true. Neither one makes the other less accurate."

"You still publish," he said, glancing at the printout.

"Of course I still publish. What did you imagine — that I'd stopped having a career the moment I became something a novel might call extraordinary?" There was real amusement in it, the specific dry humor he had come to rely on more than he usually let himself admit. "I have a paper under review right now about anomalous strata density in the western Sichuan basin. It is, I promise you, considerably less interesting than anything that happened this week, and I intend to keep writing papers like it for as long as I have data worth publishing."

"Does anyone know? At the journal. About the rest of it."

"No," she said. "And I'd like to keep it that way, for now. I'm a geologist there. I'd rather stay one, without the rest of it becoming the only thing anyone wants to ask me about."

He nodded, understanding completely.

"I worried about you," he admitted. "For longer than I probably should have, given everything you told me."

"I know you did," she said, gently. "I'd have worried too, in your position. I only ask that you actually believe me now, the way you said you would."

"I do."

She studied him a moment, something warm and steady in her expression, and picked her coffee back up despite its having long since gone cold.

"Good," she said. "Now go home. You look tired, and I have a paper to finish arguing about."

He laughed, and left her to it, the ordinary afternoon light following him out the door exactly the way it always had.

He walked, rather than folding the distance away the way he easily could have, choosing the ordinary length of an ordinary Chengdu street the way he still, deliberately, chose ordinary things whenever the choice was genuinely his to make. He thought, walking, about the specific shape of what he'd just witnessed — not a woman transformed by an extraordinary sacrifice into something unrecognizable, but a geologist, fully intact, who happened also to be something the rest of the world would have found difficult to categorize, had it known.

He understood, walking, that this was the answer to a question he had been quietly, privately afraid to ask her directly for months: whether the permanence of what she'd given the dissolution zone had cost her the ordinary shape of her own life in exchange for the extraordinary one. It hadn't. She still argued with reviewers. She still drank coffee that went cold while she worked. She had simply added an entire second, enormous dimension to an existence that had never, in six years of knowing her, been anything less than fully, stubbornly her own.

He thought, too, of what she'd said about the journal — that she'd rather stay a geologist there than become, to her colleagues, only the extraordinary thing she also was. He understood the instinct completely, and found himself, walking, grateful that she'd trusted him enough to say it plainly rather than let him assume otherwise.

By the time he reached the Strata's outer gate, the ordinary afternoon had settled fully back around him, and he found he was, for once, entirely content to let it.
