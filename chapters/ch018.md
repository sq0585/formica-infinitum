# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter Eighteen — His Father's Number

He had left his charger at his mother's.

He realized this on the train back to Chengdu, which was too late to do anything about it, and spent a moment performing the standard mental calculation: phone at 64%, the train was an hour and twenty minutes, the walk home another fifteen, he'd be fine. He put the phone in his pocket and watched the dark outside the window.

At the station he stopped at a convenience store for water — not his store, a different chain, the lighting slightly harsher and the layout mirror-imaged from the one he worked in — and stood in line behind a couple arguing pleasantly about what snacks to buy. Ordinary. He liked ordinary. He bought his water, walked home through streets that smelled of autumn and distant hotpot, and went upstairs.

It was past nine PM. He was not scheduled to work until ten. He had forty-five minutes.

He sat at his desk with his phone to find his mother had already messaged him about the charger, which he replied to — *I'll get it next time, don't worry* — and in the process of putting the phone down he saw his contacts list and in the contacts list he saw his father's name.

*Lin Guozhi — Kunming.*

He had not added the note *— Kunming* himself. He did not remember when it had appeared. At some point he had apparently needed to specify, to his own contact list, which city his father lived in, as if the information required disambiguation from some other version of him that might exist elsewhere.

He put the phone face-down on the desk and made tea.

---

He had not spoken to his father in four months.

This was not an argument. There had not been an argument — not a recent one. His father had left when Lin Mushi was fourteen, not dramatically, not for someone else, just in the way that some people discover the shape of a life is wrong for them and leave to find the right shape, which was a thing that happened and a thing that had consequences and a thing that Lin Mushi had spent several years being angry about and had eventually arrived at something closer to a complicated neutrality. His father called on birthdays. He called on his father's birthday. Occasionally there were calls in between, shorter, for no particular reason, which tended toward updates about one life relayed to another life at a slight conversational distance.

He drank his tea and did not call.

What he did do was pick up the phone and navigate to his mother's contact and then stay there, looking at it, not opening it.

His mother had a reminder set to call his father on his birthday.

He had seen it, the last time he'd borrowed her phone in the kitchen to look up something she'd asked about. A recurring calendar event, once a year: *Call Guozhi – birthday.*

She had never mentioned this. She had been divorced from his father for eleven years and she still had a reminder to call him on his birthday, and she had never mentioned it once.

He sat with this.

He did not know what to do with it. It was not his information to interpret. It was his mother's calendar and his mother's private decision and the fact that he had seen it was a small intrusion, a piece of information that arrived unbidden. He had no idea what the calls were like. He had no idea if she called from obligation or from lingering feeling or from the simple practical position that they had been married for twenty years and had a son together and civility was worth maintaining.

He picked up the pen and opened the notebook. Not the observation notebook — the general one. He wrote:

> *Saw: Māma has a recurring reminder to call Bàba on his birthday. He lives in Kunming. I have not spoken to him in four months.*
>
> *No analysis. Just noting it.*

He put the pen down.

He had thirty minutes until his shift. He ate two oranges over the sink because they were there and he was hungry and because eating an orange over the sink was one of those things that was exactly as complicated as it was, no more.

---

He did think about it, on the walk to work.

Not about his father specifically. About what it meant that his mother maintained a quiet, undisclosed connection to someone who had left — that she had filed it under *annual obligation* on her calendar and never brought it up to her son, who was the product of the relationship in question, and who might have feelings about it.

She was protecting him, probably. Or protecting herself. Or both, which was usually how those things worked.

He thought: I have been perceiving sub-verbal information for three weeks now. I perceive it in her voice. I perceived it in the apartment today. She has a quality of warmth in her kitchen that I can read now as something more than metaphor.

And I still don't know what she is holding quietly because she has decided not to say it.

The Codex was teaching him to perceive the world at a different resolution. But the world contained people, and people made choices about what to share, and no perceptual upgrade changed that. Some things were only visible if someone chose to show you.

He arrived at the store at 9:57, tied on his apron, and nodded to Xiao Dong who was counting out the till and looked relieved.

"Quiet night," Xiao Dong said, which was a hope rather than a forecast.

"They usually are," Lin Mushi said, which was also a hope.

The first customer came in at 10:14. The filaments ran steady and north-northwest beneath the floor. His wrist, which no longer hurt, moved without restriction as he restocked the front shelf.

He thought: I should call my father at some point.

He thought: not tonight.

He wrote neither of these thoughts down. Some things were observations and some things were just life, and he was still learning which was which.
