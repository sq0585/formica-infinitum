# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter Fifty — The Last Night Shift

His last shift was on a Friday.

He had told no one at the store it was his last shift, because telling people it was your last shift created a quality of occasion that he preferred not to have. Occasions changed the texture of ordinary things and the texture of ordinary things was specifically what he wanted preserved. He wanted the last shift to be a shift, not a last shift.

He tied on his apron at 10 PM. He counted the opening till. He straightened the snack display that Xiao Dong had left in a state of optimistic disorder. He served the 10:15 customer — a construction worker buying cigarettes — and the 10:47 customer — a university student buying two energy drinks and a protein bar with the focused manner of someone who had already decided sleep was for later — and made a note in the restocking log that the mandarin orange drinks were running low.

The dead stretch between two and four passed in its usual way.

---

Auntie Zhang came at 4:07.

She bought her warm soy milk from the machine by the door, folded the carton along its original crease lines, and stood at the counter with the quality of someone who had plenty to say and was deciding whether tonight was the night to say it.

"You're leaving," she said.

He looked at her. "Who told you?"

"Xiao Dong's friend's cousin works at the noodle shop on the corner." She waved this explanation away as the irrelevant logistics it was. "You're leaving. That's what I heard."

"Three weeks' notice was up today," he said.

She was quiet for a moment, looking at him with the particular assessment of someone who had been observing him for eight months at 4 AM intervals. "You're going to do something," she said. Not a question.

"Yes."

"Is it good?"

He considered this. "I think so. I don't know how to be sure yet."

She nodded, with the recognition of someone who had received this kind of answer from people she trusted. "My husband used to say that. When he decided to leave his factory job and start the business. 'I think it's good. I don't know how to be sure yet.'" She paused. "It was good. He couldn't have known that."

"No," Lin Mushi said.

She drank her soy milk. They were quiet together for a few minutes in the way they had been quiet together many times.

"You've been a very reliable young man," she said finally. "At four in the morning, reliable is rare."

He felt something that he didn't try to name. "Thank you for the stories," he said. "About your father's shop."

She looked at him, surprised, as if she hadn't expected him to have held onto that. "That old thing," she said. But she was pleased. He could feel it plainly.

She finished her soy milk, folded the carton, placed it in the recycling, and walked out into the early Chengdu morning.

---

Xiao Dong arrived at 5:58. They exchanged the usual seven words about the till. Lin Mushi untied his apron.

"You're really going," Xiao Dong said.

"Yes."

"To do research."

"Yes."

Xiao Dong nodded. He had the look of someone who had decided not to understand what he didn't understand and found this position comfortable. "Okay." He extended his hand.

Lin Mushi shook it.

"The floor really did have good bones," Xiao Dong said. "The contractor said whatever was under there, the foundation was solid."

"Yes," Lin Mushi said. "Very solid."

He collected his bag. He walked out the front door of the store for the last time as an employee.

---

Outside, the Chengdu morning was doing the thing it always did at 6 AM in late November: deciding whether to be gray or darker gray and settling, typically, on a nuanced position between the two. The delivery trucks were starting. The breakfast stalls were unfurling. The city was assembling itself back into the day.

Lin Mushi walked home with his hands in his pockets.

Under the floor of the store behind him, the filament network ran at perhaps twenty percent capacity, dimming toward its end. The investigator was in the city. The threshold waited in the northwest corner wall. Below it, forty meters down, a vaulted space held the hexagonal geometry of a civilization that had named itself after ants.

He had three months of savings and twelve open channels and a notebook full of everything he knew, which was not yet enough and was more than anyone else had.

He walked home through the city's gray morning, feeling the bedrock resonate faintly beneath his feet, the geology of Chengdu singing its quiet note to the network that ran through it.

He was not a convenience store clerk anymore.

He did not know yet what he was instead.

He turned the corner toward his street.

That was fine. He had time to find out.
