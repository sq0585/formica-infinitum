# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 472 — What This Changes — Practically

He surfaced late that evening, the relay's thin signal strengthening steadily as the descent reversed itself, and found Chen Yufei waiting at the terrestrial anchor point exactly where he'd left her, her relief at seeing him unhurried but unmistakable.

"You look like you've been somewhere very far away," she said, studying his face carefully.

"I have," he admitted. "Not in distance. In something else."

He gathered the others that night, all five of them, in the pocket dimension laboratory's warm, familiar light, and worked through the account slowly, giving them what he'd found in the same careful, complete way he'd given them everything else across the past months. He did not read it aloud a second time — some things, he understood, only needed to be spoken once, into the specific silence that had earned them. But he gave them the substance, plainly, and let them sit with its weight the way he had.

It was Meen-4 who spoke first, once he'd finished, her expression carrying something he'd rarely seen from her in five years of careful, measured precision.

"Do you understand what this actually gives you?" she asked. "Beyond the grief of it, beyond the letter itself — do you understand the practical shape of what Veth-Karun just handed you?"

He considered the question, turning the account over again in his mind, and found the answer arriving slowly, piece by piece, the way his best insights always eventually arrived.

"The mechanism of the dissolution," he said. "Veth-Karun held everything alone — accumulated knowledge, without anyone else's presence to keep the located self steady while the vastness arrived. That's what actually failed. Not the technique itself. The absence of anyone holding the observer in place while the observing expanded past any single scale a person should reasonably survive."

"Correct," Meen-4 said. "And the other half of it?"

He sat with that, and felt something settle into place with the particular, quiet weight of an answer that had been sitting directly in front of him for years, disguised as something already understood.

"The Anchor Protocol," he said slowly.

"Say it fully," Meen-4 pressed, gently. "I want you to hear yourself say it."

"The Anchor Protocol was never just an identity-maintenance tool," he said, and found, saying it aloud, that the sentence carried a weight he hadn't fully anticipated. "I built it years ago as a practical safeguard — a way to stay coherent while Colony Link, Planetary Architect, everything since, kept pulling my perception wider than any single person should comfortably hold. I understood it as scaffolding. Useful. Necessary. I never understood it as the actual answer to the exact failure that killed Veth-Karun."

"You built the solution to a problem you did not yet know existed," Meen-4 said quietly, "years before you needed it, because some part of you understood, long before the rest of you caught up to the understanding, that no amount of accumulated knowledge alone was ever going to be sufficient. You built five people into the architecture of your own survival before survival required it. That is not luck, Lin Mushi. I want to be clear about that. That is the specific, cumulative result of choosing connection over isolation, consistently, for five years, long before anyone told you it would eventually save your life."

He looked around the room at the five faces watching him — Chen Yufei, Liang Jingzhi, Dr. Shen Yue, Wei Canglan, Meen-4 — and felt the full practical weight of Veth-Karun's final lesson settle into something he could finally, concretely, act on.

"Formless Scale doesn't have to end the way it ended for them," he said slowly. "Not if the Anchor Protocol holds the way it's held every time before this. Not if I don't attempt it alone."

"You were never going to attempt it alone," Chen Yufei said, quiet and certain. "That decision was made a long time before tonight."

He nodded, understanding, with a clarity that settled rather than overwhelmed, that he now carried something considerably more valuable than mere theoretical comprehension.

He carried, at last, the actual mechanism of survival — built, unknowingly, years in advance, by a version of himself who had simply, instinctively, refused to do the one thing that had cost Veth-Karun everything.
