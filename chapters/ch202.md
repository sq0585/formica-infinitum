# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter Two Hundred and Two — Dimensional Weaving, First Attempt

The Codex called it Weaving. Document 7 called it, more precisely, *external compression of bounded space without anchor to a biological interface* — which was accurate and also the kind of phrase that explained nothing to a body trying to learn it for the first time.

Lin Mushi had spent four days failing at it.

The principle, on paper, was simple enough. Everything he'd done with Compression until now had reshaped his own interface to the Veritonal substrate — pulling his body's relationship to scale inward, like folding a map smaller without changing the territory it described. Weaving asked for something stranger: take that same folding capability and point it outward, at space that wasn't him, and hold the fold there instead of in himself.

The Strata's outer hall gave him room to try. Meen-4 had not objected to the experiments, though she'd relocated her own work to the far end on the second day, which he understood as either courtesy or a sensible precaution.

He picked a target the size of a shoebox. Small. Contained. A volume he could watch fail completely without anything important being inside it when it did.

The first attempt lasted four seconds before the geometry simply let go, the compressed space snapping back to its ordinary dimensions with a small displacement of air that ruffled the pages of his open notebook.

The second attempt didn't hold at all.

By the eighth attempt, he understood the failure pattern well enough to name it: he was trying to maintain the fold the way he maintained his own internal Compression, with continuous active attention, and external space didn't accept attention the way a body did. A body was already his. Space wasn't. The fold needed something closer to architecture than effort — a structure that held itself once built, instead of a state he had to keep choosing.

He rebuilt the notation from Document 7 with this distinction in mind, working through the geometry on paper before touching the substrate again, the way the Codex had always insisted he do for anything new.

Attempt fourteen held for nine seconds and then sheared apart unevenly, one corner collapsing before the rest, which told him the architecture wasn't symmetrical enough yet.

He adjusted. He breathed. He tried again.

---

The fifteenth attempt held.

He felt it the moment the fold stabilized — not a sensation in his body, exactly, but something adjacent to it, a small persistent pressure at the edge of his attention that corresponded to nothing his proprioception had a name for. He was thinking with space instead of about it. The shoebox-sized fold sat in the air in front of him, a slightly wrong-angled patch of darker air, holding.

He counted.

*One. Two. Three.*

He did not move. He barely breathed, half-convinced that breathing wrong would unravel whatever fragile architecture he'd built.

*Nine. Ten. Eleven.*

It collapsed on the twelfth second, gently this time, no displaced air, just a fold settling back into the rest of the room the way a held breath finally released.

He sat down where he was standing.

He picked up his notebook with hands that were, he noted with some clinical interest, not entirely steady.

> *Eleven seconds. Symmetrical collapse — better than fourteen, much better than the first attempt. The structure held its shape until it didn't, rather than failing from one point outward. That's the difference between architecture and effort. I built something instead of just holding something.*
>
> *Next: hold longer. Then: hold something inside it. Then: find out what 'inside' actually means for a fold this small — does it have its own time rate, the way Compression does? I won't know until I can hold it long enough to measure.*

He closed the notebook.

Eleven seconds wasn't much. He understood, with the particular clarity that came after four days of failure, that it was also not nothing — that somewhere in those eleven seconds, he had crossed from theory into a capability the Codex's authors had spent generations refining into something that could build cities.

He had built a shoebox.

It was, he thought, exactly the right place to start.
