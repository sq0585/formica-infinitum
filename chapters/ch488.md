# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 488 — The Strata Grows

The dissolution zone repair continued its slow, self-sustaining progress somewhere beyond ordinary perception, and the Strata City, entirely unconcerned with cosmic entropy, continued doing exactly what a growing community always did — accumulating the small, specific texture of ordinary life, one unglamorous detail at a time.

Fang Ruxue brought him the week's administrative summary the way she always did, and he found himself, reading through it, genuinely grateful for its complete disinterest in anything happening thousands of light-years away.

Twelve new residents had settled into the residential wing since he'd last properly reviewed the numbers — three researchers who'd relocated permanently after their work on the dissolution zone project deepened into something they wanted to pursue full time, a family of four who'd simply decided, after months of visiting, that they preferred raising their children somewhere that had, in Fang Ruxue's words, "considerably better community support structures than the world above currently offers most people." Strata population now stood at just past four thousand, a number that would have seemed impossible to him five years ago and now simply registered as Tuesday's ordinary business.

The workshop district's ongoing dispute over shared tool storage had, predictably, escalated into something requiring Fang Ruxue's formal governance framework rather than informal negotiation — two groups of craftspeople, equally reasonable, entirely unable to agree on whose projects deserved priority access to a particularly good set of Formican-adapted woodworking tools. He read through the mediation notes with something close to fondness.

"This is my favorite kind of problem," he told Fang Ruxue, handing the folder back. "Completely mundane. Nobody's in danger. Just two groups of people who both want the same nice thing at the same time."

"I'll pass along that you find their dispute charming," she said dryly. "I suspect the carpenter's guild will be thrilled to hear it."

But it was the final item in the summary that actually stopped him, reading it twice before he trusted the specific warmth it produced.

*A daughter, born three days ago, to two of the Strata's second wave of residents — the couple who arrived shortly after the disclosure protocol began, both former university researchers who'd relocated once their work made continuing at their original institutions impractical. Mother and child both healthy. First child born within the residential wing itself, rather than at a surface hospital.*

He went to see them that afternoon, finding the small family in one of the residential wing's warmer rooms, the mother exhausted and radiant in the particular way new parents always seemed to manage simultaneously, the father holding the infant with the careful, awed attention of someone still adjusting to the fact that this new, entire person was actually real.

"We wanted her born here," the mother explained, when he asked. "Not the surface. Here. This is where we actually built our life, once we understood what this place really was. It felt right that she'd start hers the same way."

He held the baby briefly, when they offered, feeling the particular, specific weight of new, uncomplicated life settle against him — considerably smaller than any perceptual scale he currently held, and somehow, in that exact moment, mattering considerably more than any of it.

"What's her name?" he asked.

"We haven't fully decided," the father admitted. "We've been considering something that means 'new beginning,' though we're still debating the exact character. Do you have any thoughts?"

He considered the question seriously, understanding the specific honor of being asked. "I don't think it's my place to choose," he said. "But I'll say this — whatever you choose, she's going to grow up in a place that didn't exist when either of you were her age. That's worth naming carefully."

The mother smiled, exhausted and warm. "We're taking our time," she said. "She's not going anywhere. Neither is this place, we're hoping."

He left the residential wing that evening with the infant's small, specific weight still somehow present in his memory, more vivid, in that particular moment, than anything the dissolution zone's vast entropy had shown him across the past six weeks.

The scale of what he was building had grown, genuinely, enormously, across the past months. But the Strata itself, he understood, walking home through its ordinary evening light, remained exactly what it had always been at its actual core — a place where people quarreled over tool storage, and grew families, and chose, again and again, in a thousand small specific ways, to build ordinary lives inside something extraordinary.

The scale never fully replaced the specific. He was grateful, every single day, that it hadn't.
