# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 471 — Lin Mushi Reads the Letter Aloud

He did not decide to read it aloud. He simply found himself doing it, partway through the stillness that followed the translation's end, his own voice sounding strange and small in a chamber that had never, in forty million years, heard anything spoken inside it at all.

"Teacher," he began, the word arriving in his own language now, carried across from Veth-Karun's careful notation into something he could actually speak. "I do not know if this will reach you."

He worked through it slowly, the whole account, start to finish, not performing it — he was careful about that distinction, even in the moment, some old discipline insisting he notice the difference — simply saying it, plainly, the way a person reads something aloud when the reading itself has become the only remaining way to honor what it cost to write.

*Everything is present simultaneously,* he said, into the cold, patient dark. *The mistake I made was believing that simultaneous presence required the dissolution of the observer.*

His voice caught, once, on *I should have held someone,* and he let it catch, not correcting himself, not smoothing the moment into something more composed. He had learned, across five years, that some moments deserved exactly the amount of unsteadiness they actually cost.

The Surveyor remained beside him throughout, silent, its formal presence neither intruding nor withdrawing, simply witnessing the way it had promised it would — a fair, uninvolved observer, exactly as its own nature had always described itself.

*Observation logged,* its notation registered afterward, quiet, careful, the specific formality of an institution recording something it understood mattered beyond mere procedure. *The petitioner read the full account aloud, unprompted, without pause for translation assistance. I did not interrupt. I did not consider it my place to interrupt.*

He finished the final line — *I should have held someone,* spoken once more, the account's closing thought given voice a second time, deliberately, because some sentences deserved to be said twice — and then simply stopped, the chamber's silence closing back in around the last echo of his own voice.

He did not write anything for a while afterward. He did not reach for the notebook, did not attempt an observation, a rule, a careful record of what he'd just experienced. He simply sat, in the ancient chamber's cold, patient dark, letting the weight of it settle the way weight settles into ground given enough time and enough stillness.

He thought of Veth-Karun, alone in the final 0.7 seconds of an existence considerably longer and more accomplished than his own currently was, reaching not for more knowledge, not for one final technical insight, but for a single word addressed to someone who might never receive it. He thought of the specific loneliness embedded in that choice — decades, presumably, of solitary preparation, an entire working life spent gathering understanding alone, arriving finally at the one truth accumulated knowledge alone could never have taught: that some things could only be survived with someone else's hand actually present to hold.

He thought, too, of his own life, and found, sitting there, that he had built something Veth-Karun never had — five careful, devoted Anchors, a mother who finally knew everything, a whole constructed structure of people who had, without exception, said yes before he'd even finished asking. He had built it slowly, without fully understanding, until this exact moment, precisely how much it mattered that he had.

The Surveyor did not speak again during the twenty minutes he sat there. It simply remained, quiet and formal and patient, its own version, he suspected, of respect.

When he finally reached for the notebook, his hand had steadied, though something in him, he understood, had changed in a way no observation was going to fully capture.

*Observation 472,* he wrote, finally, the entry shorter than almost anything he'd recorded in five years. *I read it aloud. I am not the same as I was before I did. I don't yet know exactly what that means. I know it matters.*

He closed the notebook, rose, and turned, at last, toward the long ascent back to the people who were waiting for him — Chen Yufei, Liang Jingzhi, Dr. Shen Yue, Wei Canglan, Meen-4, five names he found himself, for the first time in the whole of this extraordinary life, saying quietly to himself like a kind of prayer.
