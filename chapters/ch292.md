# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter Two Hundred and Ninety-Two — Dr. Shen Yue's Paper, Draft

Dr. Shen Yue found him in the laboratory on a Thursday evening, a printed manuscript in one hand held with the particular care of someone who had reread it enough times that the paper itself had started to feel consequential.

"I want you to read this before I show it to anyone else," she said, setting it on the workbench between them without further preamble. "Not because I need your approval. Because you're the only person who can tell me whether I've made an error that would embarrass us both."

He picked it up. The title alone told him the scale of what she'd been working on for the past several months: *Anomalous Dark Energy Signatures in the Chengdu Metropolitan Region: A Case for Undocumented Field Interactions.*

"You're publishing this."

"I'm drafting this. There's a difference, and the difference is where you come in." She pulled a stool over and sat, arms folded, watching him read with the focused patience of someone used to waiting for results. "I've spent four months collecting dark energy density readings across the metropolitan area — standard cosmological instrumentation, nothing exotic, nothing that would raise questions about where the data came from. The readings don't fit any existing model. Not by a small margin. By a margin large enough that if I'm right, it rewrites several assumptions the field has held for decades."

He scanned the abstract, then the methodology section, recognizing within the first page the careful, exhaustive rigor she brought to everything — control variables accounted for, alternative explanations preemptively addressed, the specific defensive thoroughness of someone who expected to be doubted and had built her argument to survive the doubting.

"You're describing Qi density fluctuations," he said. "In physics language."

"I'm describing what my instruments measured, using the only vocabulary my instruments understand." Something dry entered her voice. "I know what's actually causing this. You know what's actually causing this. The paper cannot say that, obviously, and so it says what it can honestly say: the readings exist, they don't fit current models, and they correlate — geographically, precisely — with locations I am not naming, for reasons the paper also does not explain."

He kept reading, understanding gradually the specific tightrope she was walking — evidence real enough to be taken seriously, framed carefully enough not to reveal a world that wasn't hers to reveal.

"The correlation section," he said. "You've mapped the anomalies against — what, population density? Urban infrastructure?"

"Against nothing, publicly. Privately, against every Strata location I've been able to identify within the metropolitan boundary, plus the Pacific Ring node closest to the coast." She met his eyes directly. "The correlation is close to perfect. Ninety-four percent of the significant anomalies sit within five hundred meters of a location I know, from working with you, to be either a Strata threshold or an active Circuit node."

He set the manuscript down slowly, the weight of what she'd built settling into him fully now.

"You've found us," he said. "Through physics. Without anyone telling you where to look."

"I've found the shadow you cast on instruments built to measure something else entirely." She held his gaze, something steady and unflinching in it. "I want to be honest about what I'm asking, because I think you deserve honesty rather than a paper that quietly appears in a journal one day without warning. This is real science. It will be taken seriously eventually, even if the first responses are rejection. And once it is taken seriously, people considerably less careful than me are going to start asking why unexplained energy signatures cluster so precisely around specific geographic points."

"You're asking if I want you to submit it."

"I'm telling you I'm going to submit it," she corrected, gently but without softening the substance. "I've thought about this for a long time, and I don't think withholding real findings because they're inconvenient is a choice I'm willing to make, even for you. What I'm asking is whether you're ready for what happens after."

He sat with the question honestly, the laboratory's quiet hum of instruments filling the space where an easier answer might have gone.

"Are you?" he asked finally.

Something shifted in her expression — not surprise, exactly, but the particular recognition of someone who'd expected the question turned back on her and had prepared for it anyway.

"I've thought about the timescale," she said. "Rejection first, probably for a year or two, maybe longer. Then slow acceptance, the kind that happens once enough independent groups replicate the readings and can't explain them away. Then, eventually, the kind of attention that stops being purely academic." She paused. "I don't think I'm ready for all of it. I don't think anyone could be. But I think the honest answer to your question is that I'd rather walk toward it carefully, with you knowing exactly what I'm doing and why, than pretend the data doesn't exist because pretending is more comfortable."

He looked at the manuscript again — months of careful, rigorous work building toward a door neither of them fully knew what stood on the other side of, and felt, beneath the specific worry the moment deserved, something that was also, unmistakably, respect.

"Then submit it," he said. "And let's start preparing for what happens after, together, rather than waiting to react to it."

"That's what I was hoping you'd say." She reached for the manuscript, tucking it back under her arm with the same care she'd carried it in with. "I'll send you the final draft before I submit. Not for permission. For preparation."

He nodded, watching her go, and stood alone in the laboratory afterward for a long moment, the quiet hum of instruments around him suddenly sounding less like background noise and more like the first, distant note of something considerably larger beginning to move.
