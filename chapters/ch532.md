# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 532 — The Mayor Meets Lin Mushi

Su Lian requested a formal meeting three weeks into her term — not urgent, her message had made clear, but deliberate, the specific careful scheduling of someone who understood that a first genuine conversation with the city's founding cultivator deserved more than a hurried exchange in a council hallway.

He arrived at her office at the appointed hour, and found her exactly as her reputation had described her — unhurried, precise, a woman who kept a single framed photograph of the housing project that had defined her prior career on the wall behind her desk, positioned, he suspected deliberately, where any visitor would notice it and understand something about the kind of governing she intended to practice.

"I've read the founding records," she said, once the initial formalities had concluded. "The early Strata, the first residents, the negotiations that got this city to eight thousand people without a single serious incident of the kind every honest projection warned would eventually happen. I understand the history reasonably well now."

"But."

"But I've never once heard, in any of it, a clear answer to a fairly basic question." She studied him, unhurried, the same patient directness she'd clearly brought to two decades of public housing budgets. "What do you actually want this city to be?"

He sat with the question longer than he suspected she'd expected, not because he lacked an answer, but because he understood, turning it over, that he had never once been asked it quite this plainly, by someone whose job it now was to actually build toward whatever answer he gave.

"A place where a Formican who's spent three thousand years alone with her own records," he said finally, "and a retired schoolteacher who likes gardening, can have lunch together and argue, genuinely, happily, about soil composition."

Su Lian did not respond immediately. She held the answer the way he had learned, across six years, that the best listeners held things — turning it, testing its edges, checking it against everything she already understood before deciding what it actually meant.

"That's very specific," she said eventually.

"It's meant to be. I could give you something grander — a vision statement about interspecies cooperation, historic reconciliation, the healing of a forty-million-year separation. All of that would be true. None of it is actually what I want the city to feel like, on an ordinary Tuesday, to the people living in it."

"You want it to feel ordinary."

"I want the extraordinary parts to become so thoroughly, comfortably ordinary that two people who'd have had nothing obvious in common a decade ago can spend an hour disagreeing about drainage and soil pH, and neither of them thinks, for even a moment, that the disagreement is remarkable. That's not a lesser goal than reconciliation. I think it might be what reconciliation actually looks like, once it's finished being a project and started being simply how people live."

She was quiet a long moment, and then, without further comment, picked up a pen and wrote something on the notepad in front of her, unhurried, deliberate.

"I'm going to use that," she said. "Not as a poster slogan. As the actual founding statement — the thing I measure every policy decision against, going forward. Does this help a three-thousand-year-old Formican archivist and a retired human schoolteacher have lunch together and argue about soil."

"That's a strange metric for a city government."

"It's an honest one," she said. "I've built cities around vaguer commitments than that and watched them fail to deliver on considerably grander language. I'd rather build this one around something small enough to actually check."

He left the meeting, later, with the specific quiet satisfaction of watching an idea he'd offered almost offhandedly become, in someone else's careful, capable hands, something he suspected would outlast him considerably.

He thought, walking back through the council building's ordinary corridors, of every prior version of governance the Strata had passed through on its way to Su Lian's office — the earliest informal arrangements, decided between himself and Meen-4 over questions neither of them had fully anticipated; the transitional council that had formed once the population crossed a thousand, well-meaning and often overwhelmed; and now this, a properly elected mayor with two decades of unglamorous, competent public service behind her, treating a Formican archivist's lunch companionship with a retired schoolteacher as a serious metric of civic success.

He understood, reflecting on it, that this progression was itself the clearest evidence available that the project had succeeded on its own terms. He had never wanted to remain the city's indispensable center. He had wanted, from the very earliest, uncertain days of the first pocket dimension room he'd built for a woman who couldn't climb stairs, to build something that could eventually hold itself up without him — and here, in Su Lian's careful pen strokes on a notepad, he found the clearest proof yet that it finally, genuinely could.
