# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 373 — Dr. Shen Yue — The Cost Arrives

The email arrived on a Tuesday, which Dr. Shen Yue would later note, with the particular dark humor she reserved for things that hurt too much to discuss any other way, felt insultingly ordinary. Institutions did not revoke your career on a day that meant anything. They did it on a Tuesday, in the flat administrative language of a template someone in Human Resources had probably used a dozen times before for reasons that had nothing to do with her.

Lin Mushi heard about it not from her directly but from Meen-4, who caught the change in the Colony Link's ambient signature before any human could have — a specific, contained grief that Dr. Shen Yue was clearly working very hard to keep contained, and mostly succeeding, and the effort of that containment was, in its own way, more visible to the Colony Link than open weeping would have been.

He found her in her small university office that evening, boxes already half-packed on the desk though her actual removal date, according to the email, was still six weeks out. She was efficient about it in the particular way people were efficient about things they didn't want to feel slowly.

"You didn't have to come," she said, not looking up from a shelf of journals she was sorting into keep and discard piles with more decisiveness than the task probably required.

"I know. I wanted to."

"Tenure-track position revoked pending review of research methodology." She read the phrase aloud in a flat, mocking cadence, the exact register of the email itself. "My methodology was rigorous enough to survive three rounds of peer review before either paper went out. They know that. Everyone on that committee knows that. It's not about methodology. It's about the fact that what I found is inconvenient, and inconvenience needed a respectable-sounding reason."

"I'm sorry," he said, because it was true, and because he'd learned, slowly, that there wasn't much else worth saying in moments like this.

"Don't be sorry. Be useful." She set down the stack of journals and finally looked at him directly, and he saw, underneath the brisk efficiency, something rawer that she'd clearly been keeping carefully out of sight until someone she trusted walked in. "I knew this was possible. Meen-4 told me, months ago, in almost exactly these words — the second paper would cost me something, and I said yes anyway, because the data mattered more than the position. I still believe that. Knowing something is coming doesn't make it not land."

"No," he agreed. "It usually doesn't."

She sat down heavily in the chair behind the desk that would, in six weeks, no longer be hers, and rubbed both hands over her face in a single tired motion. "I built this," she said, more to the ceiling than to him. "Eleven years. Undergraduate, doctorate, the postdoc nobody wanted, three years of adjuncting before the tenure track opened up. Eleven years of building exactly the life I told myself, at twenty-two, that I wanted. And I traded it for the truth, and I'd trade it again, and both of those things are true at the same time and I don't currently know how to hold them without it hurting."

He didn't try to fix that. The style of comfort he'd learned, over years of watching people he cared about absorb costs he couldn't prevent, was rarely to offer a solution. It was to stay in the room.

"Can I help pack," he said instead.

She looked at him for a long moment, something almost like a laugh threatening the corner of her mouth despite everything. "You're going to fold journals badly and I'm going to have to redo it."

"Probably," he admitted.

"Fine. Sit down. Box on the left is keep, box on the right is spite — anything that rejected my last grant application goes in spite, and I don't want to talk about why that matters to me right now."

He sat, and folded journals badly, exactly as predicted, and she redid several of them without comment, and for an hour neither of them talked about the review or the Surveyor or the cost that was still, six weeks out, only beginning to make itself fully known. They talked about nothing — an old professor she'd disliked, a paper that had embarrassed them both at a conference years ago, the particular indignity of academic furniture.

It was, he thought later, walking home through streets that hadn't changed while everything underneath them had, exactly the kind of evening the whole project was meant to protect. Not the grand disclosure. Not the review. This — one person, sitting with another person, folding journals badly in a room that was ending, because ending things well still mattered even when you couldn't stop them from ending.

He wrote, that night, a short line in the notebook, not analysis, just a fact he wanted to remember. *Dr. Shen Yue lost her position today. She would make the same choice again. I need to make sure the ending is worth what it's costing the people who chose it with me.*
