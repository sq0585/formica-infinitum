# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 478 — Liang Jingzhi's Decision

She asked to see him privately on the ninth day after the call went out, before the general screening process had even finished its first pass, and something in the specific formality of the request — a scheduled time, a closed door, none of their usual easy drop-by — told him this wasn't going to be an ordinary conversation.

He found her in the geology department's small shared office, the same unglamorous room she'd worked from since long before any of this began, a stack of dissolution-zone coherence data spread across her desk in the particular organized density she brought to everything she considered genuinely important.

"You're not here to volunteer," he said, sitting across from her. "You already volunteered. This is something else."

"It is." She closed the file in front of her and met his eyes directly, the same plain, unhurried steadiness she'd brought to every difficult conversation across five years. "I want to propose something the three hundred-odd volunteer structure wasn't built to include. I've been sitting with it for a week before bringing it to you, because I wanted to be certain it wasn't simply enthusiasm talking."

"I'm listening."

"The network we're building — three hundred-some anchor points, each holding a small, distributed piece of the dissolution zone's coherence pattern — is sound. I believe in it completely. But it's diffuse, by design. No single point of contact deep enough to actually read what's happening at the zone's center, the way a single well-placed seismograph reads an earthquake's actual epicenter instead of just its scattered aftershocks." She paused, choosing her next words with visible care. "I want to be that point. Not one of many. The primary anchor. Permanently."

He felt something in his chest go very still. "Permanently."

"Permanently," she confirmed, without flinching from the word. "Not a rotating shift, not a bounded commitment I can step back from once the immediate work is finished. A lasting, structural presence — part of my own Colony Link signature extended into the zone's center and left there, indefinitely, the way a person leaves a hand on something that needs steadying rather than simply checking on it occasionally."

He sat with that, the specific vertigo of it settling into him slowly, the way the largest requests always did. "You understand what you're describing costs. This isn't the same category as what I asked the three hundred and twenty-three to consider."

"I know precisely what category it's in," she said. "That's why I came to you alone first, before anyone else, before the Anchors, before Meen-4's technical review. I wanted you to hear it from me directly, plainly, without anyone else's reaction shaping how I said it."

"Why you?" he asked, and found the question arrived more raw than he'd intended it to. "Of everyone who could propose this — why does it have to be you?"

She considered the question seriously, the way she considered everything, turning it over with the same patient attention she'd once given a stubborn seismic anomaly outside a dead Qi node. "Because I've spent my entire career reading the record that rock keeps of things that no longer exist," she said finally. "Extinction events. Vanished species. Civilizations that left nothing behind but a chemical signature in a sediment layer. I understand, better than almost anyone you could ask, what it means for something enormous to have happened with no coherent witness left to account for it. The dissolution zone is exactly that — a forty-million-year gap in the Void Lattice's own record, waiting for someone to actually stand inside it and witness what's there."

"That's not the same as safety," he said quietly.

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. I'm not telling you this is safe. I'm telling you it's mine to consider, and I've considered it, and I haven't finished considering it yet — which is exactly why I'm bringing it to you now, before I decide anything, rather than after."

He looked at her for a long moment, the weight of the request settling into the room between them, and understood, with a clarity that felt both terrible and inevitable, that he owed her the same discipline he'd learned to offer everyone else facing something this large.

"I'm not going to tell you yes or no," he said slowly. "I don't think that's mine to decide. But I'm not going to let you decide it alone in a closed office either. This needs the other Anchors. All of it — the mechanism, the risk, everything Meen-4 and Dr. Shen Yue can actually tell us about what 'permanently' would really mean."

"I was hoping you'd say that," Liang Jingzhi said, something easing slightly in her shoulders. "I don't want this decided quickly, Lin Mushi. I want it decided properly. I just needed someone to hear the shape of it first, before it became a council discussion with five opinions in the room."

He nodded, and felt, sitting with her in the small, ordinary office, the particular weight of a request that was going to require considerably more care than anything the general volunteer network had asked of anyone so far.

"I'll gather everyone this week," he said. "You'll have the full technical picture before anyone asks you to commit to anything further."

"Thank you," she said, and reached for the coherence data still spread across her desk, the same steady, practical woman who had spent five years turning enormous, strange truths into something she could hold in her own two hands.
