# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter Two Hundred and Four — Two Scholars Meet

The threshold in the northwest corner of the store had stopped being remarkable to him months ago. Watching Fang Ruxue's face when she crossed it reminded him it had never stopped being remarkable to anyone else.

She didn't gasp. She didn't reach for a wall the way some people did when their inner ear briefly disagreed with what their eyes were reporting. She stood very still for perhaps four seconds, taking in the outer hall's scale — the distant ceiling lost in amber light, the shelving that ran on past the point where ordinary architecture should have run out of building to support it — and then she did something he hadn't seen anyone else do on a first crossing.

She took out a small notebook and began writing.

"You're not going to ask what this is," he said.

"I'll ask better questions once I understand what I'm looking at." She didn't look up from the page. "Right now I'd only be asking you to translate wonder into words, and words are a worse instrument for it than my own eyes."

They spent six hours in the outer library.

She asked questions for the first four of them — methodical, layered, each one building on the last in a way that told him she was constructing a model rather than collecting facts. He answered what he could. He told her, plainly, when he didn't know something, which seemed to surprise her less than it surprised most people.

In the fifth hour, she asked to see the Archive notation directly.

He brought her to a section of catalogued texts he'd worked through months ago — early material, well-translated, low risk. She read the labels slowly, her finger tracing each symbol cluster without touching the surface, the careful non-contact habit of someone who'd spent a career around fragile things.

She stopped at a cluster near the bottom of the third shelf.

"We have a fragment of this," she said. Her voice had changed — lower, more careful. "We call it the Heaven Map. Cosmological poetry. I've spent half my career on it and concluded the inconsistencies were intentional — a kind of philosophical humility about the limits of what the ancient astronomers could measure."

"It's not poetry."

"I didn't think so, after the third symbol matched exactly." She looked at him. "What is it?"

He pulled the catalogue record. The Heaven Map's full designation in the Archive's notation translated, as closely as he could render it, to something like *Survey, Stellar, Local Volume* — a working title he'd given it himself, since the Formican original had no concept that mapped cleanly onto a proper noun.

"An astronomical survey," he said. "Positions, distances, proper motions for roughly four thousand stars visible from this hemisphere, recorded by instruments more precise than anything your tradition has built. The fragment your school preserved is a small section of it — probably copied, badly, by someone working from secondhand description rather than the original."

She was quiet for a long moment.

"Two thousand years," she said. "Two thousand years of scholars treating a measurement error as a metaphor, because the alternative was admitting we'd lost the instrument that produced the original data."

"You didn't lose the instrument. You never had it. Your tradition built its own observational methods around what the survey implied, without the survey itself."

"Which means our methods worked," she said slowly, "for reasons we never understood."

"Yes."

She closed her notebook.

"I would like to see the rest of it," she said. "All four thousand stars, if it's intact. I understand that's a significant request."

He thought about the six hours behind them — her patience, her precision, the absence of anything that read like an agenda beyond the work itself.

"It's intact," he said. "It'll take longer than tonight to go through properly. But yes."

She looked, for the first time since she'd crossed the threshold, less like a scholar conducting an interview and more like a person who had just been handed back something she'd spent her whole career believing was lost.

"Then I suppose," she said, "we have a great deal of work ahead of us."
