# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 449 — What He Missed

Wei Canglan arrived the next morning with the folder he'd promised, apologetically, only to open if something genuinely required attention, and Lin Mushi understood, watching him set it down with visible relief, that eight weeks was longer than even Wei Canglan's considerable patience had been built to hold.

"Nothing catastrophic," Wei Canglan said, which had, over five years, become his standard opening for exactly the kind of news that was going to take forty minutes to fully explain. "Just — accumulated. You should probably know all of it before it becomes several smaller catastrophes instead of one manageable briefing."

He worked through it methodically, the way he worked through everything, and Lin Mushi found himself grateful, not for the first time, for a man whose entire disposition ran toward turning chaos into ordered lists.

The disclosure protocol had continued its slow, unglamorous progress — two more government delegations through the Strata's entry procedure, both handled with the same calibrated honesty Wei Canglan had brought to the first one, both departing, according to his summary, "with a lot of questions and no immediate demands." The Containment Orthodoxy had filed three additional compliance reports in his absence, all accurate, one containing what Wei Canglan described, dryly, as "a genuinely interesting misreading of our irrigation records that took Fang Ruxue two full days to correct through proper channels."

"They're still watching," Lin Mushi said.

"They're always still watching. I've stopped finding it alarming and started finding it almost reassuring. An institution that stops checking your work is an institution that's stopped caring whether you succeed or fail, and I'd rather have the Orthodoxy irritated and attentive than indifferent."

Strata administration had continued exactly as it always continued — a dispute over workshop allocation resolved through Fang Ruxue's governance framework without needing anyone's direct intervention, two new residents settled into the residential wing, a minor Circuit maintenance issue Amber-0 had caught and corrected before it became anything worth mentioning at all. The particular, steady hum of twenty-four hundred people building ordinary lives inside something extraordinary, exactly the way it had been humming when he'd left for the installation eight weeks earlier.

"And your project?" Lin Mushi asked, remembering, belatedly, that Wei Canglan had been working through something technical when the departure had interrupted it. "The fourth ratio problem — did the reframe actually hold?"

Something shifted in Wei Canglan's expression, the particular quiet pride of someone who'd been waiting for the question. "It held," he said. "Better than held. Once I stopped trying to force continuity where the technique wanted a break, the whole structure resolved in about three days. I've since applied the same principle to two other stalled designs. I owe you a debt I'm not entirely sure how to repay, given that you apparently solved it in about four sentences before disappearing for two months."

"You solved it. I just noticed the assumption."

"That's usually how the important ones go, in my experience." Wei Canglan closed the folder, the briefing evidently complete. "Welcome back, by the way. Properly, I mean — not just the administrative debrief. It's good to have you in the room again instead of eighty million kilometers away."

Lin Mushi found, hearing it, that the sentiment landed with more weight than its plain phrasing suggested it should. Five years in, Wei Canglan's objections and observations had become their own kind of steady architecture, load-bearing in ways neither of them had ever needed to state directly.

"It's good to be back," he said, and meant it in the specific, grounded way that only became available after two months spent somewhere considerably less ordinary. "Anything else waiting that can't hold another day?"

"One thing." Wei Canglan hesitated, which was unusual enough that Lin Mushi felt something in his attention sharpen. "Your mother called the administrative line twice while you were away. Nothing urgent, she said both times. She just wanted confirmation you were safe, since apparently the Sunday calls stopped without warning and she didn't want to worry you by making it sound more concerning than it was."

Lin Mushi felt something quiet settle into his chest — not alarm, not yet, simply the specific, familiar guilt of a promise quietly broken by circumstances that had felt, at the time, entirely unavoidable. "I should call her today."

"I'd imagine so," Wei Canglan said, gathering the folder and rising. "Everything else can wait for tomorrow's briefing. That one, I don't think should."

Lin Mushi watched him go, and sat for a moment afterward in the quiet of the administrative chamber, the ordinary, patient world he'd returned to settling comfortably back into place around him — governance, disputes, ratios solved by careful reframing, an entire city continuing exactly as it should have continued in his absence.

And underneath all of it, waiting, a phone call he should have made two months ago, to the one person who had never once, in five years, needed the full truth to keep loving him unconditionally regardless.

He reached for his phone before he'd finished the thought.
