# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter Two Hundred and Seventy-Three — Fang Ruxue's Ceremony

The Cultivation Research Collective's formal inauguration filled a university auditorium Fang Ruxue had spent two months arranging, eighty-nine members gathered in a room considerably larger than any previous Collective gathering had required.

Lin Mushi sat near the back, the position he'd quietly insisted on, watching the room fill with people whose presence here represented, in one form or another, the careful, difficult work of the past year — former Celestial Compass scholars sitting beside former Iron Meridian practitioners, Stone Hall's recent converts finding seats near the independent cultivators who'd never belonged to any institution at all, Lady Shen near the front, composed and formally dressed, the particular dignity of someone choosing presence over hiding from what her own transformation had cost her.

Three Formican representatives attended as well — Meen-4 among them, her presence at a human gathering of this scale a genuine, deliberate statement he understood she hadn't made lightly.

Fang Ruxue took the podium at exactly the scheduled hour, her composure carrying the same measured precision he'd come to expect from her across every careful conversation they'd shared.

"Two years ago," she began, "I was a scholar who had spent thirty-one years treating an ancestral text as cosmological poetry, because the alternative — that it might be literal, that the explanation had simply been lost — felt too large a possibility to seriously entertain. I was wrong. The willingness to be wrong, carefully and honestly, is the foundation everything in this room is built on."

She spoke for twenty minutes, measured and precise, walking through the Collective's founding principles without ever raising her voice above the room's natural volume — open access to verified knowledge, governance structures designed against the concentration of authority that had eventually corrupted every prior institution, honest acknowledgment of what had been lost in the old Courts' dissolution alongside clear-eyed recognition of what had been gained.

"We are not building something perfect," she said, near the close. "We are building something honest, which is harder, and considerably more durable. Perfection collapses the moment its flaws are discovered. Honesty simply adjusts, and continues."

Lin Mushi, listening from the back of the room, found himself thinking it was, without exaggeration, the best speech he had ever heard — not because of any particular rhetorical flourish, but because every word of it was precisely, carefully true, delivered by someone who had earned every syllable of authority she carried while speaking it.

She closed with a final observation that landed harder than anything that had come before it.

"Many of you lost something to be here today. Institutional standing, professional identity, the comfortable certainty of a tradition you'd spent years or decades building your life around. I want to say plainly: that loss is real, and it deserves to be honored rather than dismissed simply because what replaced it is, on balance, better. We are not erasing what came before us. We are building forward from it, carefully, honestly, together."

The room's applause, when it came, carried a particular quality he'd rarely heard before — not the enthusiastic noise of celebration, but something steadier, more considered, the sound of people who understood exactly what they were applauding and chose, with full knowledge of its cost, to applaud it anyway.

He found Chen Yufei beside him afterward, both of them quiet in the gathering's aftermath.

"Eighty-nine members," she said. "Eighteen months ago, this was you, me, and an archive neither of us fully understood."

"I know," he said. "I keep doing the arithmetic and it keeps not quite fitting in my head."

"Does it need to fit?"

He considered this, watching the room slowly empty into careful, hopeful conversation among people who, a year earlier, would have had no institutional reason to speak to each other at all.

"No," he said finally. "I don't think it does. I think some things are simply true regardless of whether I can hold the full scope of them comfortably in my head at once."

She reached for his hand, and the two of them stood together at the edge of a room full of careful, hard-won possibility, the Collective's primary political objective finally, fully achieved.
