# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter Two Hundred and Three — The Celestial Compass Scholar

She was waiting outside the convenience store when he arrived for his shift, which told him several things before she'd said a word.

She'd found the surface anchor point — not public information, which meant real investigative work or a contact inside one of the Courts willing to share it. She'd come in person rather than sending an intermediary, which in his limited experience of Court protocol was either a significant gesture of respect or a calculated risk someone senior enough to take it had decided was worth taking. And she'd come alone, no escort, no visible cultivator presence beyond her own, standing under the convenience store's fluorescent overhang with the patient, unhurried stillness of someone for whom waiting was not a hardship.

"Lin Mushi," she said. Not a question.

"You have the advantage."

"Fang Ruxue." She inclined her head slightly. "Celestial Compass School. I imagine you've seen the name attached to several documents recently."

He had. The anonymous message from two weeks ago sat in his saved folder, and while he had no proof, the cadence of it — measured, unthreatening, certain — matched something in how she'd just introduced herself.

"You sent the message," he said.

A brief pause, then something that might have been the beginning of approval crossing her face. "I did. I find direct approaches waste less of everyone's time, but I wanted to see how you'd respond to ambiguity first. You saved it instead of deleting it or escalating it. That told me something useful."

"What did it tell you?"

"That you're someone who collects information rather than reacting to it." She studied him for a moment, the kind of look he associated with people doing careful, unhurried arithmetic. "I'm fifty-eight years old. I've spent thirty-one of those years in the Celestial Compass tradition, the last twelve as its senior astronomical scholar. I've verified your documents against our oldest texts personally, over six days, without sleeping more than four hours on any of them."

"And your conclusion."

"My conclusion is that I would like to speak with the person who produced them, in a setting where I can ask questions and trust the answers." She did not move, did not gesture toward the store, did not perform the kind of urgency he'd come to expect from people who wanted something from him. "I understand you work nights here. I'm willing to wait until your shift ends, if that's more convenient."

He considered her for a moment — the stillness, the precision, the complete absence of the political calculation he'd grown used to reading in every other Court representative who'd approached him in the last two weeks.

"My shift starts in four minutes," he said. "Would you like to come in? It's a long shift, and I make decent tea in the back."

Something in her expression shifted, almost imperceptibly. "I would like that very much."

---

She sat on the stool behind the counter that Gao usually claimed during slow hours, accepted the tea without comment on its quality, and asked her first real question forty minutes later, after watching him handle three customers with the same unhurried attention he'd given her.

"The Celestial Compass tradition has a foundational text we call the Heaven Map," she said. "Cosmological poetry, we always assumed — beautiful, internally consistent, completely useless for prediction beyond a certain point. I've spent half my career trying to reconcile its inconsistencies with observed astronomy and concluding that the inconsistencies were intentional, a kind of philosophical humility about the limits of measurement."

She set the tea down.

"Your documents use a notation system I don't recognize. But three of the symbol clusters in your fourth document match symbol clusters in the Heaven Map exactly. Not similar. Exact."

He felt something settle into place — a connection he'd suspected existed somewhere in the Archive's astronomical sections but hadn't had a reason to chase yet.

"It's not poetry," he said.

"I didn't think so. Not anymore." She looked at him directly. "I'd like to know what it actually is. I have six days of verified evidence telling me you can tell me, and a career's worth of instinct telling me I should have come here sooner."

"It's a survey," he said. "An astronomical survey. Properly read, it should be more accurate than anything your tradition has ever produced — because it was made by people who could measure things your tradition has spent two thousand years trying to estimate."

He watched the precise, unhurried scholar across the counter go very still.

"Show me," she said.

He glanced at the clock. Five hours left on shift, and a conversation that had just become considerably more important than restocking the cooler.

"After close," he said. "I know somewhere better suited to it than a convenience store."

She didn't ask where. She only nodded, picked her tea back up, and waited — the same patient stillness she'd brought to the doorway, now aimed at a door she didn't yet know existed.
