# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter Three Hundred and Fifty-Five — Auntie Zhang, Again

He saw her at the market near his mother's neighborhood, entirely by chance, the specific ordinary bustle of a Saturday morning crowd giving no warning before he recognized, with a small jolt of surprised recognition, the familiar figure examining a stall's vegetables with the same careful, unhurried scrutiny he remembered from every previous encounter.

Auntie Zhang. Older now, visibly — her hair fully white where it had once carried streaks of grey, her posture carrying a careful deliberateness that suggested her knees troubled her more than they once had. She was selecting vegetables with the specific, practiced patience of someone who had shopped at this exact market for decades, examining each item with the same critical eye she'd have brought to it thirty years earlier.

He stood at a respectful distance, watching, uncertain whether to approach.

She didn't recognize him. He understood this immediately, watching her attention pass over him with the same mild, incurious disregard she gave every other stranger in the crowded market, no flicker of recognition crossing her weathered face, no pause in her careful vegetable selection.

He felt the specific, complicated ache of it settle into his chest — not disappointment exactly, though disappointment was somewhere in it, but something larger and more difficult to name, the particular vertigo of standing invisible before someone who had, in her own small way, been present at several of the more significant turning points of his impossible life, entirely unaware of the role she'd played.

---

He didn't approach her. He stood at the market's edge instead, watching, allowing himself the quiet, unhurried privilege of simply observing someone whose ordinary, unremarkable presence had somehow become, across two years, a kind of private touchstone for him — proof, each time he'd encountered her, that beneath everything this world had become, an ordinary life continued, unbothered, exactly as it always had.

She finished her selection, paid with the careful, practiced efficiency of someone managing a fixed budget with long habit, and turned to leave, a bag of vegetables in one hand.

"For my daughter's family," she said to the vendor, in passing, her voice carrying clearly enough across the market's ordinary noise that he caught it without meaning to. "They're coming for dinner tonight. My granddaughter's turning six."

The vendor said something warm in response, the kind of small, practiced pleasantry markets everywhere seemed to generate, and Auntie Zhang smiled — a real smile, unguarded, the specific warmth of someone whose life, whatever its aches and its slowing knees, remained full of people who mattered to her.

---

He watched her disappear into the market's crowd, her bag of vegetables carried carefully, her steps slow but purposeful, heading toward an afternoon that would hold nothing more remarkable than a family dinner and a six-year-old granddaughter's birthday.

He found, standing there, that the ache in his chest had settled, gradually, into something closer to gratitude.

She didn't need to remember him. She didn't need to know that her ordinary, unremarkable presence, glimpsed at intervals across two impossible years, had somehow become a kind of quiet anchor for him — proof, each time, that the world he'd once belonged to fully still continued, whole and unbothered, exactly as ordinary and exactly as precious as it had always been.

He walked home slowly afterward, taking the long route through streets that felt, in the day's ordinary afternoon light, entirely unremarkable — no Colony Link drift, no review clock pressing at the edges of his attention, just the simple, grounding fact of an old woman buying vegetables for her granddaughter's birthday, unaware and untouched by anything he carried, exactly the way he'd always secretly hoped, watching her, that the ordinary world might remain.

He wrote a brief entry that evening, shorter than most, the words arriving simply.

> *Saw Auntie Zhang again today. She didn't recognize me. I didn't approach. Her granddaughter turns six tonight, and she bought vegetables for the family dinner, and none of it had anything to do with me at all. I think that's exactly why it mattered. The ordinary world doesn't need to know what I'm protecting it from to remain worth protecting.*
