# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter Two Hundred and Thirty-Seven — What She Can Do

It took two weeks for Liang Jingzhi's value to become impossible to overlook, and it arrived not through any cultivation breakthrough — she had none, no Qi sensitivity of any kind, nothing the Codex's framework would have recognized as relevant capability — but through a kind of precision Lin Mushi simply didn't have access to himself.

She approached the Strata's anchor points the way she approached any geological formation: methodically, instrumentally, gathering data first and forming conclusions only once the data supported them.

"Your assessment of anchor stability is qualitative," she told him, on her fourth visit, spreading a set of readings across the table in the workspace he'd built for exactly this kind of collaboration. "You're sensing stress through whatever your Qi perception gives you access to, which is genuinely impressive, but it's not the same as a quantified timeline. I can give you a timeline."

She'd brought portable seismic equipment into the Strata — careful, unobtrusive instruments that measured the anchor points' physical signature against the surrounding rock with a precision his own perception, however refined, simply couldn't match.

Three anchor points, out of the dozen she'd surveyed so far, showed degradation patterns serious enough to flag.

"This one," she said, pointing to a reading from the western library section, "is showing stress consistent with structural fatigue. Not catastrophic, not yet. But the rate of change tells me something useful your perception alone wouldn't — at the current rate of degradation, this anchor reaches critical instability in approximately fourteen months, assuming no intervention and no acceleration in the underlying cause."

He stared at the number, the specificity of it landing with a weight his own qualitative sense of "stressed" or "stable" had never carried.

"Fourteen months. Not six weeks like the ley-line cluster crisis."

"Different mechanism, different timeline. The ley-line nodes you described to me are about Qi flow distribution — pressure building up because routine correction stopped happening. This is about the anchor's actual structural integrity, the connection between the fold and the bedrock it's tethered to. Slower. Less immediately dangerous. But also harder to fix once it starts failing, because you're not redistributing pressure anymore, you're repairing a connection that's actually degrading."

He sat with this, understanding slowly how much he'd been missing without realizing it — not through any failure of his own perception, but simply through the limits of what cultivation theory alone could measure compared to what genuine instrumental precision could add to it.

"This is exactly what I needed and didn't know I was missing," he said.

"That's generally how good instrumentation works. You don't know what you can't see until someone hands you the tool to see it." She made another notation in her field log, the careful, methodical handwriting of someone documenting a discovery she still hadn't fully metabolized was real. "I can give you the same precision for every anchor point you're willing to let me survey. Timelines, degradation rates, comparative risk assessments. The kind of data that turns 'this feels concerning' into 'this needs intervention within eight months, prioritized ahead of the anchor that needs it within sixteen.'"

"You're not a cultivator," he said. "You don't have any of the perception the rest of us are working with. And you're still finding things none of us would have found on our own."

"I'm a geologist," she said, with the particular satisfaction of someone whose entire career had just been validated in a way she could never have anticipated when she'd started it. "Turns out the universe doesn't actually care whether you can sense Qi directly, as long as you bring the right instruments and ask careful questions."

He thought about the maintenance rotation, the careful, exhausting work of eight people covering a workload built for two hundred, and understood, watching her organize her field data into something he could actually use to prioritize the effort properly, that she'd just made every hour of that work meaningfully more effective.

"Welcome to the project," he said. "Properly, this time, not just as someone I'm protecting from a discovery she stumbled into."

She looked up from her notes, something settling into her expression that he recognized — the particular, quiet satisfaction of someone who had spent years collecting careful data nobody fully appreciated, finally finding the place where it mattered as much as she'd always believed it should.

"Good," she said. "Now show me the rest of the anchors. I have a great deal of surveying to do."
