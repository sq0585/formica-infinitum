# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter Two Hundred and Ninety-Five — What Wei Canglan Asks

Wei Canglan asked the question over dinner, without warning, in the particular direct tone he reserved for things he'd clearly been considering for a while before deciding to voice them.

"Can you feel what I'm feeling? Right now?"

Lin Mushi set down his chopsticks, giving the question the consideration it deserved rather than answering reflexively. The three of them — himself, Chen Yufei, Wei Canglan — sat around the small table in the residential wing's common room, the evening's meal mostly finished, the kind of unhurried quiet that had become, over the past several months, one of his favorite parts of the day.

"Some of it," he said honestly. "Not the specifics. More like — texture. Right now you're feeling something close to tension. Not distress, exactly. More like you're bracing for a conversation you've already decided to have and are still working up to actually starting."

Wei Canglan's expression didn't change, but something in his posture confirmed the read before he spoke. "That's accurate."

"What's the tension about?"

"The question itself." Wei Canglan set down his own chopsticks, matching the deliberate care Lin Mushi had shown a moment earlier. "I've been thinking about the Colony Link. About what it means that you can feel this — feel me — without my choosing to show you anything. I wanted to ask directly rather than continuing to wonder privately."

Chen Yufei, who had gone still in the specific way she did when a conversation was heading somewhere that mattered, didn't interrupt.

"It's not a small question," Lin Mushi said. "I don't think I should give you a small answer to it."

"I'd prefer the real one."

He considered how to say it honestly. "The Colony Link doesn't read thoughts. It doesn't show me specifics — what you're thinking about, what memory you're sitting with, anything like that. What it gives me is closer to weather. Emotional texture. Whether someone's calm or tense, whether they're carrying grief or contentment, sometimes how intensely. It's involuntary on your end. You can't turn it off, and I can't fully turn off receiving it, though I can choose how much attention I give it."

"So you always know, at some level, how I'm feeling."

"At some level. Yes."

Wei Canglan was quiet for a moment, processing this with the same careful precision he brought to cultivation technique. "Is that useful to you?"

"Sometimes. It's how I knew to check on you after the apartment situation, before you'd said anything about it. It's part of how I understood Chen Yufei needed space after her Anchor designation, before she'd found words for it herself." He paused, choosing the next part carefully, because it mattered as much as the useful part did. "It's also invasive. I want to be honest about that rather than only defending the parts that sound good. I know things about the people close to me that they haven't chosen to share. That's not a comfortable amount of power to hold, even when I use it carefully."

"Useful," Wei Canglan said slowly. "Invasive."

"Both."

"Both," Wei Canglan repeated, something settling in his voice that sounded, to Lin Mushi's ear, like relief — not at the answer being comfortable, but at the answer being honest.

---

Chen Yufei spoke once the silence had stretched long enough to become its own kind of answer. "Does knowing that change how you feel about it? Practically, I mean — do you want him to be more careful about attention, more deliberate about not reaching for it when he doesn't need to?"

"I've thought about that," Wei Canglan said. "I think — no. Not more careful, exactly. I think I wanted the honesty more than I wanted a change in behavior. If he's going to know how I'm feeling regardless of what either of us decides, I'd rather know that clearly than wonder about it privately every time something shifts in a conversation and I can't explain why."

"That's a generous read of an uncomfortable situation," Lin Mushi said.

"It's not generosity. It's practicality." Wei Canglan picked his chopsticks back up, the conversation's weight easing slightly as he returned to the meal. "I've spent most of my life inside institutions that pretended not to have power over me while using it constantly and quietly. The Court did this. My own family did this, in smaller ways. I find I'd rather work alongside someone who names the power directly and lets me decide how I feel about it, than someone who denies having any."

Lin Mushi felt the comparison land with more weight than a simpler compliment would have — not absolution, exactly, but something adjacent to it, earned through the specific discipline of having asked the hard question directly and received an equally direct answer.

"Thank you for asking," he said. "I mean that. I'd rather have this conversation than have you wonder about it in silence."

"I know," Wei Canglan said. "That's precisely why I asked."

He returned to his meal, the tension Lin Mushi had felt earlier settling, visibly now, into something closer to the easy quiet that usually governed their evenings — the specific, hard-won comfort of three people who had chosen, deliberately and more than once, to keep telling each other the truth even when the truth carried weight.
