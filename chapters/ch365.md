## Chapter 365 — The Surveyor Arrives

Lin Mushi noticed it the way he noticed everything now: as a discrepancy first, a threat only after the discrepancy resolved itself into meaning.

It was 4:12 in the morning, Chengdu time, and he was not asleep. He was rarely fully asleep anymore. Some fraction of him remained awake at Planetary Architect scale even when his body lay still in the dark of his apartment above the noodle shop, monitoring eight hundred and forty Circuit nodes, the Pacific Ring's slow tectonic breathing, the Colony Link's quiet hum of a thousand connected lives. It was not exhausting in the way sleeplessness used to be exhausting. It was simply a new kind of attention, distributed thin across a planet, and he had learned to let his body rest inside it.

The discrepancy appeared in the magnetosphere.

Not a storm. Not a satellite. Something anchored — the word arrived before he had a better one — dimensionally, forty thousand kilometers up, in a region of space that should have contained nothing but solar wind and the ordinary radiation belts. He opened his eyes in the dark room and lay still, running the same measurement three times before he trusted it.

A pocket dimension. Not his. Not Formican, not in any signature he recognized from three years of studying Formican geometry down to its rhythm markers and phase boundaries. The construction was orders of magnitude more sophisticated than anything in the Strata — cleaner, denser, folded with an economy that made his own dimensional weaving look like a child's paper origami beside a cut diamond.

He sat up. Beside him, on the small table where he kept the observation notebook, the pen was already rolling gently toward the table's edge — the apartment's old habit of sloping floors, nothing supernatural about it, and something in him was grateful for the ordinariness of the detail even as he reached out and caught it before it fell.

_Rhythm,_ he thought, the old habit surfacing, _before you understand the what, find the pattern of the when._

The pattern was this: the object had arrived without transition. No approach, no deceleration, no signature of travel through ordinary space at all. It was simply, suddenly, present — the way a word appears on a page when you finally learn to read the alphabet it's written in. One moment absent from his perception. The next, occupying a fixed coordinate with the calm permanence of something that had always intended to be found.

He dressed without waking anyone, made tea he didn't drink, and sat at the window that looked out over rooftops just beginning to go grey with early light.

_Veritonal signature,_ he wrote. _Unlike any Earth-native pattern. Not Formican. Coherence density approximately forty times Strata baseline. No decay, no drift. Holding position._

He underlined _holding position_ twice, then crossed it out. It implied intention he hadn't confirmed yet.

By six, Chen Yufei had felt it too — not the object itself, which sat beyond her own perceptual range, but the change in him, the particular quality of attention that meant something had shifted in the part of his awareness she could reach through the Anchor bond. She came up the narrow stairs still in the clothes she'd slept in and found him at the window with the notebook open and untouched tea gone cold beside it.

"How bad," she said. Not a question shaped like a question. Three years of this had taught her the difference between his ordinary focus and this one.

"I don't know yet." He turned the notebook so she could see the sketch — a rough diagram of the construction, its layered geometry, the coordinate fixed against Earth's rotation like something that had chosen to stay exactly where it was. "It's not hostile. I don't think it's hostile. It's just — precise. Deliberate. Like it was built by something that has done this exact thing many times before and has stopped needing to be careful about it."

"That's not reassuring."

"No," he agreed. "It's the opposite of reassuring. Competence at that scale usually means the thing doing it has already thought through every way I might respond."

She sat down across from him, and for a moment neither of them said anything. Outside, the city was waking up in its ordinary way — a delivery scooter, a shop grate rolling upward two streets over, someone's radio playing something tinny and cheerful through an open window. He found himself listening to it more carefully than usual, the way he sometimes did before something large was about to happen. Cataloguing what the day looked like before it changed, in case he needed to remember.

Meen-4's presence arrived in the Colony Link a few minutes later, wordless at first — a kind of attentive stillness he'd learned to recognize as her equivalent of alarm — and then, through the bond, a single clear thought that was not quite language and not quite feeling.

_The review clock has seven months remaining,_ she sent. _This does not feel like a coincidence._

"No," he agreed out loud, for Chen Yufei's benefit as much as Meen-4's. "It doesn't."

He looked back out at the grey light over the rooftops, at the ordinary scooter now three streets away, at the tea he still hadn't drunk. Forty thousand kilometers up, something built with a precision that made his own best work look provisional sat and waited, holding its position with the patience of an institution rather than a threat.

He closed the notebook, finished the cold tea anyway, and began, quietly, to prepare.
