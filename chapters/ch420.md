# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 420 — Dr. Shen Yue's Next Paper

He found out about the paper the same way most of the world would find out, twelve hours after it happened — not through a private warning, not through careful advance coordination, but through the paper itself, already published, already spreading, by the time Meen-4 flagged the surge in Circuit-adjacent search activity that told her something significant had shifted in the wider world's attention.

Dr. Shen Yue's third paper, published under the Collective's own research affiliation now rather than any university's name, named everything. The Veritonal Energy framework, stated plainly rather than gestured toward through careful academic hedging. The Chitin Circuit technology, described in enough technical detail that any sufficiently motivated reader could begin to understand its function. The pocket-dimension habitation model underlying the Strata City itself, documented with photographs, data, and firsthand testimony from residents who had, apparently, agreed to be named.

He read it in full before he called her, feeling something between admiration and genuine alarm settle into his chest in roughly equal measure.

"You didn't ask permission," he said, once she answered.

"No," Dr. Shen Yue agreed, without a trace of apology in her voice. "I told you after. You should have told me first," he started, and she cut in before he could finish the thought. "You would have asked me to wait."

"Yes," he admitted.

"I've been waiting long enough."

He sat with that, the specific weight of her certainty settling into him alongside his own scattered concerns about timing, about consequence, about the careful, staged disclosure he'd spent months managing precisely to avoid exactly the kind of sudden, unmanaged exposure this paper now represented.

"Fair," he said finally, because it was the honest response, however complicated the situation it left him standing in.

"I want to explain my reasoning," she continued, "not to justify it after the fact, but because I think you deserve to actually understand it, rather than simply accept the outcome without knowing why I chose it. I have spent months inside the Collective's resources, watching the review conclude, watching the classification change, watching every careful, incremental step of the disclosure process you and Fang Ruxue built together. Every step of it was correct. I don't dispute that. But every step of it was also, by design, controlled — shared with institutions, shared with the review body, never simply handed, plainly, to the people whose world this fully documented truth actually belongs to."

"The public."

"The public," she confirmed. "You have spent three years protecting people from a truth you judged too dangerous, too destabilizing, to simply hand them directly. I understood that caution, for most of those three years. I no longer believe it's the right caution to maintain indefinitely. People deserve to know what's actually happening beneath the surface of their own world, and I decided, finally, that I was in a position to be the one who told them, rather than waiting for a moment of readiness that might never arrive on its own."

He thought about that, about the careful staged disclosure he'd designed, about the genuine risk this sudden, unmanaged exposure now represented — panic, misunderstanding, the specific chaos of an entire species learning, all at once, that the world it thought it understood was considerably stranger than it had ever imagined.

He also thought about the fifty-three Strata residents who had chosen to leave rather than live with a truth too large to easily carry, and the eleven hundred who had stayed anyway, and the particular respect he'd learned, over months, for people's capacity to handle hard truths honestly, when given the chance to actually hear them.

"I'm not going to pretend I'm not worried," he said finally. "This is going to be chaotic. Genuinely, seriously chaotic, in ways the careful disclosure was specifically designed to avoid."

"I know," she said. "I've thought about that carefully, and I've decided the chaos is worth it. Controlled disclosure protects institutions. It doesn't necessarily protect people's right to know what's true about their own world, on their own timeline rather than someone else's."

He sat with the finality of it — a decision already made, already published, already spreading faster than any careful containment strategy could now hope to manage.

"Then we'd better prepare for what happens next," he said, "instead of wishing it had happened differently."

"That," Dr. Shen Yue said, "is exactly the response I was hoping for."
