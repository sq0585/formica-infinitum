# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 451 — What the Doctors Say

She agreed to a full checkup mostly, he suspected, to stop him hovering, though she framed it as simple practicality when he raised it the following week. "If it will make you stop making that face every time you look at me," she said, "I'll go. I was overdue anyway."

He didn't go with her into the examination room — some boundaries, even now, remained hers to keep — but he arranged to meet her afterward at the small café near the clinic, and he read her face carefully as she crossed the room toward him, the way he'd learned to read faces before he'd ever learned to read Formican notation.

"Nothing dramatic," she said, sitting down and ordering tea before he could ask. "Which I suspect disappoints you slightly, given how worried you've looked all week."

"It doesn't disappoint me. I just want the actual words, not the summary."

She recited it plainly, the way she recited most things — mild osteoarthritis in both knees, more pronounced than a year ago, nothing unusual for her age. A heart working harder than the doctor would like, not failing, not urgent, but showing the particular strain of seventy-four years of steady use finally beginning to add up in small, cumulative ways. Blood pressure slightly elevated. Nothing that required immediate intervention. Everything that suggested, cumulatively, a body doing exactly what bodies her age did.

"The doctor called it unremarkable," she said, with something that might have been amusement. "I believe her exact words were 'appropriate for your age, nothing acute.' I told her that was the least flattering compliment I'd received all year, and she laughed and told me to keep walking every day and cut back on salt."

He sat with the diagnosis, turning it over the way he turned over any data set that mattered, and found the specific frustration of it settling in his chest before he'd fully articulated why. Nothing here was dangerous, not immediately. Nothing here was the kind of crisis that had, across five years, repeatedly forced his hand into action he hadn't fully chosen. This was simply time, doing to his mother exactly what time did to every human body eventually, patiently, without malice, without urgency, and entirely outside his ability to negotiate with directly.

He could fix it. That was the specific, uncomfortable knowledge sitting underneath everything else he was feeling. He had, at his current understanding, more than enough capability to address the strain on her heart, to ease the wear in her joints, to simply make the ordinary math of seventy-four years stop accumulating against her the way it currently was. He had watched Fang Ruxue's extension unfold three arcs ago, understood its mechanism thoroughly enough by now to know it wasn't beyond his reach to offer the same thing here.

*Rule,* he wrote, later, alone, and found the rule cost him more to hold than almost any he'd written before it. *I cannot simply act. Whatever I offer her, it changes something fundamental about what she is — not merely her health, but her relationship to time itself, to aging, to the specific ordinary mortality she has lived inside her entire life without ever once being asked whether she wanted an alternative. Offering that without her full, informed understanding of what it actually means would not be help. It would be a decision made on her behalf, by someone who has spent five years learning, slowly and at real cost, exactly why that kind of decision belongs to the person living inside the consequences and no one else.*

He thought of Lady Shen, once, offering power without full context to people who hadn't understood what they were accepting. He thought of every antagonist the past five years had taught him to recognize — not through cruelty, usually, but through exactly this kind of well-intentioned overreach, deciding for someone else what they needed before actually asking them.

"You've gone quiet again," his mother said, watching him over her tea with the particular attentiveness that had, he was beginning to suspect, never once actually missed as much as he'd assumed it had.

"Just thinking," he said, which was true, and also, he understood even as he said it, the same kind of careful, loving omission he'd been offering her for four years running.

She didn't press. She never pressed, which was, he was starting to realize, its own quiet form of trust — the specific patience of a woman who had raised him and had long ago decided that whatever he wasn't ready to say, he'd say when he was.

"Finish your tea," she said instead. "And come by Wednesday if you can. The garden needs turning before the weather changes, and you're better at the heavy digging than I am these days."

"I'll come Wednesday," he said, and meant it, though the promise sat oddly alongside everything he was carefully not saying — the specific capability he now understood he possessed, the door it would require opening, the five years of protection he wasn't yet certain he had the courage to finally set down.

He walked her home afterward, unhurried, matching her pace rather than his own, and said nothing further about hearts or knees or anything else the doctor's unremarkable diagnosis had quietly set in motion.

But he carried the question the entire way — not whether he could help her. He understood, clearly, that he could. Only whether he was finally, honestly, ready to tell her everything that helping her would require her to know.
