# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 534 — Dr. Shen Yue's Nobel

He watched the announcement through the Colony Link, the way he watched most things involving people he loved now — not through any screen, though a screen would have worked perfectly well, but through the direct, unmistakable fact of her presence and her feeling arriving to him across whatever distance happened to separate them that particular week.

Dr. Shen Yue received the Nobel Prize in Physics for the Veritonal Energy framework, the culmination of a paper that had once, years ago, been rejected by a skeptical journal and revised, patiently, methodically, until the data itself had become undeniable even to reviewers who had every professional instinct to distrust it.

He remembered the rejection clearly — her characteristic grace in receiving it, Fang Ruxue's calm observation that the delay had bought them time rather than cost it, and he understood now, watching her stand in Stockholm in a formal dress she had complained about for weeks beforehand while quietly, he suspected, being rather pleased with how it looked, that every careful, patient step between that rejection and this moment had been worth exactly what it cost.

Her speech was precise, characteristically hers — she thanked her research team by name, addressed the methodology in enough technical depth that he suspected half the room struggled to follow it, and then, near the very end, in the acknowledgments section every laureate was permitted a brief personal indulgence within, she said something he had not expected, though perhaps he should have.

"To Lin Mushi," she said, her voice steady, unhurried, exactly as measured as it had been through every crisis he'd ever watched her navigate, "who answered a physics forum post with something that took six years to fully understand. Thank you for the data."

He felt something in his chest that was not quite pride and not quite gratitude, some third thing that belonged to both, watching her say his name to a room full of the world's most careful, skeptical scientists and mean it as simply, as honestly, as she meant everything else she'd ever told him.

*Observation 534,* he wrote, later, once the ceremony's formal portion had concluded and she had, by every account reaching him through the Colony Link, finally allowed herself to properly enjoy the evening. *I answered a forum post six years ago because a strange energy signature had appeared in data I happened to have access to, and I was curious, and curiosity had not yet cost me anything I regretted. I did not know, sending that message, that I was handing a career-defining discovery to a graduate student who would spend the next six years turning it into rigorous, undeniable science. I want to record, plainly, that whatever credit anyone eventually assigns to me for anything this novel has built, the actual work — the years of careful data, the patient revisions, the willingness to be publicly wrong until she was finally, undeniably right — belongs entirely to her.*

He felt her presence warm, through the Colony Link, at the entry, though he had not shown it to her directly.

*You're writing about me,* she sent, amused.

*I am.*

*Say something nice,* she sent, *and then delete it before anyone else reads it. I have a reputation for rigor to maintain.*

He laughed, and left the entry exactly as it was, unedited, and felt, closing the notebook, considerably prouder of her than he suspected she would ever let him say to her face without immediately, characteristically, changing the subject to something more technically interesting.

He thought, in the days after the ceremony, about the specific shape of what her prize actually represented — not merely recognition for a single brilliant framework, but the slow, patient vindication of an entire way of doing science: rigorous, careful, willing to be wrong publicly for as long as being wrong took to correct, rather than reaching for a more comfortable, more easily defensible answer. He had watched her practice that discipline for six years, through rejection and doubt and the specific loneliness of holding data no one else yet believed, and understood, watching Stockholm finally confirm what he had known since her earliest careful measurements, that this was exactly the kind of victory that mattered most in a life like the one this arc had been quietly, patiently building toward — not sudden, not dramatic, but earned, slowly, by someone who had never once stopped doing the work properly.

He sent her a message later that week, brief, unceremonious, the kind of note he suspected she would actually appreciate more than any grander tribute. *Proud of you. Go get some sleep.* Her reply arrived within minutes, characteristically dry: *I have a symposium in four hours. Sleep is a theoretical construct at this point.* He smiled, reading it, and let the small ordinary exchange stand as its own quiet celebration.
