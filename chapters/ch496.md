# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 496 — Dr. Shen Yue's Framework

She found him in the monitoring chamber that afternoon, the same dedicated space that had tracked every threshold of the past year, and set down a densely annotated document with the particular quiet pride of someone presenting work she considered the culmination of everything before it.

"I want to walk you through this properly," she said, "before tomorrow, so you understand exactly what I'll be watching and exactly what it means if the numbers move in a direction neither of us wants."

He studied the document's opening pages, the mathematics considerably denser than anything she'd shown him before, built from years of accumulated coherence data — his own Stellar Scale crossing, the dissolution zone's steady repair, three hundred and twenty-three anchor points' worth of careful, verified measurement.

"This is a framework for verifying Formless Scale coherence," she said, "from the outside. I've been building it since the Pelagic Archive reading, honestly, though I didn't fully understand what I was building toward until recently. It gives me a way to observe, from here, from Earth, whether you've maintained individual identity throughout whatever happens tomorrow, without needing to rely on your own reporting alone."

"Why does that matter, if I can just tell you myself?"

"Because Veth-Karun couldn't," she said plainly, and he felt the specific weight of the comparison land exactly as she'd intended it to. "The account is clear about this, if you read it carefully enough — the dissolution happened gradually enough that self-report remained possible for most of it, right up until the final moments where self-report became, definitionally, impossible. I don't want to be relying on your own testimony as the only signal, in the exact scenario where testimony might fail before either of us notices it's failing."

He nodded slowly, understanding the specific, careful wisdom of it — the same instinct that had built the Anchor Protocol years before he understood why it mattered, now extended into instrumentation precise enough to catch a failure before the failing mind could report it.

"Walk me through the mechanism," he said.

"Coherence signature," she said, pulling up the framework's core model. "I've established your baseline across every previous threshold — Colony Link, Planetary Architect, Stellar Scale. Each crossing has a distinct signature, a specific mathematical shape to how your identity structure holds itself together even as the perceptual scale around it expands. I can measure that signature remotely now, through the relay network, continuously, without requiring you to actively report anything."

"And Formless Scale?"

"I don't have direct prior data for Formless Scale specifically — no one alive does, which is rather the entire difficulty we're solving for. But I have Veth-Karun's account, and I have five years of your own accumulated signature data, and I've built a threshold model from both." She traced the graph's central line carefully. "Above this threshold, the coherence signature indicates a maintained, located, individual identity — you, specifically, however vast the perceptual scale around you becomes. Below it — " she paused, the professional composure carrying just the faintest crack — "below it, something has gone wrong. The Anchor Protocol has failed to hold, or the vastness has exceeded what it was built to manage."

"And if that happens? What can any of you actually do?"

"Honestly, I don't fully know," she admitted. "This isn't a rescue mechanism. I want to be clear about that, so you don't go into tomorrow with false confidence about a safety net that might not actually exist. This is a warning system. It tells us, as early and as clearly as data can tell us, whether something has started going wrong. What happens after that warning — Meen-4 and I have theories, but none of them are tested, because nothing about tomorrow has ever been tested before."

He sat with that honesty, grateful for it in the specific way he was always grateful for people who refused to comfort him with false certainty. "Thank you for building this anyway. Even without a guaranteed rescue on the other end of the warning."

"I couldn't not build it," she said simply. "Watching helplessly was never going to be acceptable to me, whether or not the watching came with guarantees attached. I'd rather have imperfect information than none at all. I suspect, knowing you, you'd say the same thing."

"I would," he admitted, managing a small, genuine smile despite the weight of the conversation. "It's very close to something I'd have written in the notebook myself."

"I know," she said, gathering the document with the same brisk efficiency she brought to everything. "Five years of working alongside you does that to a person's thinking, apparently."

He watched her prepare the final calibrations, the monitoring chamber humming with the particular readiness of instruments about to witness something considerably larger than anything they'd measured before, and felt, beneath the fear he hadn't fully stopped carrying since Axis-7's cryptic visit the night before, a genuine and steady gratitude for every careful, rigorous person who had spent the past year making tomorrow as survivable as five years of collective preparation could possibly make it.
