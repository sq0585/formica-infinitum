# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 426 — The Isolation Option — Revisited

The proposal came from a faction of Formican Isolationists Lin Mushi had known existed but had rarely engaged with directly — older residents of the Strata, some who had lived through the original empire's collapse, who had watched the surface world's disclosure unfold over the past weeks with a mounting unease that finally crystallized, at the council's next formal session, into a concrete argument.

Axis-7 presented their case, carefully and without editorializing, the particular neutral thoroughness of someone determined to represent a position fairly regardless of her own private reconsideration.

"Some among us argue the Strata should seal itself," Axis-7 said, addressing the full council, Lin Mushi seated among them rather than presiding over them, the way he'd insisted the Strata's governance structure operate since its earliest days. "Now that the surface knows, some believe our safety requires withdrawing entirely — sealing every accessible entry point, retreating fully into pocket-dimension space no surface technology could locate or reach, and severing the tentative contact currently being established with surface governments."

The argument that followed was more thorough than Lin Mushi had expected, and he found himself, listening, genuinely respecting its internal logic even where he disagreed with its conclusion. Isolation, its proponents argued, had preserved surviving Formican communities for four hundred thousand years, across an empire's collapse and generations of scattered, careful hiding. Visibility, however carefully managed, introduced a category of risk that isolation had always successfully avoided — governments that might change their minds, populations whose fear might curdle into something more dangerous than curiosity, a world that had only just begun learning to trust something it had known about for mere weeks.

He listened to the full case, all of it, without interrupting, the same discipline he'd learned across three years of taking seriously arguments he ultimately intended to disagree with.

When Axis-7 finished, the chamber fell quiet, waiting, Lin Mushi understood, for his response — not because he held formal authority over the council's decision, but because everyone present understood, correctly, that his perspective carried particular weight given everything he'd personally built and risked to reach this point.

"This option was available thirty-six months ago," he said finally, choosing his words carefully. "Before the review. Before the disclosure. We could have sealed the Strata then, retreated fully, avoided the entire risk the Surveyor's arrival represented. We chose differently. We chose to be seen, honestly, by something with the power to end us, because we believed being seen accurately mattered more than the safety of remaining hidden."

He looked around the chamber, meeting the eyes of the residents present, aware of the particular weight this moment carried for people who had, many of them, survived exactly the kind of catastrophic exposure the Isolationists now feared repeating.

"We should decide consistently," he continued. "If isolation was the wrong choice against a body powerful enough to sterilize this entire zone, I don't think it becomes the right choice now, against a surface world that, whatever its flaws, cannot remotely match that same existential threat. I understand the fear. I don't dismiss it. But I think consistency matters here, more than comfort does. We chose visibility when the stakes were genuinely, catastrophically high. I don't think we should abandon that choice now, simply because the current stakes, while real, are considerably smaller."

The debate continued for another hour after that, the Isolationist faction pressing their concerns further, other council members offering their own perspectives, the full weight of a genuinely difficult decision worked through carefully rather than settled by a single decisive statement. But Lin Mushi felt, watching the discussion unfold, something shift gradually in the room's overall temperature — not full agreement, he didn't expect that, but a growing recognition, even among those most sympathetic to the Isolationist position, that the consistency argument carried real weight.

The council's eventual decision, reached by the session's end, was not unanimous. But it held, by a clear majority, against sealing the Strata — a continuation, deliberate and considered, of the same path they'd chosen months earlier, when the stakes had been considerably higher and the choice considerably harder.

"You did not force that outcome," Axis-7 observed afterward, finding Lin Mushi alone in the emptying chamber. "You could have. Your standing here would have carried the vote regardless of the arguments offered against you."

"I didn't want to force it," Lin Mushi said. "I wanted it decided honestly, by people who actually weighed both sides. If the Strata is going to keep choosing visibility, I want that choice to keep being ours, together, not something I simply impose because I happen to carry more weight in the room."

Axis-7 studied him for a long moment, something that might have been approval settling into the ancient, deliberate stillness of her posture. "That," the old Formican said finally, "is precisely the difference between the empire that produced Veth-Karun and whatever it is we are still, carefully, becoming."
