# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter One Hundred and Seventy-Eight — What the Courts Are For

He sat with the question late one night, after Wei Canglan had gone to sleep on the apartment's couch and Chen Yufei had gone home, and the city outside had settled into its quietest hour.

He had been avoiding it, he realized, in the specific way you avoid a question whose honest answer complicates something you'd rather keep simple. The simple version of his position had been: I share true things, institutions respond as they will, the consequences are theirs to manage. This had been functionally true and had let him act without the paralysis of trying to control outcomes he genuinely couldn't control.

But sitting with Wei Canglan's situation — his parents in a single room, his mother working at fifty-three — and with the three Iron Meridian practitioners quietly losing access for the crime of confirming the truth, and with however many more such costs were accumulating in places he couldn't see, the simple version felt increasingly insufficient.

He opened his notebook.

> *What the Courts are for: I have been treating this question as secondary to 'are the Courts built on accurate foundations,' and the second question has occupied almost all my attention. But the first question matters independently.*
>
> *The Iron Meridian Court trains practitioners. It provides structure — a path from novice to mastery, with teachers and resources and a community that holds people accountable to genuine standards. It resolves disputes between practitioners that would otherwise have no neutral arbiter. It protects vulnerable practitioners — newer, less powerful ones — from being exploited or harmed by more capable ones, through its authority and its willingness to enforce consequences.*
>
> *Wei Canglan's situation makes this concrete: before his defection, the Northern Peak Court provided his family stability — a home, social standing, a clear path for his training. Imperfect, built on flawed technique, but real and functioning. I corrected the technique. I did not provide an alternative source of the stability the correction cost him.*
>
> *If I dismantle the Courts' authority — which is, functionally, what releasing accurate information that undermines their foundational legitimacy does — without providing what they provide, the result is worse than what existed before. Not because the Courts deserved to be protected from correction. Because the people who depend on the structures the Courts provide don't automatically get something better when the structure breaks. They sometimes just get nothing.*
>
> *This is the problem I haven't solved.*
>
> *I have been thinking about the document releases as acts of honesty — and they are that. I have not been thinking carefully enough about what comes after honesty: what structure, if any, should exist to do the things the Courts were doing badly but were nonetheless doing. Training. Dispute resolution. Protection for the vulnerable. A path forward for someone who wants to learn this discipline and doesn't know where to start.*
>
> *Lady Shen's analysis — the power vacuum — was about authority and political position. This is a related but distinct question: not who fills the vacuum of legitimacy, but who does the actual work the legitimate structures were supposed to be doing.*
>
> *I don't have an answer yet. I think the answer probably isn't 'I personally become the new central authority' — that recreates the same single-point-of-failure problem the old Courts had, just with better information at the center. I think it's probably something more distributed. But I don't know its shape yet.*
>
> *I need to think about this more carefully than I have been. The information war has been going well, by most measures. The structural question underneath it has been neglected.*

He read this back.

He thought about the assembly he'd been loosely imagining — the curious recipients, the Wei Canglans and Lin Wenqings of the world, gathering around the corrected information not as subjects of a new authority but as something else, something he hadn't yet found the right word for.

He thought he might find the word soon. The shape of it was becoming clearer the longer he sat with the question honestly, rather than letting himself be satisfied with the simple version.

He closed the notebook, eventually, and went to check on Wei Canglan, asleep on the couch, the first person whose concrete cost he'd watched arrive in real time rather than understand abstractly.

He stood in the doorway for a moment.

Then he went to bed himself, the unanswered question sitting with him into sleep.
