# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter Two Hundred and Thirty-Six — The Geologist Enters the Strata

She crossed the threshold the way she seemed to do most things — with careful, focused attention rather than visible fear, the particular composure of someone whose professional life had trained her to encounter the unexpected and immediately start cataloguing it rather than reacting to it.

She stood very still in the outer hall for almost a full minute, her eyes moving methodically across the scale of the space — the shelving running past any reasonable architectural limit, the ceiling lost in amber light with no visible source, the specific, undeniable wrongness of standing somewhere that occupied vastly more volume than the modest convenience store above it could possibly contain.

"This space is significantly smaller on the outside than the inside," she said finally.

"Yes."

She nodded slowly, processing this with the same careful attention she'd brought to the readout in the coffee shop.

"That's not possible by any physics I understand."

"It's not possible by the physics most people understand. It's possible by physics that predates recorded human history and that almost no one currently alive has the means to study properly." He watched her absorb this. "You're one of the only people who's ever crossed this threshold with the specific scientific training to actually understand what you're looking at, rather than just experiencing wonder at it."

She sat down on the nearest bench, slowly, her composure holding but visibly working harder to hold than it had in the coffee shop.

"Okay," she said. "Give me a minute."

He gave her several. He'd learned, by now, that this particular silence — the silence of someone's entire framework for reality undergoing rapid, careful reconstruction — deserved patience rather than narration. He sat a respectful distance away and waited, the way he wished someone had been able to wait for him, two years ago, in the days after he'd first understood what the convenience store's northwest corner actually contained.

"The density signature I detected," she said eventually, still seated, working through it out loud. "It's not generated by this space existing. It's generated by something happening at the boundary — the place where this space connects to the world I came from. Some kind of anchor."

"That's essentially correct."

"And there are more of them. Eleven, by my own data, just in western Sichuan." She looked up at him. "Are they all like this? Spaces like this one, hidden beneath ordinary ground?"

"Most of them. Some are smaller. Some are larger than this one, by a significant margin. All of them are anchored to the surface the same way this one is, and all of them are vulnerable to the same kind of stress if the anchors aren't maintained properly."

She absorbed this, the scientist in her visibly working faster now than the part of her still adjusting to the simple fact of where she was sitting.

"That's why the signal's been drifting," she said slowly. "Not constant. Increasing, over the months I've been collecting data. Something's been going wrong with the maintenance."

He felt something settle in his chest — not surprise, exactly, but the particular weight of watching someone independently arrive, through nothing but careful observation and honest reasoning, at a conclusion that had taken him considerably longer and considerably more privileged access to reach.

"Yes," he said. "That's exactly what's been happening. And it's the reason I needed to find you before anyone else did."

She looked around the hall once more, the scale of it, the impossible amber distance, and then back at him, something settling into her expression that looked less like fear and more like the particular hunger of a scientist who had just been handed the single most significant dataset of her entire career.

"I have a great many questions," she said.

"I expected you might."

"And I assume most of the answers are going to be considerably more complicated than anything I was trained to handle in a geology program."

"Probably," he said honestly. "But I think you're going to handle them better than most people would."

She nodded, slowly, the careful, methodical scientist settling into something that looked, for the first time since she'd crossed the threshold, almost like determination.

"Then I suppose," she said, echoing a phrase he was beginning to hear from nearly everyone who crossed into this world properly, "we should start somewhere specific. Show me the nearest anchor point. I want to see what I've actually been measuring."
