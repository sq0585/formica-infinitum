# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 457 — The Consent Protocol

She asked him to come back the following afternoon, and to bring Meen-4 if that was possible, because she wanted, she said, "the person who actually built the thing, not just the person explaining it secondhand."

Meen-4 agreed without hesitation, and the two days that followed became, in Lin Mushi's own private accounting afterward, some of the most demanding conversations he had witnessed in five years of watching difficult things get explained to people meeting them for the first time.

His mother sat at her own kitchen table, notebook of her own borrowed from a drawer she'd apparently kept stocked with them for decades, and worked through the protocol the way she'd once worked through household budgets — methodically, skeptically, refusing to let a single unclear point pass simply because the larger conversation was moving quickly.

"You said the recalibration targets cellular maintenance processes," she said, partway through the first afternoon, looking up from her notes. "Does that mean my cells are replaced, or repaired? Those aren't the same thing, and I'd like to know which one you're describing."

Meen-4, Lin Mushi noticed, paused before answering — a small, genuine pause, the particular hesitation she reserved for questions that actually required her full attention rather than a rehearsed response. "Repaired," she said. "The existing cellular structure is guided back toward more efficient function. Nothing is replaced wholesale. You would remain, in every literal sense, entirely yourself, simply operating with considerably less accumulated wear."

"And if something goes wrong partway through — can it be stopped? Reversed?"

"In the small number of documented complications, yes. The protocol is monitored continuously specifically to allow for that."

"How small a number."

"Historically, roughly one case in several hundred requires intervention," Meen-4 said, without softening it. "I would rather you have the honest figure than a comfortable one."

His mother wrote the number down, underlined it, and moved to her next question without any visible alarm, the specific steady practicality of a woman deciding what she needed to know rather than what she wanted to hear.

By the second afternoon, her questions had moved from mechanism to consequence — not what the protocol did, but what living afterward with its results would actually mean.

"If I live considerably longer than I would have," she asked, "do I keep aging normally after the extension runs its course, or does something else happen at the end of it?"

"You age normally," Meen-4 answered. "The extension is not indefinite. It provides several additional decades of the health you're accepting today, and afterward, your body resumes its ordinary course, simply from a considerably later starting point than it would otherwise have reached."

"So I don't become something else. I simply get more time as exactly what I already am."

"Precisely."

She nodded, satisfied, and turned to Lin Mushi directly. "And you — if I do this, does it change anything between us? Do I become part of whatever the Collective is, obligated to whatever rules govern all of this?"

"No," he said. "This is a medical choice, not a political one. Nothing about accepting it obligates you to anything beyond what you already choose to be part of, which is currently exactly as much or as little as you'd like."

"Good," she said, closing her notebook decisively. "I have what I need for now. I'd like some time alone with all of this before I decide anything further."

"Take as long as you need," he said again, and meant it, watching her rise from the table with the same brisk practicality she'd carried through every difficult decision he could remember her making across his entire life.

She took two weeks.

He didn't press her during them, though the waiting cost him more than he let himself show — visiting for their ordinary Sunday meal exactly as scheduled, talking about the noodle shop and the garden and nothing at all about the decision quietly working itself out somewhere behind her careful, composed exterior. She worked in the garden most afternoons, he learned from Xiao Dong, turning soil that didn't strictly need turning yet, the particular physical labor she'd always reached for whenever a decision required more time than words alone could settle.

On the fifteenth day, she called him, her voice carrying a steadiness that told him, before she'd said a single word, that the waiting was finally over.

"Come by tonight," she said. "I've decided."
