# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter Two Hundred and Twenty-Four — Lady Shen's Second Visit

She came back two weeks after the conversation about institutional relevance, and this time she brought something.

A scroll case, old enough that the lacquer had worn through to bare wood along its seams, carried with the particular careful attention of someone handling an object that mattered more than its physical condition suggested it should.

"Six hundred years old," she said, setting it on the table between them without preamble. "Northern Peak's internal archive. Most members never see it. I had to invoke privileges I'm not entirely certain I still hold to retrieve it."

He opened the case carefully and unrolled the scroll inside, the brittle paper handled with the same caution she'd shown carrying it.

It was a patriarch's personal record — a journal, by the style of the entries, written by someone clearly senior within the Court's early history. He read through the careful, formal brushwork slowly, translating as he went, and understood the shape of what he was reading perhaps two-thirds of the way through.

The patriarch had studied, for several years, at what he called only *the underground library* — careful, vague language, the kind a person used when they were documenting something they didn't intend for casual readers to fully understand. He had learned techniques there. He had returned to the surface and presented those techniques to his peers as the product of his own enlightenment, a breakthrough achieved through personal cultivation rather than borrowed knowledge.

Northern Peak's foundational technique, the one every disciple since had learned as the Court's original contribution to cultivation theory, had been built on exactly this reattribution.

"This," Lady Shen said, watching his expression as he finished reading, "is what my tradition is built on."

He set the scroll down carefully.

"You found this when?"

"Three days ago. I went looking, after our last conversation, because I wanted to understand exactly how deep the borrowing went before I committed to building anything new on top of it." Her voice was steady, but something underneath it carried a weight he hadn't heard from her before — not the careful political composure she usually wore, but something closer to genuine exposure. "I could have kept this private. No one outside the highest archive tier knows it exists. I'm choosing to show you instead."

He understood, slowly, what the choice actually cost her. This wasn't evidence she was handing him to win an argument or secure a position. This was the worst thing she knew about her own tradition, offered without leverage attached, to someone who had every reason in the world to use it against her if he chose to.

"Why show me this instead of keeping it buried?" he asked.

"Because you're building something that requires honesty as its foundation, and I've decided I'd rather participate in that honestly than continue protecting an institution built on a six-hundred-year-old lie I now know about and could no longer pretend I didn't." She met his eyes directly. "If I'm asking to help build the replacement, I don't get to keep curating which parts of the old structure I'm willing to let you see."

He sat with the weight of what she'd just done — the scroll, the admission, the careful, deliberate vulnerability of someone who had spent thirty years being unassailable choosing, now, to be assailable in front of him on purpose.

He didn't ask what she wanted to do with it. He found, watching her, that the more honest question was different.

"What do you want to do with this?" he asked.

She was quiet for a long moment, the first genuine hesitation he'd seen from her since the conversation about relevance.

"I don't know," she said finally. "I haven't decided. Part of me wants to make it public, the same way you made your own discoveries public — let the Court see exactly what its foundation actually is, let people choose with full information instead of the curated mythology they've been given. Part of me is afraid of what that does to thirty years of people's faith, mine included, all at once, with no structure ready to catch them when it falls."

"Keep it for now," he said. "When you know what to do with it, I'll help you do it well, whatever shape that ends up taking. But I don't think this needs to be decided today, under pressure, just because you happened to find it three days ago."

Something in her posture eased, fractionally, the particular relief of someone who had braced for one response and received a gentler one instead.

"You're not what I expected," she said.

"What did you expect?"

"Someone more interested in being right," she said. "I handed you a weapon and you handed it back to me instead of using it."

"It was never a weapon," he said. "It's a wound. You don't use a wound. You help it heal, carefully, in whatever time it actually needs."

She looked at him for a long moment, something shifting behind her careful expression that he didn't try to name out loud, and reached, finally, to roll the scroll back into its case with the same deliberate care she'd unrolled it with.

"Thank you," she said, the words simple, unadorned, more honest than anything she'd said to him in all the months he'd known her.

"You're welcome," he said, and meant it.
