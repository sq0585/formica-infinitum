# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter Two Hundred and Seventy-Eight — The Scientific Bridge

The second session ran longer than the first, the two of them working through her dark energy dataset with the particular focused intensity of two careful minds finally given the right shared vocabulary to actually communicate through.

He'd brought a portable Circuit reader this time, careful to frame it as a "specialized geological instrument" for the benefit of anyone who might glance into her office, and let her watch its live readout against the cosmic-scale anomalies in her existing data.

"The correlation's exact," she said, working through the overlay with growing excitement. "Not approximate. Exact, down to the resolution limits of both instruments." She looked up at him. "Your device is measuring the same phenomenon my telescope array is measuring, at a completely different scale, and getting consistent results."

"That's roughly the point. Veritonal Energy doesn't change its fundamental behavior based on scale — the same structure that runs through this room runs through galactic-scale dark energy distribution. What changes is which instruments are sensitive enough to detect it at any given scale."

She spent the next hour building a new model from scratch, working through the implications with the particular ruthless efficiency of someone who had no emotional investment in preserving the framework she'd spent three years building, only in arriving at whatever framework actually matched reality.

He watched her work, offering correction exactly where her instincts diverged from what he understood of the underlying structure, and found, watching the model take shape, a particular satisfaction he hadn't fully anticipated — not the satisfaction of having the answer, which he'd had for years now, but the satisfaction of watching someone else's genuine expertise meet his own understanding and produce something neither of them could have built alone.

"This is going to require rewriting several papers I've already published," she said, somewhere past the hour mark, not looking up from her work.

"I imagine it will."

"I'm not bothered by that, for what it's worth. I'd rather have published papers that turn out to be incomplete than never have published at all out of excessive caution. Science progresses through exactly this kind of correction."

"That's a healthier relationship to being wrong than most people manage."

"I've had practice," she said, something dry entering her voice. "Theoretical physics involves being wrong rather a lot, if you're doing it honestly. The trick is treating each correction as data rather than as a referendum on your worth as a scientist."

He thought of his own notebook, the careful documentation of every failed Weaving attempt, every wrong assumption corrected through patient iteration, and recognized in her something genuinely familiar — the same discipline he'd built for himself across two years of impossible learning, arrived at independently, through an entirely different path.

By the end of the second session, she'd rebuilt her core model almost entirely, the new framework holding together with a coherence her original three years of work had only approximated.

"This implies the universe has more structure than we thought," she said again, the same observation from their first conversation, except now it carried the weight of genuine, internalized understanding rather than initial shock.

"Yes."

"A lot more structure."

"Yes," he said again, and found, watching her sit with the full implication of it now properly absorbed rather than simply stated, that he trusted her completely — not because of any test he'd consciously designed, but because every hour of careful, honest work across these two sessions had demonstrated, more clearly than any deliberate evaluation could have, exactly the kind of mind and character he'd learned to recognize as worth bringing fully into this strange, expanding world.

"I think," he said carefully, "there's more I should show you. Not data. The actual source."

She set down her pen, the focused intensity in her expression sharpening into something he recognized immediately — the particular hunger of a scientist being offered access to the single most significant discovery of her entire career.

"Show me," she said.

"Not today," he said. "This requires more preparation, and more conversation with people whose judgment I trust, before I bring anyone new in fully. But soon. I think you've more than earned it."

She nodded, accepting the careful pacing without argument, and he left her office that evening with the particular satisfaction of having found, in an ordinary, cluttered room above an ordinary university hallway, exactly the kind of bridge between his hidden world and the surface science that had been quietly waiting, for three patient years, to finally be built.
