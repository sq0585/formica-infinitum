# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter One Hundred and Forty-One — The Negotiation

The Northern Peak Court's formal response arrived through Elder Kuo on a Thursday.

Not a letter, not an official communication — a meeting request. Kuo, in Chengdu for the past week following his Council session, wanted to see Lin Mushi at his earliest convenience. He phrased it with the specific restraint of someone who had been told to find out what was available and was accurately conveying the situation's urgency without overstating it.

Lin Mushi chose the tea house on Wulin Street, where he had installed a third-generation Chitin Circuit in the decorative planter near the entrance.

---

Kuo arrived on time, without visible support. This was accurate — his circuit network showed no cultivation signatures in the vicinity that weren't accounted for. He was, as represented, alone.

He ordered tea without looking at the menu, which was the behavior of someone who had spent enough time in unfamiliar tea houses to have a default.

"The Council session," he said.

"Tell me."

"Contested, as I predicted. The senior body is divided approximately sixty-forty." He paused. "Sixty percent consider the document a significant challenge to Court authority and want a coordinated response across the five Courts. Forty percent consider it accurate information that the Courts should engage with constructively."

"And the sixty percent's definition of 'coordinated response'?"

"Varied. The most aggressive faction wants to pursue access to the archive directly — bypass your involvement. The more moderate position is to negotiate for shared access." He looked at his tea. "Lady Shen's recent activities are known. The Northern Peak Court is aware she is in Chengdu, operating independently, and is no longer confident that her assessment of the situation matches their institutional interests."

"Because she's working toward cooperation rather than acquisition."

"Yes."

"What does the forty percent want?"

Kuo was quiet for a moment. "To understand what the document says fully. To revise their cultivation framework accordingly. To build a relationship with the archive that serves their practitioners." He met Lin Mushi's gaze. "I am in the forty percent."

"I know," Lin Mushi said. "You've been in the forty percent since you came to see Chen Yufei."

Kuo looked at him. "How do you know that?"

"You told her to arrange a meeting. Not to compile information. Not to secure cooperation. To arrange a meeting. That's what someone does when they think a conversation is possible and worth having." He paused. "The sixty percent would have moved directly to acquisition after the meeting failed."

Kuo was quiet.

"What do you want from me?" Lin Mushi asked.

"The Council wants a formal channel. Not a deniable informal arrangement — they're aware you have one with Chen Yufei, and they respect what it's produced, but they want something they can present to the other Courts as a legitimate engagement." He paused. "A formal statement of your relationship to the archive, your intentions regarding information sharing, and a mechanism for ongoing communication."

He thought about this.

"The archive's relationship to the Courts is not mine to formalize," he said. "I have access to the archive. The archive's governance is the Formican community's. Any formal arrangement would need to be between the Courts and the Formicans, not between the Courts and me."

Kuo blinked. "The Formicans are available for diplomatic engagement?"

"The Layer 3 community communicates through the archive station's Keeper function. I'm developing the communication capability to bridge between human language and the Formicans' register." He paused. "In four to six months, I should be able to facilitate structured exchanges."

A long silence.

"You're proposing to act as translator," Kuo said.

"I'm describing what I can do. What the Courts do with that capability is their decision." He met Kuo's gaze. "What I won't do is represent the archive or the Formican community as if they're mine to represent. I'm a practitioner with access. The community is the authority."

Kuo was quiet for a long time.

"This is not the conversation I was sent to have," he said.

"I know."

"The Council expects to negotiate with you."

"I know. You can tell them that the appropriate negotiating party is not me, and that I will facilitate contact with the appropriate party when I am able to." He paused. "In the meantime: the document contains accurate information. Any practitioner in any Court who applies the corrected techniques will see improved results. The improvements are self-evident. The Courts don't need me to decide to benefit from the information — they just need to read it carefully."

Kuo looked at his tea.

He looked like a man who had arrived expecting one conversation and was processing that he'd received a different, more complicated, and ultimately more significant one.

"The forty percent," he said.

"Yes."

"They'll receive this well. The sixty percent will not."

"Probably."

"What do you suggest I tell the Council?"

He thought about it carefully.

"Tell them the truth," he said. "That the archive exists, is maintained by an active Formican community, and operates on its own terms. That access to it is possible and is expanding. That the document is accurate and the techniques in it work." He paused. "And that the most productive approach is to send practitioners who are genuinely curious rather than practitioners who are looking for leverage. Curious practitioners get access. Practitioners looking for leverage find that the archive is less accommodating than they expected."

Kuo looked at him.

"You're twenty-two," he said. Not for the first time. But differently.

"Yes," Lin Mushi said.

Kuo finished his tea.

He stood.

"I'll report what you've said accurately," he said. "I can't predict what the Council will decide."

"I know." Lin Mushi stood too. "That's all I'm asking."

Kuo left.

Lin Mushi sat back down and checked the circuit network. Clear. He had been certain of this but it was good to confirm.

He ordered a second cup of tea.

He had told the Northern Peak Court's representative that the archive was not his to negotiate over. He had also, functionally, negotiated with the Northern Peak Court.

These were not contradictory. They were the same thing, from different angles.

He was, he noted, more comfortable with this kind of multi-angle thinking than he had been a year ago.

Practice made it natural.

He went home and wrote the report for Axis-7's benefit — a summary of the exchange for the archive's cross-layer communication record, which she had asked him to maintain since the document release.

He was the archive's human-language note-taker, apparently.

He was comfortable with this too.
