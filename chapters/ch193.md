# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter One Hundred and Ninety-Three — The Injury

Lin Mushi found Chen Yufei in the outer hall the following morning, sitting against the wall near the threshold, pale in a way that registered immediately as wrong.

He had left her briefly the night before to make a final round of the territory, confirming the Strata's stability after the confrontation, checking with Axis-7 about the collective signal and what, if anything, it required of him going forward. He had assumed Chen Yufei was resting, recovering from the adrenaline of the previous hours.

She had not been resting.

"How long have you been like this?" he said, crossing the hall quickly and crouching beside her.

"A while," she said, her voice carrying a thinness that alarmed him more than the words themselves. "I'm fine. I just need a minute."

He reached for her wrist, the same diagnostic gesture he'd used dozens of times over the past months, and felt immediately what was wrong: her Qi circulation, normally clean and well-routed since the fire meridian correction, was disrupted — not blocked exactly, but strained, running ragged through channels that should have been smooth.

"The interference field," he said slowly, understanding arriving with the specific guilt of recognizing a cost too late. "You pushed past your limit."

"It needed to work," she said simply. "I didn't have time to calibrate it carefully. I just needed it to disrupt her technique long enough."

"You could have told me you were straining yourself."

"There wasn't time," she said again, with a flash of the directness that had been part of her from the beginning. "And it worked. That's what mattered in the moment."

He sat with this — the specific, uncomfortable recognition that she had absorbed a cost, deliberately and silently, to buy him the seconds he'd needed, and had said nothing about it because saying something would have meant pausing in a moment that couldn't afford a pause. He thought, briefly and unwillingly, of Wei Canglan's family — another cost absorbed quietly, on his behalf, by someone who had decided not to make him carry the knowledge of it in real time. He was beginning to recognize a pattern in the people who chose to stand near him, and he did not entirely like what the pattern said about what proximity to him seemed to cost.

"I should have checked on you sooner," he said. "I went to do a final round of the territory instead of coming to find you first. That was the wrong order."

"You were managing a genuine crisis," she said, some of her usual dryness returning despite the visible strain. "I don't expect you to triage perfectly in the middle of one."

"I'd like to anyway," he said. "Going forward."

She looked at him for a moment, something in her expression softening slightly at that, before the exhaustion reasserted itself.

"How bad is it?" he asked.

"Not dangerous," she said. "Painful. My channels need time to settle back into correct routing. A few days, probably, with rest and care."

He helped her up, slowly, and walked her — supporting more of her weight than she would admit to needing — back through the threshold and to her apartment.

He did not say *thank you* in the moment. It felt insufficient for what she'd actually done, and he suspected, correctly, that she would have waved it away if he'd offered it too quickly, too lightly.

What he said, once she was settled on her couch, was: "I'm staying. For the next few days. I want to make sure you're actually recovering, not just telling me you are."

She looked at him for a long moment.

"You don't have to do that," she said.

"I know," he said. "I'm doing it anyway."

She didn't argue further.

He made her tea — properly this time, paying attention to the steeping in a way he usually didn't bother with — and sat with her through the rest of the morning, the adrenaline of the previous night settling slowly into something quieter and more grounded: the simple, unglamorous work of taking care of someone who had taken care of him first.
