# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter Two Hundred and Forty-Eight — The Iron Meridian Fractures

The fallout, when it arrived, arrived through channels Lin Mushi hadn't anticipated and couldn't have engineered if he'd tried.

The demolition crew, working without any awareness of what actually lay beneath the parking structure they'd been hired to clear, had damaged twelve parked cars in the surrounding lot during the night's work — structural debris falling further than the permitted blast radius accounted for, a mundane, ordinary kind of negligence that had nothing to do with dimensional architecture and everything to do with a rushed, secretive job executed without proper public notice.

The city investigation that followed was swift and entirely conventional.

Chen Yufei brought him the news three days later, the careful precision of her network having traced the unraveling thread back to its source faster than he'd expected.

"The construction company's ownership came apart under scrutiny," she said. "Once investigators started pulling at the shell layers to figure out who'd authorized an unpermitted night demolition with inadequate safety barriers, it didn't take long to reach Iron Meridian's senior leadership."

"Publicly?"

"Publicly enough. Not the full cultivation context, obviously — the news coverage thinks it's a straightforward story about corporate negligence and falsified permits. But within the cultivation world, everyone who matters knows exactly what actually happened and why."

He absorbed this, understanding slowly the shape of what an institution's last desperate gambit had actually cost it.

"How bad is the damage to them?"

"Catastrophic, by every report I'm getting. Sixty percent of the Court's general membership has resigned in the past three days alone — not defected to a rival Court, simply walked away entirely, unwilling to remain associated with an institution that had ordered a reckless, secretive demolition that endangered ordinary people's property for reasons most of the membership never knew about and wouldn't have sanctioned if they had."

"Sixty percent."

"Sixty percent, and climbing, by my contact's estimate. Several of the resigning members have already reached out to the Collective directly, asking about working group participation." Chen Yufei studied him for a moment, the careful weighing he'd learned to recognize when she was deciding how much of her own reaction to share. "This isn't a victory you should feel proud of, exactly. A lot of ordinary practitioners just lost the institution they'd built their lives around, through no real fault of their own."

"I don't feel proud of it," he said honestly. "I feel something closer to vindicated and uneasy at the same time. They tried to destroy something I'd spent two years protecting, and the consequence is genuinely their own making — their leadership's choice, not anything I did to them directly. But sixty percent of ordinary people losing their professional and spiritual home overnight isn't something I get to celebrate just because the institution deserved the consequence."

"That's a fair distinction."

He thought about Wei Canglan, two years ago, losing everything for a choice that had been entirely his own, entirely justified, and entirely costly regardless of how right it had been. He thought about the particular cruelty of institutions that collapsed not from any single dramatic confrontation, but from their own leadership's choices landing, finally, on people who'd had no say in making them.

"What's left of the remaining forty percent?" he asked.

"Consolidating around the hard faction. The ones who agreed with Tong Wei's decision, or who are too institutionally invested to walk away even now. Smaller, but more focused — and considerably more willing, I'd guess, to escalate further now that they've lost everything they had left to lose by staying moderate."

He sat with this, the particular weight of an institutional collapse that had produced not peace but a harder, more concentrated remainder.

"Then we should expect them to be more dangerous," he said, "not less, even though there are fewer of them now."

"That's my assessment as well," Chen Yufei said. "I'll keep watching. But I think we should both be ready for whatever the remaining forty percent decides comes next."

He nodded, already turning the question over with the careful, methodical attention he brought to every threat this world handed him, and found, sitting with the strange mixture of vindication and unease the day's news had produced, that he agreed with her completely.
