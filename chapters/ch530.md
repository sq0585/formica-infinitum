# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 530 — The World, Two Years On

Two years since the disclosure had first reached the wider public, and the world had settled into something he had learned, watching it happen in careful, patient increments, to call simply the new normal — a phrase he distrusted slightly, for its implication that normal was a fixed destination rather than an ongoing, uneven, endlessly negotiated process, but a phrase that had, nonetheless, worked its way into enough daily conversation that he'd stopped correcting people who used it.

Government protocols existed now where none had before — clumsy in places, genuinely thoughtful in others, the specific patchwork texture of institutions built too quickly by people doing their honest best with a situation none of them had trained for. University departments had opened, formally, to study what they were now permitted to call Veritonal Energy rather than the careful euphemisms researchers had once been forced to smuggle their work under. He had read three separate doctoral theses in the past month alone, each one earnest, each one arriving at conclusions he already knew from six years of direct experience, and had found himself, reading them, moved in a way he hadn't quite expected by the simple fact of watching human curiosity catch up, methodically, honestly, to a truth it had finally been given permission to pursue openly.

The Cultivation Research Collective — Fang Ruxue's organization, careful always to keep textually and practically distinct from the vast galactic institution that shared a portion of its name — had grown to roughly six hundred members, formal recognition now extended from a dozen national governments who had, each in their own time, each for their own mixture of reasons ranging from genuine curiosity to careful strategic positioning, decided the organization was worth engaging rather than suppressing.

*Observation 530,* he wrote. *Six hundred members. Three hundred and eighty at the close of the last threshold I crossed. The growth has been steady, not explosive — which I think, on reflection, is exactly correct. An explosive growth would have suggested a fad, a curiosity racing ahead of genuine understanding. Steady growth suggests something else: people taking the time to actually learn a discipline properly before claiming it, the way Fang Ruxue insisted from the very beginning that they should.*

It was not utopia. He wanted that recorded plainly, without the softening a more triumphant account might have been tempted toward. Adjustment remained messy in a hundred small, human ways — a government here still quietly monitoring its cultivation-capable citizens with more suspicion than the situation warranted; a community there still working through the specific, difficult grief of discovering that half-remembered folk traditions their grandparents had practiced were, it turned out, genuine fragments of a suppressed Formican inheritance, and wrestling with what to do with the anger that discovery sometimes carried alongside the wonder.

He did not pretend any of it was simple. He had learned, across six years, that pretending complexity away rarely made it resolve faster, and usually only made the eventual reckoning with it harder.

But it was moving. He could feel that clearly, at every scale he now held simultaneously — a world learning, slowly, unevenly, honestly, how to hold a truth it had spent forty million years being kept from, and doing the specific, unglamorous work of holding it well rather than merely holding it at all.

He thought of Lady Shen Baoling, somewhere in the Collective's political machinery, still unaligned, still watching, still waiting to see whether the caution she'd once acted on so drastically would prove, in the end, to have been worth the cost she'd paid for it. He hoped, quietly, that watching this — the steady, patient, genuinely careful growth of a world finally being trusted with itself — might eventually give her something closer to peace than she'd allowed herself in years.

He closed the entry, and turned his attention toward the day's actual work, which remained, as it always had, considerably more ordinary than the scale of what surrounded it.

He thought, too, about the specific texture of the disagreements that still surfaced most often — not fear of the Formicans themselves, which had largely, over two years, given way to curiosity and, in the Strata's case, genuine friendship, but the more mundane, more human friction of any large institutional change: which agency held jurisdiction over Veritonal research funding, whether cultivation practitioners required licensing the way other specialized professions did, how school curricula ought to introduce a truth an entire generation of children were now simply growing up already knowing, the way earlier generations had grown up already knowing the Earth was round.

He found these disagreements, on the whole, encouraging rather than discouraging. A world arguing over licensing boards and curriculum standards was a world that had stopped treating the underlying truth as an emergency and started treating it as a subject — messy, ongoing, occasionally frustrating, and, in exactly the way that mattered most, entirely ordinary.
