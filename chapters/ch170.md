# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter One Hundred and Seventy — Meeting Wei Canglan

They met in person two weeks later — Chen Yufei had arranged it, judging that the relationship had developed to the point where it warranted moving beyond photographed scrolls and careful written exchanges.

Wei Canglan was younger in person than his messages had suggested — twenty-two by stated age but carrying himself with a gravity that read older, the specific seriousness of someone who had grown up inside an institution's expectations and had learned early to measure his words. He arrived at the tea house on Wulin Street precisely on time, sat with correct posture, and did not relax noticeably even after the formal courtesies were exchanged.

"Thank you for the verification," he said, once they'd settled. "I read your analysis of the scroll four times."

"What did you conclude?"

"That you were right." Wei Canglan's expression didn't change, but something in his Qi field — Lin Mushi reading it at the Colony Link's close range — carried a quality of controlled distress underneath the composure. "The notation sequence my grandfather called 'incomplete and potentially dangerous' is, in fact, the complete and correct version. What the Court teaches officially is the corrupted, simplified derivation. My grandfather's scroll is closer to the source than three generations of official transmission."

Lin Mushi nodded slowly. "That must have been difficult to confirm."

"It explains things," Wei Canglan said, with the precision of someone who had spent two weeks thinking carefully about exactly this. "My grandfather's cultivation was always considered unusual — more efficient than his official rank suggested it should be. The Court attributed it to natural talent. I think now it was because he privately practiced from the more complete scroll, even though he taught the official version to his students, including my father, including eventually me."

"Why would he do that?"

"I don't know. I think — " Wei Canglan paused, choosing the words carefully. "I think he was afraid of what it would mean to challenge the official teaching. He kept the more accurate version as a private practice, but he never tried to correct the Court's instruction. He just quietly benefited from knowing better, without sharing it."

Lin Mushi thought about this — a small, contained version of exactly the dynamic playing out at institutional scale across all five Courts now: people who knew, or half-knew, or suspected, choosing silence over disruption because disruption was costly and silence was comfortable.

"What are you going to do with what you've confirmed?" he asked.

Wei Canglan was quiet for a long moment.

"I've been asking myself that question for two weeks," he said. "I keep arriving at the same answer, even though I don't like where it leads."

"Tell me the answer."

"I think I have to say something publicly. Not just confirm it privately to you, the way my grandfather confirmed it privately to himself. If I know, and stay silent, I become exactly what he became — someone who benefited from a corrected understanding while letting everyone else continue believing the corrupted version."

Lin Mushi was quiet. This was not a decision he was going to make for Wei Canglan, and he understood, with the clarity of someone who had been in a version of this position himself, that saying the encouraging thing right now would be a kind of manipulation, however well-intentioned.

"What would saying something publicly cost you?" he asked instead.

Wei Canglan's expression didn't change, but the field underneath did — a flicker of something that read as fear, quickly contained.

"I don't know precisely," he said. "I know the Council has already voted to suppress the document. Speaking publicly in support of it, with my own family's scroll as supporting evidence, would put me in direct conflict with that decision. The Northern Peak Court doesn't tolerate that well." He paused. "My family has a property loan, co-signed by the Court as part of a sect membership benefit three generations ago. I don't know if that's connected to what I'm considering. I suspect it might become connected, if the Court decides to make an example."

Lin Mushi held this.

"Whatever you decide," he said, "understand the cost before you decide it. Not as a reason to choose the safer path — I'm not trying to talk you out of anything. As a condition of choosing honestly. If you act without understanding what it might cost, the cost will feel like a betrayal when it lands. If you understand it going in, it's still painful, but it's not a surprise."

Wei Canglan looked at him for a long moment.

"That's not the speech I expected," he said.

"What did you expect?"

"Something more like — 'the truth is worth any cost.' Something inspiring."

"The truth might be worth any cost," Lin Mushi said. "That's a question I can't answer for you. What I can tell you accurately is what the cost is likely to be, as best I understand it, so your decision is informed rather than romantic."

Wei Canglan was quiet for a long time.

"I'd rather carry the truth than carry the lie," he said finally. Quietly, but without hesitation.

Lin Mushi nodded.

He didn't say anything else. There was nothing else that needed to be said.

Three days later, Wei Canglan spoke publicly within the Northern Peak Court.

The cost arrived faster than either of them had expected.
