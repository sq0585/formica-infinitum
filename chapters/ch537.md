# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 537 — What the World Is Now

Six years, almost to the day, since a dying man had leaned against a convenience store counter at 3:17 in the morning and closed his fingers around a stranger's wrist.

He let himself hold the number plainly, once, the way the notebook's oldest habits had taught him to hold anything that actually mattered — not as an abstraction, but as a specific, felt weight, six full years compressed into a single breath before he let it settle back into the ordinary rhythm of an ordinary morning.

Cultivation was practiced openly now in forty-seven countries, regulated with the specific, unglamorous care of governments that had, each in their own time, decided the practice deserved structure rather than either suppression or unchecked freedom. He had read enough of the resulting legislation, across the past year, to understand that none of it was perfect, and most of it was, on the whole, honest — the particular, hard-won honesty of institutions doing their genuine best with a truth they had never trained to hold.

The Strata network had grown to twelve cities.

He sat with that figure a moment, understanding, even now, that it still carried some capacity to surprise him. Chengdu had been the only Strata for so long — years, in fact, when a single pocket-dimension residential quarter housing a handful of families had felt like an achievement worth defending with everything he had. Twelve cities now, scattered across a dozen countries, each one grown from its own particular local negotiation, its own particular blend of human and Formican community, none of them identical to Chengdu's model, each of them recognizably, unmistakably built from the same founding instinct Su Lian had written down on her notepad — a place where difference could sit down together and argue, happily, about something as small as soil.

*Observation 537,* he wrote. *The Pacific Ring stands fully maintained, its structures load-bearing and sound in every measurement Dr. Shen Yue's instruments can offer. The Under-Web is better tended now than it has been in two thousand years — a fact Meen-4 confirmed to me last month with something close to wonder in her formal register, an emotion I have learned, across six years, to recognize as considerably rarer from her than it sounds when translated into human language.*

Everything that had once been hidden was visible now. He understood the specific weight of that sentence more than he once had — visibility had never been an unqualified good, not entirely, the disclosure's uneven, ongoing adjustment proof enough of that. But hidden things could not be tended properly, could not be repaired, could not be argued over honestly by the people whose lives they actually touched. Visible, even messily, even incompletely, was better.

Everything broken was being repaired. The dissolution zone continued its slow, patient healing, two centuries from completion now rather than twelve million years, a number that had stopped feeling impossible to him the longer he'd sat with it. The Pacific Ring's oldest fatigue points, tended now by a generation of practitioners who understood their function fully rather than by half-remembered fragments of suppressed tradition, held steadier than they had in centuries.

And everything that had once been faced alone was, now, thoroughly, unmistakably shared.

He thought of the man who had died believing he was handing an impossible burden to a single, unprepared stranger — a night-shift clerk with no framework for any of what was coming, no allies, no institution, nothing but curiosity and a willingness to keep showing up for whatever the Codex asked of him next. He thought of everything that stranger now stood surrounded by: five Anchors, three hundred and twenty-three volunteers, twelve cities, forty-seven countries, a mayor who measured her policies against lunch between an archivist and a schoolteacher, a Nobel laureate who had once answered his forum post out of nothing more than honest curiosity.

Ren Wuji had never met any of them. He had trusted, entirely, that they would eventually arrive anyway.

They had.

Lin Mushi closed the notebook on that thought, unhurried, and sat a while longer in the ordinary morning light, in no particular hurry to move on to whatever the rest of an entirely ordinary day still held.
