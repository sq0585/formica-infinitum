# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter Two Hundred and Sixty-Four — Liang Jingzhi's Thesis

She brought the final draft to him three weeks before her submission deadline, the careful, deliberate timing of someone who wanted enough margin to make changes if the conversation that followed required them.

"I need you to read this before I submit it," she said, setting the printed manuscript on the table between them. "Not for accuracy — I know it's accurate, I've checked every figure a dozen times. I need you to read it for what it doesn't say."

He read through it carefully over the following hour, the dense, methodical prose of someone who had spent years learning to write exactly what her data supported and nothing more. The density anomalies, the structural patterns, the careful correlation with surface geology — all of it precise, defensible, exactly the kind of work that would earn her doctorate without controversy.

The explanation for what actually caused the anomalies was, deliberately and carefully, absent.

She'd built the thesis around a careful scientific honesty he recognized immediately: stating clearly what the data showed, acknowledging explicitly what current geological theory couldn't explain, and stopping there, without speculation, without reaching for an explanation she knew to be true but couldn't ethically present as scientifically supported.

"You left the gap open," he said, setting the manuscript down.

"Deliberately. We talked about this three separate times, and I kept coming back to the same conclusion." She met his eyes steadily. "I can't claim what I actually know caused these anomalies. Not because I'm uncertain — I'm not, not anymore. But because the scientific method I've spent years training in doesn't have room for an explanation grounded in evidence I'm not able to share publicly, verified through methods I can't describe to my committee."

"So you said nothing instead of saying something false."

"I said exactly what the conventional data supports, and explicitly noted where current theory fails to fully explain the pattern. That's honest. It's also incomplete, and I know it's incomplete, and I've decided that incomplete honesty is better than complete dishonesty dressed up as a comprehensive theory."

He considered the choice she'd made, understanding slowly the particular discipline it had required — not the easier path of inventing a plausible cover explanation, not the riskier path of hinting at something her committee would dismiss as speculation, but the harder, more honest middle ground of simply stating the limits of what she could currently claim.

"This is going to bother you," he said. "Submitting work you know is incomplete, when you have the actual answer sitting right there."

"It already bothers me," she said. "Every day, a little. But I'd rather carry that discomfort than either lie to my committee or expose something that isn't mine alone to expose. This is the most honest version of incomplete I know how to write."

"I think it's exactly the right choice," he said. "I think most people, given what you actually know, would have found a way to hint at it — enough to seem brilliant and mysterious, without quite crossing into outright disclosure. You didn't do that. You just said what you could defend and stopped."

"Is that meant to be reassuring?"

"It's meant to be accurate. I respect the discipline this took more than I'd respect a cleverer, more self-serving version of the same gap."

She picked the manuscript back up, holding it for a moment with the particular weight of someone about to submit years of careful work into a process that would, regardless of how honestly she'd written it, never fully capture what she actually knew.

"My committee is going to ask follow-up questions," she said. "About the explanation gap specifically. I've prepared an answer: more research is needed, the mechanism remains under investigation, further study is ongoing. All of it technically true."

"It is."

"It still feels like a kind of lie, even when every individual word is accurate."

He thought about his own years of careful, honest evasions — the geological consulting cover story, the explanations he'd given his mother, the particular discipline of telling the truth in pieces small enough not to demand explanations he couldn't yet give.

"It's not a lie," he said. "It's a boundary. There's a difference, even when the discomfort feels similar from the inside."

She held onto that for a moment, turning it over, and something in her posture eased slightly.

"A boundary," she repeated. "I can submit something built around an honest boundary. I don't think I could have submitted something built around a lie, even a comfortable one."

"Then submit it," he said. "It's good work. The gap doesn't diminish that. If anything, the gap is the most honest part of the entire document."

She nodded, gathering the manuscript carefully, and he watched her leave with the particular satisfaction of having helped someone hold a difficult line without ever once suggesting she should simply abandon it.
