# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter Two Hundred and Eighteen — The Colony Link Expands, Unwanted

It started as a kind of background weather he could mostly ignore, and became, over the course of about ten days, something he could no longer pretend was background at all.

He'd known the Colony Link could read emotional states at close range — his mother's voice over the phone, Chen Yufei's careful composure when something underneath it wasn't actually composed. He'd thought of it as a tool, something he reached for deliberately, the way he reached for the Circuit network's readouts. Deliberate. Bounded. His to use or not use.

He noticed the change on a Tuesday, standing in the outer hall, when he became aware — without trying, without reaching for anything — of Wei Canglan's low simmering frustration two corridors away, and Su Lan's careful, contained loneliness somewhere past the inner archive, and the quiet, settled contentment radiating from Chen Yufei's usual workspace, all three arriving in his attention at once, unbidden, the way ambient sound arrives in a room you haven't asked to be quiet.

He tried to turn it off.

He spent an hour that evening attempting every technique he knew for narrowing the Colony Link's range back down to where it used to sit — the same disciplined contraction he used for Compression, applied in reverse. Nothing worked. The awareness remained, diffuse and constant, three distinct emotional signatures sitting at the edge of his attention regardless of what he did to dismiss them.

By the end of the week, it was five signatures. By the following Monday, everyone currently inside the Strata, all at once, all the time.

---

He didn't tell anyone immediately.

He told himself this was caution — that he needed to understand the mechanism before he explained something he didn't yet fully control, that announcing an unbounded ability before he knew its limits would only frighten people without giving them anything useful to do with the fear. He suspected, underneath the reasoning, that some part of it was simply reluctance to say out loud that he was now carrying other people's private feelings without their knowledge or consent, and didn't know how to stop.

It was Wei Canglan who forced the issue, eleven days after the expansion began, catching something in Lin Mushi's expression during an otherwise unremarkable conversation that didn't match anything either of them had said out loud.

"You can feel what we're feeling, can't you," Wei Canglan said. Not a question, despite the phrasing. "How long?"

Lin Mushi considered, for a moment, whether there was a version of the truth that softened the answer without actually changing it, and found there wasn't.

"Just over a week and a half," he said. "It started small — a few people, only when I wasn't actively trying to block it. It's not small anymore. I can feel everyone currently inside the Strata, continuously, and I haven't found a way to turn it off."

Wei Canglan's expression didn't shift toward anger immediately, which Lin Mushi had half expected. It shifted instead toward something quieter and more unsettling — the particular stillness of someone recalculating, in real time, everything they'd said and felt in the past eleven days under the assumption that none of it had an audience.

"And you were planning to tell us when?" he said finally.

"I was hoping it would resolve. That it was a temporary expansion, the way the early Colony Link growth was, and it would settle back down once I understood the mechanism better."

"It won't, though. Will it."

"I don't know that yet."

"You don't know it'll stop," Wei Canglan said, "and you've known for eleven days that it's currently a problem, and you decided that not telling people their feelings were being read without their consent was a reasonable choice to make on their behalf."

The accusation landed exactly as hard as it deserved to.

"You're right," Lin Mushi said. "I should have told everyone immediately, even without a solution ready. I made the wrong call, and I made it for reasons that were more about my own discomfort than about what was fair to the people affected."

Wei Canglan studied him for a long moment, the anger present but controlled, the way most of his anger had been since the day he'd lost everything for a choice he still believed had been correct.

"Tell everyone," he said. "Now. Tonight. Whatever it costs you to say it out loud, say it anyway."

"I will."

"I mean it, Lin Mushi. Not next week. Tonight."

"Tonight," Lin Mushi agreed, and meant it, already turning the words over in his mind, trying to find the version of this confession that would do the least additional harm to people who deserved, at minimum, to know exactly what they were dealing with before they decided whether they could still trust the space they'd chosen to live in.
