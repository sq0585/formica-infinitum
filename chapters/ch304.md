# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter Three Hundred and Four — The Strata City, Design

The idea had been circling for weeks before anyone said it directly, the way important ideas sometimes accumulated weight in the margins of ordinary conversation before finally demanding to be named. It was Fang Ruxue who finally said it, during a routine planning session, with the flat clarity she brought to most things.

"We're not building a residential wing anymore," she said, looking around the table at the four of them — herself, Liang Jingzhi, Meen-4 present through the translation display, and Lin Mushi. "We're building a city. I'd like us to plan accordingly rather than continuing to treat this as an extension of something smaller."

No one disagreed. The numbers had been saying the same thing for a month — three hundred and forty residents and climbing, the workshop district's friction resolving into genuine shared practice, the laboratory's eight stations nearly all occupied. What had started as a handful of rooms built around one old woman's complaint about the cold had become, without any single dramatic moment marking the transition, something considerably larger.

"Then let's plan a city," he said. "What does that actually require, that a residential wing didn't?"

---

The design session that followed ran for the better part of three days, each participant bringing a piece of expertise none of the others fully possessed, the four of them gradually assembling something that felt, by the second day, less like a construction plan and more like the founding document of an actual place.

Liang Jingzhi anchored the geological reality first, spreading survey data across the table with the same unhurried precision she brought to everything. "The Strata's current footprint sits within stable bedrock, but a city-scale expansion needs load distribution planned from the start, not patched in after the fact. I can give you the safe expansion envelope — where the geology genuinely supports growth, and where it doesn't, regardless of what the Dimensional Weaving can technically build."

"Show me where it doesn't."

She traced a section along the Strata's northeastern boundary. "Here. Not because the fold itself would fail, but because the surface geology above it is already under stress from the metropolitan construction happening independently overhead. Building density into this section risks feedback we can't fully predict."

He noted it, understanding, not for the first time, exactly why he'd wanted her voice in this room from the beginning — a caution grounded in something considerably more concrete than instinct.

Fang Ruxue spoke next, her contribution less about physical space and more about the structure that would eventually govern it. "Districts, clearly defined, each with its own character and its own local decision-making within the broader framework we've already established. Residential. Workshop. Laboratory. And — " she paused, glancing at Meen-4's translation display, "a Formican section that isn't simply the older Strata sections we've inherited, but something deliberately built to reflect what Formican residents actually want from a shared city, rather than assuming they'll adapt to human-designed spaces."

*This is correct,* Meen-4 signaled. *I would add a specific requirement. Meditation and reflection spaces, distributed rather than centralized, sized appropriately for individual Formican practice. Several of the community's oldest members have practiced solitary reflection in specific locations for centuries. Any redesign must account for existing use, not merely available space.*

He made the note carefully, understanding the weight underneath the request even before Meen-4 elaborated further.

*There are locations within the current Strata boundary that specific individuals consider theirs, in practice if not in formal designation,* she continued. *Axis-7, for instance, has used a particular meditation space for slightly over three thousand years. I would ask that the design process consult directly with residents whose existing use of space might be affected, rather than assuming available square footage is simply available.*

"Agreed," he said immediately. "I'd rather build around what's already meaningful than build over it and apologize later."

---

They spent the final day on environmental physics, Dr. Shen Yue joining briefly to walk through the requirements a city-scale population would place on the Strata's air, temperature, and Qi circulation systems — considerably more complex than the residential wing's needs, requiring active management rather than the passive stability smaller spaces had allowed.

"At this scale, you need redundancy," she said, sketching rough calculations on the shared display. "Not just capacity — capacity plus a margin wide enough that a single system failure doesn't cascade into an actual crisis. I'd rather over-engineer this now than discover the gap during an emergency."

By the end of the third day, the design sat spread across the table in its full, ambitious shape — districts, geological constraints, Formican meditation spaces preserved and honored, environmental redundancy built in from the start rather than patched on later. It wasn't finished. It would need revision, probably many revisions, as the actual work of building it revealed problems no amount of careful planning could have fully anticipated.

But looking at it, Lin Mushi felt something he hadn't quite expected to feel this early in the process — not the satisfaction of a completed plan, but the specific, grounded confidence of watching four different kinds of expertise fit together into something none of them could have built alone.

"This is good work," he said, echoing, without quite meaning to, the words Meen-4 had once said to him standing in an empty hall two years earlier.

"It's a start," Fang Ruxue corrected, already gathering her notes for the next round of revisions. "But yes. It's good work."

He looked around the table one final time before they dispersed — geologist, administrator, physicist, and archivist, four very different minds who had spent three days building the outline of an actual city — and understood, with quiet certainty, that whatever came next, they weren't building it alone.
