# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 456 — Her Answer

She called him the next morning, before he'd finished his own tea, which surprised him enough that he answered before checking who it was, an old habit from years when only two or three people ever called this early.

"I've been thinking," she said, no greeting, the same particular directness she'd carried the night the world first started reading her son's name in a physics paper. "I want to say something properly, and I didn't trust myself to say it well enough last night, with everything so fresh."

"I'm listening."

"You are still the person who calls every Sunday and asks about my garden," she said, and he could hear, even through the phone, the specific care she'd put into choosing the words beforehand, the way she prepared things that mattered. "Whatever else you are — however large it turns out to actually be — that part hasn't changed, and I don't believe it's going to. Everything else is something I will learn about slowly. I would like to learn about it. I am not asking you to make it small enough for me to understand all at once. I am asking you to be patient with me while I catch up."

He felt something settle in his chest at the specific generosity of it — not forgiveness exactly, because she hadn't framed the five years of careful omission as something requiring forgiveness in the first place, but something steadier, an acceptance offered without conditions he hadn't already, somehow, met simply by finally telling her the truth.

"I can be as patient as you need," he said.

"Good. Because I intend to ask a great many questions, and I expect complete answers, not the gentle version you've apparently been giving me for years."

He smiled, despite everything, at the particular, familiar bluntness of it. "What would you like to know first?"

"The extension," she said, and he heard, in the shift of her tone, the moment the conversation moved from the emotional ground of the previous night onto something more practical, more like the woman who had spent thirty years running a household on careful budgets and clear-eyed decisions. "You mentioned it last night, briefly, before I sent you home to sleep. Something that could help with my knees, my heart. I want to hear about it properly."

"Are you sure you want to get into this today? There's no urgency —"

"Lin Mushi." The particular weight she put on his full name again, the same weight she'd used the previous night. "I have spent five years not asking questions I clearly should have asked. I am not interested in easing back into this slowly out of politeness. Tell me about the extension protocol. Start from the beginning, and don't skip anything, the way you apparently skipped things for five years running."

He found himself laughing, briefly, the sound surprising him with how much lighter it felt than anything he'd managed in days. "Start from the beginning," he agreed. "All of it."

He told her, then, carefully and completely — the mechanism Meen-4 had explained to him, the three months it would require, the real risk alongside the real benefit, Fang Ruxue's own precedent, everything he understood about what accepting it would actually mean for a woman who had spent seventy-four years entirely, ordinarily human.

She listened the way she'd listened the night before, without interrupting, though he could see, even over the phone's limited connection, the particular focus of someone building a careful mental framework rather than simply absorbing information passively.

"This is a great deal to consider," she said, once he'd finished.

"I know. There's no timeline. Take however long you need."

"I intend to," she said, with the specific practicality that had, he suspected, kept an entire household running smoothly through every difficulty life had handed it. "But I want you to know something first, before I go away and think properly." Her voice softened, just slightly, underneath the practicality. "I am not frightened of this. I want that understood clearly. I am cautious, because caution has served me well my whole life, and seventy-four years of habits don't disappear simply because my son turned out to be considerably more extraordinary than I ever expected. But I am not frightened. Whatever I decide, it won't be from fear."

"I believe you," he said, and found, saying it, that he meant it entirely.

"Good," she said. "Now go eat something. You still sound too thin, even over the phone, which I didn't think was possible until just now."

He laughed again, the sound settling something further in his chest, and promised he would, and let her go to whatever quiet, careful thinking she now had ahead of her.
