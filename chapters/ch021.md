# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter Twenty-One — Learning the Alphabet

He had three anchors.

Three symbols in the Codex's notation that he was certain about — certain meaning: he had verified them across enough contexts that his confidence exceeded ninety percent. Three was not many, out of sixty to eighty total. But three was enough to start, because three symbols that you understood with certainty were a key, and a key let you begin testing locks.

The first symbol he'd decoded was the category marker for heavy nodes — the three-symbol cluster that appeared at every threshold and concentration point. Working backward from known spatial referents (the node under the store, the threshold in the northwest corner) he had isolated the symbol that carried the meaning *transition* or *interface*, distinct from the ones that specified the type and scale of the transition.

The second he'd decoded from context: a symbol that appeared at the beginning of every block of notation he could identify as instructional rather than descriptive. He was calling it the *procedural prefix* — something like a grammatical marker that said *this is a how, not a what*.

The third had come from the second message itself. The second message had used readable characters alongside the notation for the first three words — *You are reading* — and the notational symbol paired with *reading* appeared elsewhere in the diagram in contexts that made sense as *perceiving* or *accessing*.

Three symbols. Transition. Procedure. Perception.

He spread his notebooks across the desk and began.

---

Linguistic archaeology, he had decided, was the right frame. Not cryptography — cryptography assumed a code with a one-to-one mapping to a known language, a puzzle with a definite solution. This was something older: a symbol system evolved for purposes he didn't fully understand yet, by minds that thought in ways that didn't precisely overlap with his. The goal was not to decode it into Chinese. The goal was to learn it as itself.

He started with distribution analysis. Every occurrence of every symbol he'd catalogued, marked by context: where it appeared (in a line of notation, at a node marker, at the beginning or end of a block), what symbols it appeared adjacent to, and whether the sequence it was part of seemed to function as a name, an instruction, a measurement, or something else.

The work was slow. It required the kind of attention that forgot to notice hunger or the passage of time, which was his preferred working state.

By the end of the first afternoon he had a revised symbol count: sixty-three distinct units, not sixty to eighty. He had probable identifications for eleven of them, tentative identifications for another eight, and a structural insight he hadn't expected: the notation didn't use spaces between units. Segmentation — knowing where one unit ended and another began — was itself part of the grammar. The way the symbols combined told you how to read the boundaries.

This was not unlike certain Southeast Asian scripts he'd briefly read about. He wrote *cf. abugida structure?* in the margin and filed it as a research direction.

---

At 9 PM, with his shift starting in an hour, he wrote the day's summary:

> *Notation analysis, Day 1 systematic.*
>
> *Confirmed units: 63. Probable identifications: 11. Tentative: 8.*
>
> *Key structural insight: boundary segmentation is grammatically encoded, not spatially marked. Reading the notation requires learning to parse the grammar simultaneously with the vocabulary — you cannot learn words first and structure second. They are learned together.*
>
> *This is why the first message 'became readable' rather than being decoded. The Chitin Seed didn't translate it. It gave me the grammar. With the grammar, the vocabulary followed immediately.*
>
> *Implication: further Codex comprehension is not about accumulating vocabulary. It is about grammatical depth. Each new level of grammar will unlock large sections simultaneously.*
>
> *Next: find the next grammatical layer.*

He made tea. He ate leftover rice from the fridge standing at the kitchen counter, which was not ideal but was fast.

Outside, the October evening was settling into cold. He put on his jacket, picked up his bag, and walked to the store with his mind still half in the notation, turning the abugida hypothesis over like a coin he'd found and wasn't yet sure was currency.

The alphabet was becoming legible.

Slowly. But that was the point.
