# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter One Hundred and Seventy-One — What It Costs

Wei Canglan's public statement was made at a regional Northern Peak gathering, not the central Council session — a deliberate choice, he later explained to Lin Mushi, made to reach the practitioner community directly rather than going through channels the Council could more easily suppress before it reached anyone.

He presented the scroll. He presented his own analysis, supported by Lin Mushi's independent verification, properly cited. He was careful, methodical, the same precision he'd brought to every exchange since his first message.

He did not denounce the Court. He did not call for its dissolution. He said, according to the account Chen Yufei's network relayed afterward: *I am not telling you the Court is wrong to exist. I am telling you that the technique we teach is an incomplete derivation of something more complete, and that I believe we owe it to every practitioner training under us to teach the complete version, regardless of what that means for how we explain our own history.*

It was, Lin Mushi thought when he heard the account, almost exactly the position he himself had tried to take with the document's release — correction rather than condemnation.

It did not matter.

The disciplinary proceedings began within twenty-four hours. Wei Canglan was stripped of his third rank. His training privileges were suspended pending formal review. And — the detail that arrived three days later, relayed through Chen Yufei's network with the specific grimness of bad news that had been confirmed rather than rumored — the property loan his family held, co-signed by the Court as a three-generation membership benefit, was called in for immediate repayment.

His parents could not repay it on short notice. They lost the apartment.

Lin Mushi learned this on a Thursday evening, sitting in his own apartment with Chen Yufei across from him, both of them quiet with the specific weight of consequences that had been theoretically anticipated and were now concretely real.

"He knew this might happen," Chen Yufei said. "You told him to understand the cost before deciding."

"I know," Lin Mushi said. "Knowing it might happen and watching it happen to someone's parents are different things to carry."

She didn't disagree.

"What does he need right now?" Lin Mushi asked.

"I don't know yet. I haven't spoken to him since the proceedings began. I think he's — processing." She paused. "I think he might come to us. He doesn't have many other places to go right now."

Lin Mushi sat with this.

He thought about Chen Yufei's own expulsion, months ago — the formal process, the loss of institutional standing, and her surprise at how little it had ultimately changed about what she actually had. He thought about whether Wei Canglan's situation would follow the same trajectory, or whether the concrete material cost — his parents' apartment, his family's stability — made it a fundamentally different kind of loss.

He suspected it did. Chen Yufei had lost status. Wei Canglan's family had lost a home.

"I want to help," Lin Mushi said. "Not in a way that makes him feel like a charity case, or like I'm purchasing his gratitude. But I caused this, in the sense that my document created the conditions that led him here, and I don't think I get to simply observe the consequence from a comfortable distance."

Chen Yufei considered this carefully.

"Let him come to you on his own terms first," she said. "Don't reach out preemptively. If he asks for help, help. If he doesn't ask, find a way to help that doesn't require him to ask — something that doesn't put him in the position of having to perform need."

He nodded. This was good counsel, and it matched something he'd already been turning over.

Two days later, Wei Canglan's younger sister, Mei Lan, contacted Chen Yufei's network directly.

The message that followed changed the shape of what Lin Mushi understood the cost to actually be.
