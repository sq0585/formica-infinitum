# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 406 — Month 36 — The Separation

Three years, almost to the week, since Ren Wuji had died in a small apartment with a Codex and a Chitin Seed and no one left to explain either one to him. Lin Mushi noticed the anniversary the way he noticed most things now — not with ceremony, just a quiet mental note, filed alongside everything else the past three years had accumulated.

He and Chen Yufei had been circling this conversation for six months, though neither of them had said so directly until tonight. He recognized the circling now, looking back — the small ways their conversations had grown careful around certain subjects, the way she'd started arriving at his apartment slightly later than she used to, not from any lack of affection but from something more like hesitation, the particular reluctance of two people who both sensed a conversation waiting and kept, gently, without quite admitting it, finding reasons to delay it one more evening.

Tonight, there was no more delaying it.

They sat together on the floor of the apartment above the noodle shop, the same spot where Chen Yufei had once held the Anchor bond steady for four hours straight, and for a long moment neither of them spoke, the silence carrying a weight that felt less like avoidance and more like both of them gathering the courage to finally say something true.

"I've been thinking about how often you're partially absent," Chen Yufei said finally, choosing her words with visible care. "Not accusingly. I want to say that first. I'm not upset about it. I've watched it happen gradually, over months, and I don't think it's a failure of anything. I think it's just what happens when someone's cognition starts operating at speeds and scales a single conversation can't fully hold anymore."

He didn't argue, because he'd noticed it too — the way he sometimes surfaced from a distributed thought mid-sentence, unsure how much of the conversation he'd genuinely been present for and how much he'd simply been managing on some background layer of attention while the rest of him monitored Circuit diagnostics or the Colony Link's ambient state. He had gotten better at hiding it. He had not gotten better at not doing it.

"I know," he said quietly. "I've felt it too. I just didn't know how to bring it up without it sounding like I was already saying goodbye."

"Maybe you were," she said, not unkindly. "Maybe some part of both of us has been saying it for a while now, quietly, without either of us admitting the words out loud."

He looked at her, and felt something in his chest that was not quite grief and not quite relief — something more complicated, the specific ache of watching a true thing finally become spoken instead of merely felt.

"I don't want this to be a crisis," he said. "I don't want it to be a betrayal, or a failure, or something either of us has to carry blame for. I think — " he paused, gathering the honest shape of it. "I think you deserve to be with someone who's actually, fully present when you're talking to them. Not partially. Not most of the time. And I don't know how to promise you that anymore, the way things are going."

"I know," she said, and something in her voice carried the particular steadiness of someone who had already arrived at this conclusion on her own, independently, in whatever quiet hours she'd spent over the past six months turning the same question over. "I've been trying to decide, for months, whether the distance is temporary — something that might ease once the review settled, once you had more room to breathe — or whether it's simply the shape of who you're becoming now, permanently, and I need to decide what I want in relation to that shape rather than waiting for it to change back into something smaller."

"And what did you decide?"

She was quiet a long moment, and when she finally answered, her voice was steady, careful, and entirely without bitterness.

"I decided it's the second one," she said. "And I decided that's all right. Not easy. But all right."

He felt the words land, gently, the way the biggest truths in his life had always landed — not with drama, not with tears, just a quiet, clear-eyed acknowledgment of something that had already been true for a while before either of them found the courage to name it.

"Then tell me the rest of it," he said softly. "Whatever you've been carrying toward saying, for six months. I'd rather hear it fully tonight than keep circling it any longer."

She reached for his hand, held it, and began.
