# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter Two Hundred and Seventy-Seven — The Physicist

Dr. Shen Yue's office occupied a cramped corner of Sichuan University's physics department, every available surface covered in printouts, the particular organized chaos of someone whose mind moved faster than her filing system could keep pace with.

She was younger than he'd expected — thirty-four, by the department directory, a junior professor whose office nameplate sat slightly crooked, as though hung in passing by someone with more urgent things on her mind than perfect alignment.

"You're the forum account," she said, by way of greeting, studying him with the same direct, assessing attention he remembered from people who trusted their own judgment more than social convention. "I half-expected someone considerably older. The way you described the structures suggested decades of specialized training."

"It's been an unusual few years," he said, which was, he reflected, probably the most understated sentence he'd produced in a long time.

She gestured him toward a chair buried under printouts, which she cleared with the brisk efficiency of someone accustomed to making space for visitors on short notice, and pulled up a dataset on her monitor without further preamble.

"Three years of dark energy anomaly readings," she said. "Correlated against the images you posted. I built a working model — incomplete, I knew it was incomplete the entire time, but internally consistent enough to keep refining. Quantum field theory framework, extended to account for the structural patterns your images suggested."

He studied the model on her screen, working through the dense mathematical notation with the particular satisfaction of recognizing genuine, careful rigor even before he'd determined how accurate the underlying framework actually was.

It took him perhaps ten minutes to understand the shape of what she'd built.

"This is remarkably close," he said slowly. "Closer than I expected anyone working without direct access to the actual phenomenon could get."

"Close to what, specifically?"

He considered how much to say, the same careful calculation he'd made with Liang Jingzhi, with Fang Ruxue, with every person he'd eventually decided deserved the full truth rather than a careful, protective fragment of it.

"Close to correct," he said. "About seventy percent, by my own rough estimate. You've built the right model with tools calibrated for the wrong reference frame. The mathematics aren't wrong. The framework they're operating inside of is incomplete in a specific, identifiable way."

She sat back, something sharpening in her expression — not offense at the correction, but genuine, focused hunger for exactly the kind of precision he'd just offered her.

"Explain the reference frame problem," she said. "Precisely, please. I don't want a simplified version."

He spent the next two hours walking her through the foundations of what she'd been circling for three years without quite having the conceptual vocabulary to name directly — not dark energy in the sense her field currently understood it, but Veritonal Energy, a structured substrate with its own internal logic that conventional physics had been measuring the effects of without ever correctly identifying the cause.

She didn't flinch at any of it. She took notes continuously, asked precise follow-up questions exactly where her existing model's assumptions broke down, and rebuilt entire sections of her framework in real time as he spoke, the particular intellectual hunger of someone who had spent three years certain something larger existed just beyond her current tools' reach, finally given the missing piece she'd needed.

"This implies the universe has more structure than we thought," she said, near the end of the second hour, staring at the reworked model on her screen.

"Yes."

"A lot more structure."

"Yes."

She sat with this for a long moment, the particular stillness of someone whose entire professional framework had just been quietly, completely reshaped, and then, instead of the shock or disorientation he might have expected, simply pulled out a fresh notebook page and began writing, fast and focused, the careful organized chaos of her office's surfaces about to gain one more contributing layer.

"I have considerably more questions," she said, not looking up.

"I expected you might."

"Most of them are going to be technical, and I suspect this is going to take more than one conversation."

"It will," he agreed. "I'd like that, actually. I haven't had many conversations with someone who could engage with this at the level you just demonstrated."

She finally looked up, something assessing and genuinely pleased crossing her expression.

"Then I suppose," she said, "we have a great deal of work ahead of us."

He felt the familiar echo of the phrase settle into him — the same words Fang Ruxue had once offered, the same words Liang Jingzhi had eventually arrived at herself — and found, hearing it now from someone entirely new to this strange, accumulating community, that the pattern had become, somewhere along the way, one of the most reliable signs he'd learned to recognize that he'd found exactly the right kind of person to trust with the truth.
