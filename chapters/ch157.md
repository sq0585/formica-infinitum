# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter One Hundred and Fifty-Seven — The Tea House Meeting

The two senior Council members introduced themselves as Elder Sun and Elder Bai. Elder Sun was the one who spoke first, after the formal courtesies — a woman in her seventies with the unhurried precision of someone who had outlasted several generations of institutional politics and had developed, as a consequence, a kind of patience that read as authority.

"You've caused the Northern Peak Court a great deal of reconsideration," she said, without preamble, once the tea had been poured.

"I've shared accurate information," he said. "The reconsideration is what accurate information does to institutions built on partial understanding."

Elder Sun looked at him for a moment with an expression he couldn't fully read — not hostility, something closer to the careful assessment a skilled player gives an opponent whose skill they're still calibrating.

"That's a generous way to describe upending eight hundred years of doctrine," she said.

"I didn't upend the doctrine," he said. "I corrected the explanation. The technique still works. The practitioners' results are still real. What changed is the understanding of why."

Elder Bai, quieter, said: "Lady Shen's report described the archive as a functioning Formican institution with its own authority structure. Is that accurate?"

"Yes."

"And your role within it?"

"I have access, earned through demonstrated capability and good faith. I don't have authority over it. I can't grant or withhold access on the archive's behalf — only my own continued cooperation."

Elder Sun and Elder Bai exchanged a brief look.

"The Council's position," Elder Sun said, "after considerable internal debate, is that the Northern Peak Court wishes to establish a structured, respectful relationship — with you, as the practitioner who bridges our world and the archive's, and eventually, we hope, with the archive itself, on terms it sets."

"That's a reasonable position," he said.

"We are prepared to offer formal recognition," she continued. "Independent practitioner status, acknowledged by the Court, with the protections and standing that come with it. In exchange for — not exclusivity, we understand that's not realistic given the document's broad distribution — but for being treated as a good-faith partner in whatever structure eventually emerges."

He considered this carefully. It was, as Kuo had predicted months ago, a better offer than the original acquisition mandate — recognition rather than control, partnership rather than possession.

It was also, he understood clearly, still an offer that asked him to accept legitimacy from an institution whose foundational authority was built on the very misunderstanding he'd spent a year correcting.

"I appreciate the offer," he said. "I'd like to think about it properly rather than answer today."

Elder Sun's expression shifted slightly — not displeased, recalibrating. "How long would you need?"

"Two weeks," he said.

She considered this. "That's acceptable."

Elder Bai spoke again. "May I ask — and forgive the bluntness — what would make you decline? We've offered what we believe to be a generous and respectful overture."

He thought about how to answer this honestly.

"What I'm uncertain about," he said, "is whether accepting recognition from an institution implies an endorsement of that institution's authority structure — an authority structure that has, until very recently, been operating on an incomplete and partially incorrect understanding of the very phenomenon it claims to govern." He met Elder Bai's gaze. "I'm not trying to be difficult. I'm trying to be accurate about what's actually at stake in the offer."

A long silence at the table.

"That is," Elder Sun said slowly, "a more sophisticated objection than I expected."

"I've had a year of practice being precise about things," he said.

She almost smiled — the first crack in the formal posture since the meeting began.

"Two weeks," she said. "We'll await your answer." She paused. "For what it's worth — speaking now not as a Council representative but as someone who has practiced this discipline for fifty-one years — the offer is not contingent on your answer being yes. We would still value the relationship either way."

He looked at her, reading the field at close range: the statement carried genuine weight, not performance.

"Thank you," he said. "That matters."

The meeting concluded shortly after, with the formal courtesies repeated in reverse.

He walked home afterward, turning the conversation over.

He had two weeks.

He was going to need every one of them.
