# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter Three Hundred and Twenty-Two — The Maintenance Burden

The list started as an exercise, something he'd sat down to do one evening after Fang Ruxue mentioned, in passing, that she wasn't certain anyone had ever fully catalogued exactly what the Strata City actually required to keep functioning day to day. By the time he finished it, three hours later, he understood why she'd never quite managed it herself.

Dimensional wall integrity — every corridor, every room, every fold he'd built across two years, requiring periodic reinforcement as the accumulated stress of daily use, temperature variation, and ordinary foot traffic slowly wore at structures that had never been designed to last indefinitely without attention.

Qi supply — the entire city's ambient field, drawn and distributed continuously, requiring careful balance between the workshop district's heavier demands, the laboratory's precision instrumentation, and the residential wing's simple, steady need for comfortable, livable warmth.

Environmental control — air quality, humidity, the manufactured daylight cycling through windows that weren't real but needed, nonetheless, to behave as though they were, all of it requiring constant, invisible calibration that no resident ever thought about because it worked, and it worked precisely because someone was always, quietly, making sure it did.

Waste management, water reclamation, security at the Strata's various thresholds, the ongoing maintenance of Amber-0's own substrate now that its unauthorized reroute had made clear the system needed closer monitoring than he'd initially planned.

He set the pen down, looking at the full list, and felt something settle into his chest that was less like fear and more like a specific, sobering weight he hadn't fully anticipated.

"I built a city," he said, when Chen Yufei found him still at the table an hour later. "I don't think I fully understood, until tonight, that building a city means you're also responsible for everything a city needs to keep existing, every single day, forever."

She looked at the list, her expression shifting as she read through it. "This isn't a one-time project anymore."

"No. It's never going to be a one-time project again. Every one of these items renews itself daily. There's no finished state. There's just — maintained, or not maintained, and the difference between those two things is attention, distributed constantly, indefinitely."

---

He raised it with Fang Ruxue the following morning, the two of them working through the list together with the specific practical focus she brought to any problem that could be solved through structure rather than heroics.

"This isn't something you should be carrying alone," she said, reviewing the categories with the same efficient precision she'd brought to the governance framework months earlier. "Some of this belongs to specialized teams — Liang Jingzhi's geological monitoring already covers part of the structural integrity work. Dr. Shen Yue's laboratory can absorb the environmental physics oversight. What's left, distributed properly, is considerably smaller than what you've written here as though it's all yours."

"It felt like it was all mine when I was building it."

"Building and maintaining are different labors," she said. "You did the building. The maintaining shouldn't fall to one person indefinitely, no matter how capable that person is. That's not sustainable, and more importantly, it's not necessary. You have a city full of people now. Distribute the work the way any functioning city distributes it."

He sat with that, understanding the wisdom in it even as some old, familiar instinct resisted the idea of letting go of responsibility he'd carried alone for so long.

"I don't know how to do that without it feeling like I'm abandoning something," he admitted.

"You're not abandoning it. You're finally treating it like the actual city it's become, instead of the personal project it used to be." Fang Ruxue's tone softened slightly, the practical efficiency giving way, briefly, to something warmer. "You built something that outgrew what one person should carry alone. That's not a failure. That's success, doing exactly what success is supposed to do."

---

He spent the following week working with her to distribute the list properly — structural monitoring to Liang Jingzhi's team, environmental oversight folded into the laboratory's existing responsibilities, a rotating maintenance schedule for the Dimensional Weaving work that, for the first time, didn't depend entirely on his own daily attention.

It didn't disappear. He understood, watching the distributed system settle into place, that it never would — the unglamorous, constant, distributed cost of having built something real was simply the price of the thing existing at all, a cost that would never be fully paid off, only managed, continuously, by more hands than his own.

He wrote about it that evening, the entry brief but honest.

> *The Strata City doesn't have a finished state. It has a maintained state, renewed daily, forever. I spent two years building. I'm only now understanding that building was the easy part. This is the part that actually never ends, and I think learning to share it, rather than carry it alone, might be the most important lesson this entire arc has tried to teach me.*
