# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 367 — What the Surveyor Is

"It isn't a person," Meen-4 said. "You should stop imagining it as one. It will make the review harder, not easier."

They were in the Archive's outer chamber, the one with the low chitin-ribbed ceiling that always made Lin Mushi feel, faintly, like he was standing inside something that had once been alive and was now merely very well preserved. Meen-4 had brought him here deliberately, he suspected, because it was a room built entirely by Formican hands, no human materials anywhere in it — a reminder, maybe, of scale, before she told him something that required scale to understand.

"Then what is it," he said.

"A consensus-entity." She let the term sit for a moment, the way she did when she wanted him to feel its edges before she filled in the middle. "Not one mind. A gestalt — representatives from multiple member civilizations, folded into a single operating structure for the duration of a review. Each representative contributes judgment, memory, precedent. The Surveyor speaks with one voice because it has been built to, not because it is one thing."

"How many civilizations."

"I don't know precisely. The number varies by assignment, and it is not customary to disclose it. Historically, between six and thirty."

He tried to picture it and found the picture didn't hold together — not one alien intelligence, vast and singular, the shape every instinct he'd inherited from decades of films and stories reached for automatically, but something more like a standing committee that had learned, over millions of years, how to think as a single deliberative organ without losing the individual perspectives that fed into it. Less a god. More an institution wearing the grammar of a person because that was the most efficient interface for the conversation it needed to have.

"That's why the notation felt legal," he said slowly. "Not because it was being cold. Because it actually is procedure. Every phrase is load-bearing because it has to satisfy — how many parties agreed on that wording?"

"Correct." Something in Meen-4's posture eased slightly, the way it always did when he arrived at an answer through his own reasoning rather than needing it handed to him. "The Surveyor is polite because politeness, at that scale, is efficient. It is bound by process because process is the only mechanism that lets thirty different value systems act as one without collapsing into thirty separate demands. It is not malevolent. I want to be very clear about that, because I have watched you prepare for threats before, and I do not want you preparing for the wrong one."

"What's the right one, then?"

"Indifference dressed as thoroughness," she said. "It will ask you everything. It will not rush. It will not lie to you, because lying is inefficient for an institution that intends to exist for another forty million years and cannot afford a reputation for it. But it also will not save you out of kindness. If Earth fails this review, the Surveyor will not feel grief. It will simply record the outcome and move to the next zone on its schedule."

He absorbed that in silence for a while, running a hand along the ribbed wall, feeling the faint temperature difference between the chitin and the air — cooler than he expected, always, no matter how many times he touched it.

"So it's not the enemy," he said finally.

"No. It may, in fact, be the closest thing Earth has to a fair witness in this entire process. Everyone else who cares about the outcome has a stake in it. The Surveyor's only stake is that the review be conducted correctly." She paused. "Which is not nothing. A fair witness, in a proceeding where the alternative outcome is sterilization, is not a small thing to have."

"And the Containment Orthodoxy? Are they part of the consensus too?"

Something shifted behind Meen-4's dark, faceted eyes — a flicker he might have missed three years ago, before he'd learned to read the small differences in how still she went when a subject mattered more than she wanted to admit. "They are a faction within the Collective," she said. "Not the Surveyor itself. But they will have submitted their position for the Surveyor's consideration, and their position carries institutional weight whether or not you find it reasonable." She let out something that in a human would have been a sigh. "We will discuss them. Not today. Today I wanted you to understand what you are actually speaking to, before you learn what it might decide."

He nodded, and looked once more around the low chitin-ribbed room, built by hands that had understood, long before humans had language for it, exactly what it meant to be reviewed by something larger than yourself.

"Thank you," he said. "For telling me the true version instead of the reassuring one."

"You have never once preferred the reassuring version," Meen-4 said. "I would find it strange to start offering it to you now."
