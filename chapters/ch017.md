# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter Seventeen — A Visit Home

The train to Leshan took an hour and twenty minutes.

He took the window seat and watched the Chengdu basin flatten and then the land begin its slow lift toward the mountains that were visible today as pale shapes above the haze, and he thought about nothing in particular, which was useful. He had been thinking about specific things for twenty-two days. The train allowed him to think about the window.

His mother met him at the station with the expression she reserved for him in person, which was different from her phone expression — warmer, and also more assessing, the way people who love you look when they've been monitoring you remotely and now finally have full information to work with.

"You're thin," she said.

"I'm the same."

"You're thinner." She hugged him anyway. She smelled like the kitchen at home. "Come. I made lunch."

---

Her apartment was on the fourth floor of a block built in the nineties, solid and slightly damp in winter, with a view of the road and the small park across it where old men played cards in the afternoons. He had grown up in this apartment through secondary school. He knew the layout well enough to navigate it in the dark, which he had done as a teenager and which now seemed like the only relevant credential he carried.

He stepped inside and the Qi hit him like stepping into warm water.

Different from Chengdu. Quieter. Slower. In Chengdu the filament network ran with a directionality that suggested urgency — something going somewhere, following a path with intention. Here the energy moved in something closer to pooling: no strong heading, just a warmth that had accumulated in the space over decades of habitation, finding the low spots and settling.

The warmth was thickest in the kitchen.

He stood in the kitchen doorway while his mother arranged lunch, watching her move between the counter and the stove with the economy of someone who had performed the same sequence ten thousand times, and the Qi in the room was so much softer than anything he'd encountered in the past three weeks that for a moment he wasn't sure what he was perceiving.

People left traces in spaces.

His mother had left thirty years of herself in this kitchen. Not metaphorically. The filament pattern here was shaped by habitation, by presence, by something he didn't have language for yet — the warm impression of a life lived consistently in one place.

"Sit," she said, without turning around. "You're standing in the doorway like you're deciding whether to stay."

He sat at the kitchen table.

---

Lunch was rice congee with pickled vegetables and the particular version of braised pork that she had been making since he was six years old and that he had never been able to replicate and had stopped trying to replicate because the inability was somehow meaningful. She ate with the focused attention she brought to meals and asked him questions between bites.

"Are you sleeping better?"

"Some."

"Are the noodles downstairs still good?"

"Adequate."

"That's not the same as good." She poured him more tea. "You look like you've been thinking hard about something."

"I have been."

"Is it something you can tell me about?"

He looked at his rice congee. The Qi in the kitchen pooled around them, soft and steady. He could feel the difference in his own body — the ambient energy here was not the same frequency as the filament network at the store, and the contrast after three weeks was noticeable, like taking off shoes you didn't realize were too tight.

"Not yet," he said. "I'm still understanding it myself."

She nodded. She had always accepted this answer — had accepted it when he was twelve and staring at the pigeon flight patterns, and when he was sixteen and briefly convinced he'd found a proof of something he hadn't been able to articulate, and when he was nineteen and left university in a way that must have looked to her like falling off the edge of something solid. She had watched him work through things in silence many times.

"As long as it isn't something bad," she said.

"It isn't bad."

"Then eat your congee."

He ate his congee.

---

After lunch she showed him the garden on the building's small rooftop terrace, where she kept a container garden that had become increasingly serious since his father had left — tomatoes and herbs and a stubborn eggplant that she narrated for him with the devotion usually reserved for grandchildren.

He watched her explain the drainage problem she'd solved with a layer of gravel and noticed, as she gestured at the containers, that her hands looked older than he remembered.

He had noticed sub-verbal information in her phone voice three weeks ago and not known what it was. Standing next to her in the afternoon light with the Qi moving slowly through the rooftop's open air, he could perceive something more specific: a slight unevenness in the energy around her lower back, a place where the warmth ran inconsistently, as if something there was not quite moving as freely as the rest.

Her back.

She had mentioned it on the Sunday call. A month of intermittent pain. The doctor had said stretching. He hadn't pushed.

He said, carefully: "How's the back?"

She looked at him. "I mentioned that on the phone."

"You said it was fine."

"It is fine." She turned back to the eggplant. "I stretch every morning. The doctor said it's muscular."

"Okay."

"You're not going to look things up?"

"I wasn't planning to."

She turned back to him again with the expression that meant she was recalibrating. "You seem different," she said. "From the phone."

"How?"

She considered this seriously, the way she considered things she meant. "More settled," she said finally. "Like you've made a decision."

He had made a decision. Twelve days ago, he had written *I am going to stop treating this as a possible symptom* and something had shifted in him that had been continuing to shift ever since.

"I have," he said.

"Good," she said, and didn't ask what it was.

---

He took the evening train back to Chengdu.

He sat in the window seat with the dark outside now instead of the haze, the mountains invisible, the lights of small towns flicking past in the gaps between hills. He had his observation notebook on the tray table but he didn't open it.

He thought about the kitchen. The accumulated warmth of thirty years of his mother cooking and eating and cleaning and sitting at the table with her tea on Sunday mornings while she called him. The way the Qi had pooled there — not because of the filament network but because of the habitation. A human presence, sustained and consistent, had shaped the energy in that space.

He thought: the watch-repair shop. Auntie Zhang's father saying *something was in there that shouldn't have been in the rubble.*

He thought: what a person leaves in a space is not nothing.

He opened the notebook. He wrote:

> *Visit home. Leshan.*
>
> *Observation: Qi in long-inhabited spaces has a different character from the filament network. Less directed. More accumulated. Shaped by presence over time.*
>
> *Mother's kitchen: thirty years of habitation, consistent use. The warmth there is not from the ley-line tributary — it runs much weaker under Leshan than under central Chengdu. The warmth comes from something else.*
>
> *Working hypothesis: biological systems generate a low-level Qi output. Over time, this output accumulates in frequently occupied spaces. The accumulation is specific to the habits and patterns of the person — the kitchen is warmer than other rooms because that's where she spends the most time.*
>
> *Her back: uneven energy flow in lumbar region. Consistent with her description of intermittent pain. Whether this is cause or effect I don't know yet.*
>
> *Gap between my inner world and what I can share with her: significant. She noticed I seem settled. She didn't ask what about. She trusted me.*
>
> *I am going to carry this gap for a while. That seems right.*

He capped the pen.

The train moved through the dark. He watched his own reflection in the window, slightly transparent over the passing lights outside, and thought about what it meant to leave traces in places you loved.

Home arrived at him from the direction of a kitchen where the air had been shaped by thirty years of his mother making lunch.

That was real.

He could perceive it now.

He didn't need to understand it yet to know it was worth protecting.
