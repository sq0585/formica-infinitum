# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 370 — What Containment Orthodoxy Means

Meen-4 confirmed the shape of it within a day, which told him, more than anything she said outright, how seriously she was taking Lady Shen's warning.

"They are not new," she said. They were in the Archive again, but a different chamber this time — one Lin Mushi hadn't been shown before, its walls lined with something that looked, in the low light, like pressed layers of translucent chitin arranged in dense concentric rings, each ring subtly different in color. Historical record, he guessed. He didn't ask what it recorded. Not yet.

"The Containment Orthodoxy has existed, in some form, for longer than the Collective has kept formal records of internal factions," Meen-4 continued. "Their founding position is simple, and I want you to hear it stated plainly, because it is easier to fight a caricature than a real argument, and I do not want you fighting a caricature."

"Go ahead."

"Any Quarantine Zone civilization that develops dimensional technology past a specific threshold constitutes an unacceptable contamination risk. Not because the civilization is malicious. Not because its intentions are bad. Because the technology itself — dimensional folding, Veritonal manipulation at scale, anything that touches the structures the Void Lattice depends on — carries risk regardless of the hand that wields it. Competence does not reduce the danger. In their view, it increases it."

He turned that over slowly. "So it's not about me being dangerous. It's about the category I'm in."

"Correct. And this is the part I need you to actually absorb, not merely acknowledge." Meen-4's antennae angled toward him with an intensity he associated with her most serious lessons. "They are not wrong that the risk is real. Veth-Karun happened. A Formican civilization, more advanced than anything currently on Earth, reached a threshold of power without full understanding, and the consequence was catastrophic enough that this entire zone has been quarantined for forty million years as a direct result. The Orthodoxy's position exists because that catastrophe was real, and because it has happened before, in other zones, under other names. Their remedy is monstrous. Their premise is not."

"Their remedy is genocide," he said, quietly, "dressed as prevention."

"Yes. And I am not asking you to forgive that. I am asking you to understand it, because an enemy you understand is one you can actually argue against, and an enemy you only despise is one you can only fear." She paused. "The Surveyor will weigh their submission on its logical merits, not its cruelty. If you want to defeat it, you cannot simply be more sympathetic than they are. You have to be more correct."

He thought of Veth-Karun — the clinical facts he'd assembled over months of study, the connection to Ren Wuji's death, the untranslated symbols still sitting in his notebook like a locked door he had not yet earned the key to. He thought of the dissolution itself, the thing that had ended an entire civilization and left this quarantine standing for forty million years afterward as a scar no one had been willing to let heal.

"They're afraid of the thing that killed Ren Wuji's people," he said slowly. "And they think I might become the thing that does it again."

"Yes."

"Do you think that."

Meen-4 was quiet for longer than he expected, long enough that he understood the answer would not be simple.

"I think," she said finally, "that the difference between you and the civilization that produced Veth-Karun is that they never asked the question you have spent three years asking. What happened. Why. How to make sure it does not happen again. They reached power and stopped being curious about its cost. You have never stopped." She turned toward the concentric rings on the wall, layered chitin recording something ancient he still hadn't been told. "Whether that difference is enough to satisfy a review body built by institutions who have watched forty-seven zones fail before this one — I do not know. But it is a real difference, Lin Mushi. Not a comforting fiction. A real one. Hold onto it. You are going to need it."

He looked at the rings himself, trying to read a pattern he didn't yet have the vocabulary for, and thought — not for the first time, and not, he suspected, for the last — that understanding a threat correctly was its own kind of armor, even when it did nothing to make the threat smaller.

"Teach me to read this room," he said, nodding at the walls. "If I'm going to argue against forty million years of precedent, I should probably understand where the precedent came from."

Meen-4's antennae dipped, something that in her was close to approval. "That," she said, "is exactly the right question to ask next."
