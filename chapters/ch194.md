# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter One Hundred and Ninety-Four — Recovery, and Something Else

He stayed for three days.

He made tea badly, despite his best intentions on the first morning — he had paid attention to the steeping time and somehow still produced something Chen Yufei described, with the dry humor that returned as her strength did, as "aggressively mediocre." He cooked simple meals that were, by his own honest assessment, functional rather than good. He sat with her through the long, slow hours of recovery, reading aloud from whatever was in front of him because she'd mentioned, on the second day, that she found it easier to rest when there was something to half-listen to.

What was in front of him, mostly, was Formican agricultural theory — the same fourteen-thousand-year-old crop rotation records she'd been working through for weeks, since he'd brought a stack of containers from the archive in case she wanted something to do during the parts of the day when she felt well enough to engage but not well enough to move much.

He read about soil rotation cycles. He read about a Formican irrigation system that predated human agriculture by an order of magnitude. He read about a specific historical dispute, documented in painstaking detail, between two Formican agricultural communities over water rights, resolved not through force but through a complex multi-generational arbitration process that he found, despite the dry subject matter, genuinely fascinating.

Chen Yufei, lying on the couch with her eyes closed, said at one point: "This is the most boring recovery I've ever had."

"Is that a complaint?"

"No," she said, and he could hear, even without opening his eyes to check, that she was almost smiling. "It's the best kind of boring. Keep going."

He kept going.

On the third evening, her Qi circulation had settled back into its correct routing — he could feel it clearly now, the smoothness restored, the strain gone. She sat up properly for the first time in days, looking steadier than she had since the confrontation.

"I think I'm actually recovered," she said.

"I think so too," he agreed, setting down the agricultural record he'd been reading from.

They sat in the quiet of her apartment for a moment, the three days of careful, unglamorous care settling into something neither of them had quite named yet, though both of them, by now, understood it was there.

"Thank you," she said finally. "For staying. You didn't have to."

"You bought me the seconds I needed in that hall," he said. "Without even telling me what it was costing you. I think 'staying for three days and making mediocre tea' is a fairly small repayment by comparison."

She looked at him for a long moment.

"It wasn't small to me," she said quietly.

He held her gaze.

Neither of them said anything else specific about what was developing between them — not that night, not in the days that immediately followed. But something had shifted, in the quiet accumulation of three days spent reading aloud about ancient irrigation disputes and making badly steeped tea, that neither of them was going to be able to pretend hadn't happened.

He went home that night for the first time in three days.

He found himself, walking back through the Chengdu evening, thinking about her more than he was thinking about the Northern Peak Court, the assembly, the ultimatum, or any of the political machinery currently in motion around them.

He noted this, briefly, in his notebook, before deciding — the same instinct that had let the silence in the library three weeks ago simply be what it was — that some things didn't need to be analyzed to be understood.

He let it be what it was.
