# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter Two Hundred and Thirteen — The Ley-Line Crisis

The alert came through the Circuit network on a Tuesday afternoon, buried among the ordinary status pings he'd grown accustomed to ignoring — until the pattern underneath it resolved into something that made him set his tea down and read the readout twice.

Western Chengdu's Under-Web node cluster was showing overload stress.

He pulled up the historical comparison, the months of baseline data his network had quietly accumulated since the early Circuit deployments. The pattern was unmistakable once he looked for it: the node cluster's pressure readings had been climbing steadily for three weeks, a slow accumulation he hadn't caught earlier because he'd been watching for sudden anomalies rather than gradual drift.

He understood the cause before he finished the analysis.

Routine maintenance. The Under-Web required regular resonance adjustment — small, deliberate corrections that kept Qi flow distributed evenly across the node network rather than pooling unpredictably at points of least resistance. The Courts had always handled this, quietly, as part of the infrastructure work that justified their territorial claims over the nodes in the first place. It was unglamorous, essential, the kind of labor nobody noticed until it stopped happening.

Nobody had been doing it for three weeks.

The Courts were distracted — fracturing into factions, drafting ultimatums, watching entrances, recalculating alliances. Maintenance protocols that depended on coordinated, voluntary labor from people currently busy fighting each other had simply stopped, quietly, without anyone deciding to stop them.

He checked the projection twice before he trusted it.

Six to eight weeks before critical failure, if the current drift continued unaddressed. Critical failure meaning, as far as he could reconstruct from the Archive's notation on the subject, an uncontrolled release of accumulated pressure through whatever path offered least resistance — surface manifestation, most likely, given the cluster's proximity to populated ground above it.

He sat with the number for a long moment.

---

He wrote it in his notebook before he said it out loud to anyone, the way he tried to do with things he needed to understand fully before he let other people's reactions shape his own.

> *Three weeks of drift, caused by Court infrastructure neglect during the political fracture. Six to eight weeks to critical failure if unaddressed. This is a direct, traceable consequence of releasing the comparison documents — the political disruption I created has a maintenance cost nobody was paying before, and now nobody is paying it.*
>
> *I didn't cause this by accident. I caused it by doing something necessary that had a cost I didn't fully account for. The Courts existed for reasons beyond hierarchy and authority. This is one of them. Infrastructure doesn't maintain itself just because the institution maintaining it deserved to be questioned.*
>
> *I don't get to feel righteous about disrupting something and then act surprised when the disruption has consequences. I need to decide: let the Courts fail visibly at the thing they were actually for, or take on the maintenance burden myself.*

He closed the notebook.

He already knew, sitting with the weight of the number, which answer he was going to choose. Letting the Courts fail visibly would prove a point. It would also mean an uncontrolled pressure release somewhere under a populated district, and he had never once, in two years of discovering what this world actually contained, found himself willing to let a point prove itself at that cost.

He thought about the two hundred qualified practitioners the Courts had, scattered across five institutions now consumed by their own fracture. He thought about his own network — Chen Yufei, Wei Canglan, the handful of Assembly members still learning the corrected framework, Fang Ruxue's small circle of Celestial Compass students.

Fewer than ten people, against a maintenance burden built for two hundred.

He picked the notebook back up and started a new page, the careful, methodical handwriting of someone beginning to map a problem he already knew was bigger than the resources he currently had to solve it.

It would have to be enough. He'd find out, over the following weeks, exactly how much enough actually required.
