# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 399 — The Vote

The formal vote opened two days after the Veth-Karun presentation, and Meen-4 explained, with the particular carefulness she reserved for information she knew would be hard to sit with, that it would take considerably longer than anything he'd waited on before.

"Eleven days," she said. "At minimum. Interstellar Veritonal transmission, even at its most efficient, requires time to reach every member civilization and carry their formal response back through the Witness Node. This is not the Surveyor deliberating. This is simply distance."

"Eleven days," he repeated, feeling the number settle into him with a particular weight — not overwhelming, not by the standards of everything else the review had already asked him to carry, but strange in its specific, unavoidable shape. Eleven days of no new information. Eleven days of a decision already made, somewhere across the galaxy, simply taking its time arriving.

"What do I do," he asked, "for eleven days?"

"Whatever you would do if the vote were not happening," Meen-4 said. "I mean that seriously. There is no additional preparation available to you now. The presentation is complete. The evidence has been offered. Anything further you might attempt in these eleven days would not change a single vote already being cast. It would only exhaust you before you need to be ready for whatever the result requires of you next."

He tried, in the days that followed, to actually follow that advice, with mixed and occasionally comic success.

He kept running the Strata's daily operations, because twelve hundred people still needed food distribution schedules coordinated and Circuit maintenance overseen regardless of what a distant vote decided. He kept the Under-Web's node monitoring current, kept the Pacific Ring's stability checks on schedule, kept doing the unglamorous, essential work that had nothing to do with the review and everything to do with the ordinary continuation of a civilization that had, somehow, kept functioning through every crisis of the past three years.

He ate at Xiao Dong's noodle shop twice that week — not because he was hungry, particularly, both times, but because sitting at the familiar counter, watching Xiao Dong argue cheerfully with a delivery driver about the price of green onions, offered a version of the world that had nothing at all to do with sterilization thresholds or coherence restoration or eleven days of unbearable distant waiting.

"You're not eating," Xiao Dong observed, the second visit, watching him push noodles around a bowl without much enthusiasm. "You're just holding the chopsticks so people don't ask if you're all right."

"Something like that."

"Big week?"

"Big week," he agreed, not elaborating, and Xiao Dong, who had learned over years not to push where pushing wasn't wanted, simply refilled his tea and let him sit in silence for as long as he needed to.

He was, by every account from the people around him, visibly nervous the entire eleven days — Chen Yufei noted it gently on day four, catching him staring at nothing in particular for the better part of ten minutes. Wei Canglan noted it more bluntly on day seven, setting a plate of food in front of him without comment, the way he did when words felt insufficient to the moment. Even Meen-4, on day nine, observed with something close to dry amusement that his Circuit diagnostics review had taken forty minutes longer than usual that morning, a delay she attributed, correctly, to the difficulty of concentrating on ordinary numbers while an extraordinary verdict slowly crossed an unimaginable distance toward him.

He did not sleep well, most of those nights. He found himself, more than once, standing at the window above the noodle shop in the small hours, looking out at a city that had no idea how much of its future currently hung suspended somewhere between here and a vast, patient body still weighing his life's work.

On the tenth day, his mother called, unprompted, the way she sometimes did when some instinct told her something was weighing on him that he hadn't mentioned.

"You sound tired," she said.

"I am."

"Waiting on something?"

"Something like that."

"Well." He could hear her moving around her kitchen, the familiar domestic clatter of a life continuing regardless of what he was carrying. "Whatever it is, it'll resolve or it won't, and either way you'll still need to eat tomorrow. Come by. I'm making the soup you like."

He went, the next evening, and ate the soup, and let one more ordinary hour pass without checking the Witness Node once, and found, in that small act of deliberate patience, something that felt almost like the last honest thing he had left to offer the eleven days still stretching ahead of him.
