# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 397 — What the Collective Is

Full access to the Surveyor's formal communications arrived quietly, almost as an afterthought — a procedural courtesy extended, Meen-4 explained, to zones whose review had progressed far enough that transparency about the review body's own internal structure no longer posed a meaningful risk of manipulation.

Lin Mushi spent three full days reading through it, in the low chitin-ribbed chamber that had become, over the past months, something close to a second home. Meen-4 sat with him for most of it, translating where his own fluency still faltered, answering questions as they arose rather than delivering the information as a single lecture, the way she seemed to understand he absorbed things best.

"I expected something more like a government," he admitted, partway through the second day, setting down a dense section of structural documentation. "A hierarchy. Someone at the top making decisions that flow downward."

"That is the most common misconception new zones bring to this stage of understanding," Meen-4 said. "And it is entirely reasonable, given how thoroughly the Surveyor's formal notation performs the appearance of unified authority. But the reality is considerably messier, and considerably more interesting, once you see it clearly."

The structure, as it slowly resolved for him across the documentation, was this: over two hundred member micro-civilizations, each occupying its own compressed pocket dimension, distributed across a galaxy too vast for any single authority to meaningfully govern. No central government. No enforced hierarchy. Simply an agreement, ancient and continuously renegotiated, held together by shared interest and mutual deterrence rather than any capacity for one member to compel another.

"It's not an empire," he said slowly, working through the implications. "It's not even really a federation, the way I understood that word. It's more like — a very long-running agreement between neighbors who've decided cooperation costs less than conflict."

"That is a reasonably accurate characterization," Meen-4 said. "And it explains a great deal about how this review has actually functioned, if you look back at it with this understanding in place. The Orthodoxy is not a majority faction wielding institutional power over dissenters. They are a vocal minority — persistent, well-organized, historically influential in certain kinds of proceedings, but a minority nonetheless. The framing vote you watched several weeks ago was not a formality. It was a genuine contest of persuasion, decided by the actual, distributed judgment of member civilizations who could have gone either way."

He sat with that, feeling something shift in how he understood the entire shape of the review he'd been fighting for months. He had been treating the Surveyor's consensus body as a monolith to be won over collectively, a single audience whose approval he needed to earn all at once. That had never been accurate. It had always been two hundred separate audiences, each weighing his case according to its own values and history, the Orthodoxy simply the loudest and most organized voice among many quieter ones.

"I don't need to convince everyone," he said slowly, the realization arriving with the particular clarity of a piece finally locking into place. "I need to convince the majority that's already doubtful of the Orthodoxy's position, not fully committed to mine. That's a completely different problem than the one I thought I was solving."

"Yes," Meen-4 said, something like approval settling into her voice. "That is, I believe, exactly the correct reframing. You have spent weeks preparing arguments aimed at an institution monolithic enough to require total capitulation. What you actually need is considerably more achievable — evidence compelling enough that the already-skeptical majority finds continuing to doubt you more costly than extending you the benefit you're asking for."

He thought of the Veth-Karun analysis, still incomplete, still built around symbols he had not yet earned the right to translate, and felt its importance sharpen into something more specific than it had been before. Not a single grand argument meant to overwhelm a unified judge. A genuine contribution, offered honestly, to two hundred separate parties who each needed their own reasons to believe extending trust was worth the risk.

"I need to finish the presentation," he said. "Not just complete enough to be technically thorough. Complete enough that the doubtful majority has something concrete to hold onto, instead of an abstract claim they have to take on faith."

"Yes," Meen-4 agreed. "And you have, I believe, precisely the material to make that case, if you are willing to show your work as thoroughly as your instincts have always led you to show it."

He nodded, already reorganizing weeks of scattered research in his mind into something sharper, more targeted, built not for a single vast judge but for two hundred quieter, more persuadable ones.
