# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter Two Hundred and Fifty-Six — What It Means for the Anchor

He brought it up three days later, carefully, the way he brought up anything that required her to understand a consequence before she agreed to anything attached to it.

"There's something in the Codex I haven't explained to you," he said. "About the Colony Link. I think, given where things are between us now, you deserve to understand it before it becomes relevant in a way that surprises you."

Chen Yufei set down the tea she'd been making and gave him her full attention, the particular focused stillness she brought to anything she sensed mattered.

"The Colony Link isn't just sensory," he said. "Document 9 describes a deeper structural function — something the notation calls an Anchor designation. A small number of people, formally bound into the Link at a level beyond passive sensing, become load-bearing points for my own continuity. Not metaphorically. Structurally. The Codex describes Carriers who lose every Anchor eventually losing coherent selfhood entirely — the distributed awareness fragmenting without enough fixed points to hold it together."

She was quiet for a moment, processing this with the same careful attention she brought to everything that genuinely mattered.

"You're telling me this is more than affection," she said slowly. "There's an actual mechanism."

"Yes. I haven't formally designated anyone yet. The mechanism requires consent, deliberately — the Codex is explicit that an unwilling Anchor produces instability rather than stability, the same principle as the Colony Link's broader consent protocol. I wanted you to understand exactly what you'd be agreeing to before I asked."

"And you're asking now."

"I'm asking now," he confirmed. "I'd like you to be my first Anchor. Not because of what happened three days ago specifically, though I won't pretend that's unrelated to my timing. Because I trust you more completely than I trust almost anyone else alive, and because I think you deserve to make this choice with full information rather than discovering the structural weight of it later, by accident."

She considered this for a long moment, turning the implications over with the careful precision he'd come to rely on in her.

"What does it actually mean for me? Practically."

"Practically, very little changes day to day. You already carry most of what an Anchor carries, informally — the trust, the proximity, the understanding. What changes is the structural weight underneath it. If something happened to you — permanently, I mean, not metaphorically — I would lose part of what currently holds my own continuity stable. Not immediately. Not catastrophically, with only one Anchor lost. But the architecture would be weaker, and depending on how many Anchors I eventually have and lose, the consequence could compound."

She was quiet again, and he watched her arrive, slowly, at the same blunt conclusion he'd already reached himself days ago.

"So if I die," she said, "you lose yourself."

"Eventually, yes. If I lose all Anchors, not just one. But yes, that's the honest shape of it."

"That's a lot to put on a relationship that's three days old."

"I know," he said. "You can decline. I'd understand completely, and it wouldn't change anything else between us. The Colony Link's baseline connection would remain exactly what it already is."

She set the tea aside entirely now, giving the question the full weight it deserved, and he waited, the way he'd learned to wait for her honest answers rather than rushing to fill the silence with reassurance she hadn't asked for.

"I'm not declining," she said finally. "I'm noting the weight of it. There's a difference between refusing something and understanding exactly how heavy it is before you pick it up anyway."

"That's fair."

"I want to," she said, more certainly now. "I've spent six months building trust with you under conditions considerably more dangerous than most relationships ever have to survive. I don't think a structural designation changes the fundamental fact that I already chose you, in every way that's mattered so far. This is just naming what was already true."

He felt something settle in his chest — not relief exactly, though relief was part of it, but something closer to the particular gravity of being trusted with someone's continued existence in a way he hadn't fully understood the weight of until he'd had to ask for it directly.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't thank me yet," she said, something dry returning to her voice, easing the conversation's weight just enough to be bearable. "Ask me again once you've explained exactly what the designation process actually involves. I have a feeling it's not simply a conversation over tea."

"No," he admitted. "It's somewhat more involved than that."

"Of course it is," she said, and reached for her tea again, ready, with the same steady, deliberate courage she brought to everything else in this impossible life she'd chosen, to hear exactly what came next.
