# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter Two Hundred and Thirty-Two — The Seismic Signal

The paper appeared in his news feed because he'd set up an alert months ago for anything involving unusual geological activity in the Chengdu region — a habit born from caution rather than any specific expectation, the kind of net he cast wide and mostly forgot about until something actually landed in it.

*Anomalous Subsurface Density Variations in Western Sichuan: A Preliminary Survey*, published in a respectable regional geology journal, authored by a name he didn't recognize: Liang Jingzhi, PhD candidate, Chengdu University Department of Geological Sciences.

He read the abstract first, the careful, hedged academic language of someone reporting results she clearly found significant but didn't yet have a confident explanation for. Then he read the full paper, twice, with growing and specific unease.

The density variations she'd mapped — using standard seismic survey equipment, nothing exotic, nothing that should have detected what it was apparently detecting — corresponded, point for point, with locations he recognized immediately from his own work. Strata anchor points. The physical geological locations where pocket dimensions across western Sichuan tethered themselves to the surface world.

He pulled up his own internal map, the one he'd built over months of exploring the Under-Web's broader network beyond the Chengdu Strata's immediate boundaries, and overlaid her published coordinates against it.

Eleven matches. Eleven anchor points, each one showing up in her data as an unexplained density anomaly, each one technically impeccable by the standards of conventional geology, each one a discovery that, if pursued by the wrong audience, could expose the physical footprint of structures that were supposed to remain entirely invisible to ordinary survey methods.

He sat with the paper for a long time, the cold, procedural clarity he'd learned to recognize settling over him.

This wasn't malicious. This wasn't anyone trying to find what she'd found. It was simply good science, applied carefully and honestly by someone with no idea what she was actually looking at, arriving independently at a discovery that two thousand years of cultivation secrecy had never anticipated a geologist with standard equipment might stumble into directly.

---

He thought about the timeline. The paper had been submitted three months ago, accepted two months ago, published this week — the slow, deliberate pace of academic publishing, which meant the data underneath it was older still, gathered sometime during the same period the Courts' political fracture had left ley-line maintenance unattended and the western cluster's pressure had begun its quiet, accumulating drift.

He checked the correlation carefully, cross-referencing her published density anomalies against his own maintenance logs.

The anomalies tracked, almost precisely, with the nodes under the heaviest stress. The unmaintained Under-Web, straining under months of neglected resonance correction, had apparently begun radiating a detectable signature into the surrounding geology — a consequence he hadn't anticipated, hadn't accounted for in any of his careful assessments of what the maintenance gap might cost.

He wrote it into his notebook, the weight of the realization settling slowly and completely.

> *Liang Jingzhi's paper is technically impeccable and will attract attention — it's exactly the kind of clean, unusual result that gets picked up by larger outlets, replicated by other researchers, eventually noticed by people with resources and motivations very different from a PhD candidate's honest curiosity.*
>
> *This is a direct consequence of the maintenance gap I'm already trying to close. The Under-Web's stress isn't just risking a localized failure anymore — it's becoming visible, from the surface, to anyone with a seismograph and the patience to ask the right question.*
>
> *I need to find her. Not to silence her — that's not who I want to be, and it likely wouldn't work regardless. I need to understand what she's seeing, and decide, carefully, what she deserves to know about what she's actually found.*

He closed the notebook and looked again at the paper's author photo, small and grainy in the journal's online listing — a young woman, serious-faced, the particular focused intensity of someone who had found something she didn't yet understand and clearly intended to keep pulling at the thread until she did.

He thought, not for the first time in the past two years, about the particular weight of bringing another person into a world they hadn't asked to discover.

He started composing the search query anyway. Some decisions, however careful they deserved to be, couldn't be delayed simply because they were difficult.
