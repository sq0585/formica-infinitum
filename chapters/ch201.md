## The Eternal Ant Legacy

### ARC III — THE COURTS AND THE CODEX

---

## Chapter Two Hundred and One — The New Landscape

The phone had thirty-one unread messages by the time Lin Mushi finished his tea.

Two weeks since Lady Shen's withdrawal. Two weeks of the cultivation world rearranging itself around a hole that used to be a settled hierarchy, and the rearrangement was not quiet. He'd learned to recognize the shape of it in his notifications before he even opened them — the cluster of messages that arrived within minutes of each other, the way certain names appeared and disappeared from group threads, the silence from accounts that had gone dark rather than pick a side.

He sat at the kitchen table and went through them in order.

Nine were from independent cultivators he'd never met, each asking some version of the same question: was the document real, and if it was real, what did that mean for them specifically. He answered the ones that seemed to be asking honestly. He ignored the ones that read like a Court testing whether he'd respond to an unfamiliar number.

Six were from Court-affiliated accounts requesting meetings. Three different Courts, three different tones — one formal, one conciliatory, one barely disguising the threat underneath the politeness.

Four were from Chen Yufei's network, forwarded without comment, which meant she wanted his read on them before she offered hers.

Eleven were noise. Spam, mostly, the kind that found any sufficiently public phone number eventually.

The thirty-first message had no sender name. No prior history. It said:

*You've started something you cannot finish. Good.*

He read it twice.

It wasn't a threat. Threats had a shape he'd grown used to recognizing — demands, deadlines, leverage stated or implied. This had none of that. It read like an observation made by someone who'd been waiting a long time for the observation to become possible.

He saved it.

---

Chen Yufei arrived at noon with rice and a folder of printed pages, the folder thicker than it had been the week before.

"The coalition's fracturing faster than I expected," she said, setting both down on the table. "Northern Peak's trying to hold the other four together with a unified response. It's not working."

"Define not working."

"Two Courts have stopped returning Elder Kuo's calls." She pulled a chair out and sat, already reaching for chopsticks. "The Celestial Compass School in particular. Their senior scholar's gone quiet in a way that usually means she's making her own decision instead of waiting for someone else's."

He thought about the Heaven Map reference buried somewhere in Document 4, a section he hadn't fully worked through. He wondered, briefly, whether the Celestial Compass tradition's astronomical cultivation was closer to the source material than the others — close enough that their scholars might recognize fragments of their own founding texts in what he'd released.

"What about the others?"

"Iron Meridian and Stone Hall are staying close to Northern Peak. Hard institutions. They'll close ranks before they question their own foundations." She ate for a moment, then added, without looking up, "River Jade's the interesting one. Nobody knows where they'll land yet."

"Five Courts splitting into camps that don't match their old alliances."

"Five Courts splitting into camps that match what each of them actually values, now that pretending otherwise isn't free anymore." She looked at him. "You did that. You should probably feel something about it."

He considered this honestly, the way he tried to consider most things she put in front of him.

"I feel responsible," he said. "I don't feel guilty. There's a difference, and I'm still working out where the line is."

"That's a fair answer."

He showed her the thirty-first message.

She read it, frowned, and read it again — the same double-read he'd given it.

"Whoever wrote that already knows you," she said. "Or thinks they do."

"That's what I thought too."

Outside, the city moved through an ordinary Tuesday — buses, lunch crowds, the particular grey light of a Chengdu sky deciding whether to rain. None of it knew that thirty meters below the convenience store on the corner, five institutions that had stood for two thousand years were quietly coming apart at a seam he'd cut without fully understanding the fabric.

He folded the message into the part of his attention reserved for things that would matter later, filed under: *find out who.*

It would have to wait. He had somewhere to be.
