# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 450 — His Mother's Decline — First Perception

She'd sounded fine on the phone. She always sounded fine on the phone, and he'd let that fact do more work over the past four years than, he understood now, it had actually earned.

He went to see her in person the following Sunday, properly, the way he used to before the review and the disclosure and everything after it had compressed his available hours into something he'd learned to spend carefully. Her apartment smelled the way it always smelled — ginger and something simmering, the particular warmth of a kitchen that had fed him his entire childhood — and she opened the door with the same brisk, practical delight she always greeted him with, pulling him into a hug before he'd fully crossed the threshold.

"You're too thin," she said, the same complaint she'd offered on the phone weeks ago from eighty million kilometers away, before he'd even properly returned. "Sit. I made too much soup on purpose, in case you finally remembered where I live."

He sat, and let himself, for the length of the meal, simply be her son rather than anything larger — laughing at her account of the new noodle shop's owner's ongoing feud with the parking committee, listening to an update on Xiao Dong's grandson's university applications, the small, specific texture of an ordinary life continuing exactly as it always had, patient and warm and entirely unconcerned with stellar mechanics.

It was only afterward, helping her clear the bowls, that his attention — still carrying, even at rest, the considerably wider range Stellar Scale had opened — caught something it hadn't caught before.

He stopped, a bowl still in his hand, and made himself look again, carefully, the way he'd trained himself to look at anything that registered as slightly wrong.

*Observation 431,* he wrote later, once he'd found a private moment to actually think it through properly. *Something in her cardiovascular rhythm reads inconsistent with what I remember from previous Sunday visits. Not alarming by any obvious measure — she moved normally, spoke normally, showed no visible sign of distress. But the underlying pattern, at the resolution Stellar Scale's refinement now lets me perceive, carries a specific strain I don't believe was present a year ago.*

He sat with the observation a long moment, the bowl still in his hands, cooling water pooling faintly at its bottom.

"You've gone quiet," his mother said, taking the bowl from him gently and setting it in the sink. "That's the thinking face. The one you've had since you were small, standing in the kitchen doorway working out some question you weren't ready to ask yet."

"I was just noticing you seem tired," he said carefully, choosing the least alarming true thing he could offer.

"I'm seventy-four," she said, with the particular dry practicality he'd inherited more of than he'd ever fully admitted. "Tired is mostly what seventy-four comes with. Nothing dramatic. My knees complain more than they used to, and some mornings my heart reminds me it's been working for a while now. The doctor says it's ordinary. I believe her."

He nodded, and let the conversation move on, because pressing further, right now, in her own kitchen on an ordinary Sunday, felt like exactly the wrong way to hold something this fragile.

But he sat with it, all evening, after he'd left — the specific, quiet wrongness his new perception had caught, and the uncomfortable, guilty math underneath it. Four years of careful Sunday phone calls, each one reassuring in exactly the ways phone calls were built to reassure, each one letting him miss whatever this was accumulating, slowly, in a body he loved and had not looked at closely enough in far too long.

*I should have visited more,* he wrote, and found the sentence, once written, harder to look at than almost anything else he'd recorded in five years of careful, disciplined observation. *Not because I could have fixed anything sooner necessarily. Because I might have known sooner. And knowing sooner is, I am only now understanding with any real weight, its own kind of love — one I let a comfortable, dishonest phone call quietly substitute for, four years running.*

He thought of the doctors she'd mentioned, the ordinary diagnosis she'd delivered so casually, and understood, sitting alone in the pocket dimension laboratory's warm light, that Sunday's ordinary meal had just quietly become the first entry in a chapter of his life he wasn't yet ready to name, but could no longer, honestly, pretend he hadn't started.
