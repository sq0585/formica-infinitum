# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter Thirteen — The Biochemist's Paper

He found the paper by accident, the way he found most things.

He had been looking for literature on biological electromagnetic sensitivity — whether human nerve tissue could in principle detect weak field gradients, the way some migratory birds were thought to — when one of the related-articles panels served him something from a different journal entirely. The title was: *Coherent Proton Tunneling in Peptide Bonds: Evidence for Quantum Biological Superconductivity in Neural Microtubule Networks.*

He clicked it because the word *superconductivity* caught him and he had not expected to see it next to *neural.*

---

The paper was from 2019. Three authors from a biochemistry department he didn't recognize, a fairly small journal, a modest citation count. He spent the first twenty minutes understanding what he was reading well enough to realize how much of it he didn't understand, which was most of it. He had no formal biochemistry background. He had physics self-education that stopped around the level of things he could follow in accessible papers, and biology that was patchy in the way of someone who had read a lot without a curriculum.

He read anyway. This was his standard operating mode with difficult material: read it once for shape, flag the terms he didn't know, read it again with the terms defined, look for the core argument underneath the technical language.

The core argument was this: in certain configurations of protein chains in neural microtubules, quantum coherence could be maintained for biologically significant timescales at body temperature — longer than conventional models said it should be possible. This coherence allowed proton tunneling across peptide bonds in a way that mimicked, at the molecular scale, the behavior of a superconductive material. Not actual superconductivity, the paper was careful to say, but a functional analog of it — energy transmission with near-zero loss along specific molecular pathways.

He read this paragraph three times.

Near-zero loss along specific molecular pathways.

He put the paper down. He picked up the Codex notebook and looked at the diagram.

The amber-gold lines. Moving slowly. The geometry at every scale hexagonal, nested, connected — not randomly, but according to rules he was still mapping. Lines that seemed to carry information in their direction and weight.

He put the notebook down. He picked up the paper again.

---

He spent the next six hours reading. He didn't track the time except by noticing when his tea went cold and making more, twice. The sun moved across the window and disappeared. The room got darker. He turned on the desk lamp and kept reading.

He understood perhaps forty percent of what he was reading at any given moment. He wrote questions in the margins of the printed page in pencil, small and precise, filling the white space:

*If coherence timescales are this long, what maintains them? Thermal noise should decohere this within picoseconds by conventional model — what's the mechanism of protection here?*

*The geometry of the microtubule is hexagonal. Is that load-bearing for the superconductive behavior or coincidental?*

*"Specific molecular pathways" — how are these pathways selected? Is it purely structural or is there some energetic gradient that determines which route the proton takes?*

He did not connect any of this to the Codex. Not yet. The paper was about biochemistry. He was reading it because the phenomenon it described was interesting on its own terms, and because he had learned years ago that the most useful thing you could do with a new piece of information was understand it as fully as possible before deciding what to do with it.

But the hexagonal geometry sat in his peripheral attention like a word you've been trying to remember that keeps almost arriving.

---

At 11 PM he set the paper aside and looked at what he'd written.

Seventeen marginal questions. A summary paragraph he'd drafted twice and crossed out once because it was missing something. A circled phrase: *coherent energy transmission through structured biological networks.*

He opened his observation notebook and wrote:

> *Paper: Coherent Proton Tunneling / Quantum Superconductivity in Neural Microtubules. (2019, [flagged for full reference later])*
>
> *Core finding: quantum coherence in nerve tissue allows near-lossless energy transmission along specific structural pathways. Geometry of the microtubule appears to be relevant to the function — hexagonal arrangement.*
>
> *What I'm not yet saying: this might be relevant to what the Codex is describing. Not saying it yet because I don't have enough of the Codex decoded to make the connection without speculation.*
>
> *What I am saying: biological tissue can carry structured energy in ways that existing models don't fully account for. If that's true, then the gap between 'this is a physical phenomenon' and 'this is a biological phenomenon' is smaller than I assumed.*
>
> *The warmth in my palm is not my imagination. It is a thing happening in my body. That thing might have a biochemical substrate. If it does, the biochemical substrate might be describable by something like this paper.*
>
> *Questions I cannot answer yet: (1) how does the energy enter the system? (2) what determines the pathways it moves through? (3) what happens to the system under sustained load?*

He capped his pen.

He had not connected the paper to the Codex. He had been scrupulous about that. Connection required evidence, and he had only proximity — a hexagonal diagram and a hexagonal geometry in nerve tissue, which was either convergent or coincidental and he could not yet tell which.

But the question was open now, and open questions in his experience had a way of not closing until they had been properly answered.

He filed the paper in a folder he labeled *Biochem/Biological Substrate*.

He made tea. He ate a bowl of instant noodles because it was late and he hadn't noticed until now that he was hungry.

Outside, the city was doing its nighttime things. Chengdu in October at 11 PM: distant traffic, the smell of hotpot from the restaurant on the ground floor, a neighbor's television murmuring through the wall.

Ordinary. Sustaining.

He washed his bowl and went back to the notation notebook.

The hexagonal geometry turned slowly on his phone screen, patient as a planet.
