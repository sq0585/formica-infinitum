# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter Forty — Gao

He had been avoiding the conversation for six weeks.

Not from cowardice — or not only from that. He had been avoiding it because he hadn't known what to say, and saying the wrong thing first would be harder to correct than saying nothing. Gao Yichen was not a person who forgot what you told him. He was an applied physics graduate student with the specific kind of intelligence that organized information efficiently and permanently, and once Lin Mushi put something into that organizational system it would stay there, indexed, retrievable, connected to everything else Gao knew about him.

He wanted what he told Gao to be accurate.

He waited until he was confident enough in his framework that he could describe it without significant error, and until the situation — the node depletion, the monitoring practitioner, the six-week timeline — had reached a point where not telling Gao was itself a kind of decision he hadn't made consciously.

He called him on a Wednesday evening.

"You alive?" Gao said on the third ring.

"I need to tell you something. Can you meet?"

A pause. In the pause Lin Mushi could hear Gao recalibrating — this was not the usual tone, this was a different kind of call. "The tea shop?" Gao said.

"Yes. An hour."

---

The tea shop had its terrible jazz playlist, which was playing something that might have been Miles Davis put through a wood chipper. The tea was good. Gao arrived seven minutes late, which for Gao meant he had run part of the way.

They sat across from each other. Gao looked at Lin Mushi with the attention of someone who had known him well enough and long enough to read the register-shift that had happened.

"You look the same," Gao said. "But you're not the same."

"No," Lin Mushi agreed.

"How long?"

"Six weeks."

"Since the man who died in the store?"

"Yes." He hadn't told Gao about the man. He had mentioned, in the first week, that there had been an incident at work. Gao had understood that the incident was what he was about to describe.

Lin Mushi put his hands flat on the table. He looked at them for a moment. Then he began.

---

He told it in order. Not every detail — the full documentation was nineteen notebooks and would take more than a tea shop evening — but in the correct structural order: what happened, what he observed, what he tested, what he concluded, what the evidence was, what the current situation was.

He described the Codex. He described the Under-Web. He described the depth beneath the store. He described the three open channels and what each did. He described the depletion, the monitoring practitioner, the timeline.

He described it factually, the way he'd describe an experiment. He did not editorialize.

Gao listened. He asked no questions during the telling, which was how Gao received technical information — he let it in first, asked questions after.

When Lin Mushi finished, Gao picked up his tea. He drank from it. He set it down.

"The battery anomaly," Gao said. "You measured the drain rate."

"Approximately."

"And the electromagnetic sensitivity with the gauss meter."

"Correlation of around 0.85."

Gao nodded slowly, the way he nodded when he was building an internal structure for information. "And the dark energy hypothesis — Veritonal Energy being the same phenomenon."

"Unverified. Working hypothesis."

"But the notation. It maps onto the quantum vacuum anomalies you'd expect if the field had structure."

"Yes."

"And you've decoded forty percent of the notation system."

"Higher now. Fifty-five, closer to sixty with the rhythm marker key."

Gao was quiet for a moment.

"Okay," he said.

Lin Mushi looked at him. "Okay?"

"I believe you." Gao said this without inflection, not as a dramatic concession but as a logical conclusion. "The gauss meter test isn't consistent with pure hallucination. The wall crack isn't consistent with coincidence. The cold room effect persisting isn't consistent with furniture placement being the only cause." He picked up his tea again. "I don't understand the mechanism. But the data is what it is."

"Most people would not say 'okay,'" Lin Mushi said.

"I'm a physicist. I believe anomalous data." Gao paused. "What do you need?"

Lin Mushi had not expected to feel anything specific when Gao said this. He felt something. He noted it and moved on.

"Right now: nothing you can directly help with. But I needed someone who knew. The situation is — there are people looking for the source of the anomaly I'm causing. I have approximately six weeks. I wanted someone in the world to have the correct information about what's happening in case I need backup thinking."

"Backup thinking," Gao repeated. "I'm your backup thinking."

"You're good at it."

"This is true." Gao thought for a moment. "The dark energy thing. If you're right about Veritonal Energy being the same phenomenon — the structure of the vacuum field has implications for quantum gravity. For the cosmological constant problem. For—"

"I know."

"You know and you're just documenting it in notebooks."

"I'm documenting it because I don't have enough information yet to do anything else with it. When I do, the question of what to do with it is one I will need to think about carefully."

Gao was quiet again. Then: "The ant colony question."

Lin Mushi looked at him.

"You used to talk about it. The colony that makes decisions no individual initiates." Gao tilted his head. "The people who built this — the Formicans — they named themselves after ants."

"Yes."

"And their system is based on the Colony Link. The distributed field."

"As far as I've understood it so far."

Gao set down his tea cup with the precise placement of someone making a decision. "You've been sitting with this alone for six weeks."

"Yes."

"That was probably necessary. Also you should have called me earlier."

Lin Mushi almost said *I know.* He said instead: "Yes. You're right."

This was accurate and they both knew it.

The jazz playlist shifted to something more recognizably structured. They sat for a while with the comfortable specific silence of two people who had known each other long enough to be quiet together.

Then Gao said: "What's channel four?"

Lin Mushi picked up his pen.

They talked for two more hours.

Outside, the city held its ordinary evening sounds, and somewhere to the north-northwest, beneath a convenient store that was now less depleted than it had been yesterday because he'd spent last week testing whether focused attention on the Phase Three exchange slowed the drain rate (it did, slightly), a vaulted space waited in the dark, hexagonal tiles patient under four hundred meters of Chengdu's ordinary sky.

He had told someone.

The situation had not changed. But something in it was lighter.

He paid for both teas, because Gao had run to get there, and they walked out into the November evening that had arrived while they weren't paying attention, and Lin Mushi went home to open channel four.
