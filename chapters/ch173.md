# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter One Hundred and Seventy-Three — The Weight of Other People's Choices

Wei Canglan integrated into the loose orbit around Lin Mushi and Chen Yufei over the following two weeks, slowly and without ceremony.

He started by helping Chen Yufei catalog the archive's agricultural records — a task that required patience and methodical attention more than cultivation capability, and that gave him something productive to do while he was, by his own quiet acknowledgment, not yet ready to think clearly about his own training or his own future. He was good at it. He had the same careful precision that had made him write a falsifiable verification request rather than a reactive accusation, months ago, and that precision translated well into the unglamorous work of organizing fourteen thousand years of preserved farming records.

Lin Mushi watched this happen without intervening much. He had learned, by now, that the most useful thing he could often do for someone in crisis was provide structure and let them find their own pace inside it, rather than directing their recovery according to his own sense of what recovery should look like.

He checked in periodically. He did not perform concern. He asked practical questions — *did you sleep,* *do you need anything from the surface,* *is the archive work interesting or are you just doing it to have something to do* — and let the answers be whatever they were.

Three weeks in, Wei Canglan asked to see the Strata properly, not just hear about it secondhand. Lin Mushi took him.

Wei Canglan's reaction to the vaulted hall was quieter than Chen Yufei's had been, months ago — less immediate excitement, more a kind of sustained, careful absorption, the reaction of someone whose capacity for wonder had been temporarily depleted by everything else he was carrying but who could still recognize, intellectually and then slowly emotionally, that he was standing somewhere extraordinary.

"This is what they were teaching a corrupted version of," he said quietly, standing in the main hall.

"Yes."

"My grandfather's scroll. It came from somewhere connected to this."

"Probably. The Northern Peak's foundational fragments likely trace back to contact with this station, or one like it, generations before the Court's formal founding."

Wei Canglan was quiet for a long time, looking at the scale of the hall, the columns disappearing into the vaulted dark above.

"I gave up my family's stability for the right to stand here," he said. Not bitterly. Just accurately.

"I know," Lin Mushi said. "I'm not going to tell you that makes it worth it. I don't think that's a calculation either of us gets to make on your family's behalf."

Wei Canglan looked at him.

"You keep doing that," he said. "Refusing to tell me it was worth it."

"Because I don't know if it was," Lin Mushi said honestly. "I know the document was true, and I believe sharing true things is, on balance, better than hiding them. I know your decision to speak publicly was made with clear eyes about the cost, which matters. But whether the specific outcome — your parents in a single room, your mother working again at fifty-three — was 'worth it' in some final accounting sense isn't something I can calculate for you. I think that calculation belongs to your family, made over years, not to me, made in a single conversation."

Wei Canglan absorbed this.

"That's harder to hear than reassurance would be," he said.

"I know," Lin Mushi said. "I think it's also more honest, and I think you specifically are someone who values honesty over comfort, or you wouldn't have written that first message asking me to verify a scroll that might prove you wrong."

Something in Wei Canglan's expression shifted — not quite a smile, but adjacent to one.

"That's accurate," he said.

They stood in the hall a while longer.

"I want to learn this properly," Wei Canglan said eventually. "Not the corrupted version. The actual thing. If I'm going to have given up what I gave up, I want to at least understand fully what I gave it up for."

Lin Mushi nodded.

"We'll start with the basics," he said. "The same way I started, fifteen months ago, behind a convenience store counter, not knowing what any of this was."

Wei Canglan looked around the hall one more time.

"I think I understand a little better now," he said, "what that must have felt like."

They began his training the following week.
