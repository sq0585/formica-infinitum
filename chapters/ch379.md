# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 379 — The Surveyor's Hesitation

For four days, nothing came through the Witness Node at all.

Lin Mushi had, by now, learned the Surveyor's rhythms well enough to notice the absence as its own kind of signal. Every prior exchange had arrived within hours, sometimes minutes — the calm, unhurried cadence of something that never needed to rush because it had already accounted for every possible response before sending the question that provoked it. Silence, from something that precise, did not read as neglect. It read as processing on a scale he had no reference point for.

"Is this normal," he asked Meen-4, on the second day, when the quiet had started to feel less like patience and more like held breath.

"No," she said simply. "I have no record of a pause this long between sessions, at any stage of any review I know of. I want to tell you that means something good. I do not actually know that it does."

He tried to work through it — Circuit diagnostics, Strata administration, an afternoon spent, deliberately, helping his mother repaint a wall in her apartment that did not need repainting, just to put his hands somewhere useful while his mind kept circling back to the silence. She noticed, of course. She always noticed.

"You're somewhere else today," she said, handing him a roller loaded with too much paint.

"A little."

"Work?"

"Something like it." He dabbed at a drip before it could run, grateful, not for the first time, that some problems were small enough to actually solve with a rag and a steady hand. "Waiting on someone to make a decision that affects a lot of people. I don't get a vote in when they make it."

"Ah." She said it with the particular flatness that meant she understood far more than she was going to ask him to explain. "Waiting is the worst part of everything. Worse than the bad news, sometimes, because at least bad news tells you where you stand." She reached over and fixed a section he'd rushed, without comment, the way she'd been correcting his sloppy work since he was small. "Eat something tonight. You get thin when you're waiting on things."

He ate something that night, and slept badly, and on the morning of the fourth day, the Witness Node stirred.

The transmission was brief, and it did not, at first, feel like an answer to anything.

*Elaborate on the proposed mechanism for Void Lattice coherence restoration in the Veth-Karun dissolution zone.*

He read it twice, and then a third time, and felt something shift in his chest that he did not immediately have a word for — not relief, not yet, something more like the specific vertigo of a door opening where he had expected, at best, a wall.

"Meen-4," he said slowly. "This isn't a question about the review anymore, is it."

"No," she said, and her voice carried something he had rarely heard in it — a controlled, careful astonishment, the sound of someone recalculating a probability she had considered essentially fixed. "This is a technical question. A research question. The kind of question one civilization asks another when it is beginning to consider what a working relationship between them might actually look like, rather than merely whether one should be permitted to continue existing."

"They're not asking whether to spare us anymore."

"I would not go that far. Not yet. The threshold has not formally shifted, and the Orthodoxy's counter-submission remains fully in play." She paused, weighing something. "But four days of silence, followed by a question this specific, about a mechanism rather than a threat — that is not how a review body behaves toward a zone it has already decided to close. That is how it behaves toward one it has started, cautiously, to find interesting."

He sat with the transmission a while longer, feeling the shape of the last week rearrange itself around this single unexpected turn — the flat accounting, the careful presentation, the Orthodoxy's cold and technically correct counter-argument, and now this: a question that assumed, implicitly, that there might be a future in which the answer mattered.

He did not yet have the answer. The Veth-Karun symbols remained untranslated, the full mechanism of the dissolution still a shape he could sense the edges of without holding its center. But for the first time since the Surveyor had anchored itself in Earth's magnetosphere, he let himself believe, cautiously, that the question itself was a door, and that his job, now, was simply to walk through it as honestly and as carefully as he had walked through every door before it.

"Draft me a response," he told Meen-4. "Honest about what we know. Honest about what we don't. No overselling. If this is the turn, I want to earn it, not perform it."

"That," Meen-4 said, "is exactly why it may be turning at all."
