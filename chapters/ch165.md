# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter One Hundred and Sixty-Five — Elder Kuo's Fury

Kuo arrived at Chen Yufei's apartment the next morning, unannounced, which was itself a departure from the careful procedural courtesy he'd maintained across every previous interaction.

Lin Mushi was there — they had agreed the day before that he would be present for whatever the institutional reaction looked like, rather than letting Chen Yufei absorb it alone.

Kuo did not sit when she opened the door. He stood in the small living room with the specific quality of a man holding himself in check through long practice rather than natural calm.

"Do you understand," he said, looking at Lin Mushi, "what you have done?"

"Tell me what you think I've done," Lin Mushi said. "I want to understand your view of it accurately before I respond."

Kuo's jaw tightened slightly — he had clearly expected either defensiveness or performed contrition, and received neither.

"You have simultaneously delegitimized the foundational authority of five institutions that have, between them, governed cultivation practice across this region for the better part of a millennium," he said. "Institutions that, whatever their flaws, provide structure. Training pathways. Dispute resolution. Resource allocation. Protection for vulnerable practitioners against more powerful ones." He paused. "You released this to everyone, simultaneously, with no preparation, no negotiated transition, no plan for what replaces the structure once it's gone."

"I haven't proposed that anything replaces it," Lin Mushi said. "I've shared accurate information about how a phenomenon works. What the institutions do with that information is their decision."

"That is a convenient abdication," Kuo said. "You know precisely what accurate information does to an institution whose authority rests on people believing it possesses unique, irreplaceable understanding. You are twenty-two. You are not naive. You knew this would destabilize."

Lin Mushi was quiet for a moment.

"You're right," he said.

Kuo, who had clearly braced for an argument, was thrown slightly off balance by the direct agreement.

"I knew it would destabilize," Lin Mushi continued. "I thought about this carefully before I sent it. I want to acknowledge what you're describing without minimizing it: stability built on people not having information they need is real stability, in the sense that it functions. People rely on it. Removing the foundation it sits on has real costs, to real people, who didn't choose any of this and are not responsible for the institutions' historical errors."

"Then why did you do it?"

"Because stability built on a wrong foundation isn't actually stability," Lin Mushi said. "It's a structure waiting to fail, on a timeline you don't control, in a way you can't prepare for. The Iron Meridian array under the mah-jong hall was running at thirty-five percent efficiency for forty years because no one understood the actual mechanism. The dead node beneath the 7-Eleven died, in part, because the system around it was being maintained by people working from incomplete information about what they were maintaining. Wrong foundations don't hold. They just fail later, with less warning."

Kuo was quiet.

"I'm not telling you this to win the argument," Lin Mushi said. "I'm telling you because I think you already know it. You verified the document's accuracy yourself, against materials your own Court possesses. You came here furious about the consequences, not the content. That tells me you don't actually dispute that the information is true."

Kuo looked at him for a long moment.

"No," he said finally. "I don't dispute the content."

"Then the argument we're having," Lin Mushi said, "is about timing and method, not about truth. Those are real and important questions. I don't think I got them perfectly right. I think I got them as right as I could, given that there was no version of releasing accurate information about a millennium of misunderstanding that wouldn't cause significant disruption."

Kuo sat down, finally, in the chair Chen Yufei had been quietly gesturing toward for the past several minutes.

"The disruption is going to be severe," he said, more quietly now. "Council sessions across all five Courts in the next week. Factional splits that have been suppressed for decades are going to surface. Some practitioners are going to lose standing they've spent lifetimes building. Some institutions may not survive this in their current form."

"I know," Lin Mushi said. "I've thought about that, and I don't think it absolves me of responsibility for what happens to the people caught in it. I think the responsibility is real, and I'm going to carry it, and I'm going to try to be useful in the aftermath rather than disappearing into the abstraction of 'I just shared information.'"

Kuo looked at him for a long moment.

"What does being useful in the aftermath look like?" he asked.

"I don't fully know yet," Lin Mushi said honestly. "I expect I'll find out as it happens. I expect some of it will look like this — listening to people who are angry, with reasons to be angry, and not pretending their anger is misplaced just because I believe I made the right choice."

Kuo was quiet for a long moment.

"I came here to tell you that you were wrong," he said. "I find I cannot fully say that. I can say that you were right and reckless simultaneously, which is a more difficult thing to hold than simple wrongness would have been."

"I think that's accurate," Lin Mushi said.

Kuo stood. He looked tired in a way that hadn't been visible when he'd arrived, the controlled fury having burned through to something more exhausted underneath.

"The Council session is in three days," he said. "I don't know what they'll decide. I wanted you to hear the cost from someone who has spent thirty years inside the structure you've just shaken, before you hear it from people with less restraint."

"Thank you," Lin Mushi said. "Genuinely."

Kuo left.

Lin Mushi sat with Chen Yufei in the quiet apartment for a while afterward.

"He's not wrong," she said finally.

"No," Lin Mushi said. "He's not."

They sat with that, together, for a long time.
