# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter Three Hundred and Thirty-Nine — Dr. Shen Yue's Paper, Rejected

The email arrived on a Wednesday, and Dr. Shen Yue read it standing at her laboratory station, her expression giving away nothing until she'd finished the full length of it.

"Rejected," she said, when Lin Mushi arrived twenty minutes later, having felt the shift in her mood through the Colony Link clearly enough to know something had landed. "As expected."

He studied her carefully, looking for the specific quality of disappointment beneath her composed exterior. "How do you feel about it?"

"Honestly? Fine." She turned the tablet toward him, showing the reviewer comments in their full, careful academic language. "Read the actual feedback. It's not dismissive. It's skeptical, which is different, and it's skeptical in exactly the places I expected it to be skeptical."

He read through the comments, recognizing, even without her expertise, the shape of what she meant — reviewers questioning the instrumentation's calibration, asking for additional controls, raising alternative explanations for the anomalous readings that, while ultimately incorrect, were entirely reasonable given what the reviewers actually had access to.

"They're not saying you're wrong," he said slowly. "They're saying you haven't ruled out every alternative yet."

"Precisely. And they're right that I haven't, within the bounds of what a single paper can reasonably address. This is how the process is supposed to work. I'd be more worried if they'd rejected it for sloppy reasoning rather than legitimate methodological questions." She set the tablet down, something almost satisfied in her tone despite the rejection itself. "I'm resubmitting immediately, with additional controls addressing their specific concerns. I've had a revised methodology drafted for two weeks, in anticipation of exactly this outcome."

---

He stayed with her through the afternoon, watching her work through the revision with the same rigorous, unhurried precision she brought to everything, and found himself genuinely admiring the specific quality of resilience she was demonstrating — not forced optimism, not denial of the setback's real weight, but a clear-eyed, patient persistence that treated rejection as data rather than as failure.

"You told me once you expected years of this," he said, watching her cross-reference the reviewers' specific objections against her revised control set. "Does the first rejection feel different than you expected, now that it's actually happened?"

"A little," she admitted, not looking up from her work. "Intellectually, I knew it was coming. Emotionally, there's still a small, human part of me that hoped, despite my own careful expectations, that the reviewers might simply see what I see immediately. They didn't. That's a small disappointment, even against expectations that should have fully absorbed it."

"That's honest."

"I try to be, especially about my own reactions. Pretending I feel nothing about a rejection would be its own kind of dishonesty, even if the disappointment is small and manageable." She finally looked up, meeting his eyes directly. "I'd rather you understand that I'm handling this well and that it still costs something, both at once, rather than pretending scientific patience means an absence of any feeling at all."

---

He found Fang Ruxue later that evening, curious whether the rejection carried any implications beyond Dr. Shen Yue's own research trajectory.

"It changes the timeline, not the direction," Fang Ruxue said, when he raised it. "I'd expected a longer runway before the paper reached public circulation regardless. This just confirms it. If anything, a careful, methodical resubmission process gives us more time to prepare for what happens once it eventually succeeds, rather than less."

"You still think it will succeed."

"I do. Dr. Shen Yue's data is sound, and sound data eventually finds its way through peer review, even skeptical peer review, provided the researcher keeps addressing the specific objections rather than abandoning the work." Fang Ruxue's tone carried the same practical confidence she brought to most institutional questions. "The real question isn't whether this paper eventually succeeds. It's whether we're using the time it takes wisely, or simply waiting passively for the outcome to arrive."

He thought about that, understanding, with growing clarity, that the rejection — however small a setback it represented to Dr. Shen Yue personally — had bought them something considerably more valuable than a delayed publication.

Time.

He wrote about it that evening, brief but attentive.

> *Dr. Shen Yue's paper: rejected, expected, already being revised. She handled it with more grace than I think I'd have managed in her position. Fang Ruxue's right that this buys us time rather than costing it. I don't yet know exactly what to do with that time. I think I need to find out quickly, given everything else this world has been quietly accumulating around us.*
