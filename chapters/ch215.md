# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter Two Hundred and Fifteen — Recruiting for Maintenance

He called the meeting for a Saturday afternoon, in the outer hall, and made sure everyone who came understood beforehand exactly what they were being asked into.

"I'm not selling this," he said, once the small group had settled — Chen Yufei, Wei Canglan, the two independent cultivators from the original assembly, Su Lan quiet in the back. "Forty-one nodes in the western cluster alone need resonance correction every three to four weeks or the accumulated pressure releases somewhere we don't get to choose. The Courts used to cover this with two hundred people. Right now I have fewer than ten, and most of those ten have other responsibilities already."

"What does the work actually involve?" one of the independents asked — quieter than the others, someone Lin Mushi had never quite gotten a clear read on.

"Stable Compression, held for roughly twenty minutes per node, performing a correction sequence I'll teach directly. It's not dangerous if done correctly. It's tedious, recurring, and offers nothing in the way of recognition. It will eat hours every week for as long as it needs doing, which is probably indefinitely."

He let that sit without softening it further. He'd learned, slowly, that people respected an honest description of a hard thing more than they respected a generous one.

"Why does it matter that it's us doing it instead of waiting for the Courts to sort themselves out?" Wei Canglan asked, though his tone suggested he already suspected the answer and wanted it said plainly.

"Because the Courts aren't going to sort themselves out in six weeks, and six weeks is roughly what we have before the drift becomes critical. This isn't a problem with a comfortable runway. It's a problem with a deadline that doesn't care whether the institutions responsible for it are currently busy fighting each other."

Silence settled over the group for a moment — not reluctant, he thought, but considering, the kind of quiet that preceded people actually deciding something rather than simply agreeing out of momentum.

"I'll do it," Wei Canglan said first, unsurprising. "I left Northern Peak because I didn't want to be part of an institution that protected its own authority over doing the actual work. This is the actual work."

"I'm in," the same cultivator said. "Tedious is fine. I didn't come here looking for glory."

The young Iron Meridian defector added his name a moment later, quieter, with the particular focus of someone still finding his footing in a community he hadn't fully trusted yet but was beginning to.

That was four, including himself.

---

Fang Ruxue arrived an hour later than the others, having been delayed by a Celestial Compass matter she didn't elaborate on, and listened to a condensed version of the same explanation Lin Mushi had given the group.

"I can offer three students," she said, without needing to be asked. "All stable at Compression, all trained well enough that I'd trust them with careful, supervised work. I'll vet the rotation schedule myself to make sure none of them are overextended."

"That's generous."

"It's practical," she said. "The Collective's credibility depends partly on demonstrating that an institution built outside the Courts can still do the unglamorous work the Courts used to claim as justification for their authority. This is exactly that demonstration, whether either of us intended it that way or not."

He hadn't thought of it in those terms, and found, turning it over, that she was right.

By the end of the afternoon, he had eight names committed to a rotation — barely sufficient against forty-one nodes, the margins tight enough that any illness, any unexpected absence, would strain the schedule past comfortable. But sufficient, if everyone held their commitment and nothing went badly wrong in the meantime.

He wrote the rotation schedule into his notebook that evening, eight names against forty-one nodes, and felt something shift quietly into place that had nothing to do with cultivation technique and everything to do with what an institution actually was underneath all its formal language.

Not authority. Not hierarchy. Just people, choosing repeatedly to show up for work nobody would ever thank them for, because the alternative was letting something dangerous go unattended.

It was, he thought, closer to the actual foundation of trust than anything the Courts had built in two thousand years of pretending otherwise.
