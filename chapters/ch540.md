# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 540 — Xiao Dong's Noodle Shop

He walked to Xiao Dong's third location on an entirely ordinary Tuesday afternoon, choosing his feet over any faster method the way he still, deliberately, chose ordinary things whenever the choice was genuinely his to make.

Xiao Dong had opened the new shop two streets over from the original, a modest, cheerful space that had, within its first month, developed the same easy, particular warmth the first location had taken years to earn. He was there when Lin Mushi arrived, apron on, arguing amiably with a supplier about the price of Sichuan peppercorns, and he broke off the argument the moment he spotted him, the same wide, unguarded grin he'd worn since long before either of them had any reason to suspect the other of being extraordinary.

"You're late," Xiao Dong said, which was not true, and which they both understood was simply his way of saying he was glad to see him.

"I walked."

"You could fold space directly into my kitchen and you still choose to walk," Xiao Dong said, shaking his head with the specific fond exasperation of a friend who had, by now, entirely made his peace with the strangeness of who he knew. "Sit. I have opinions about the broth today."

He sat, and listened, with genuine attention, as Xiao Dong described the day's batch — a specific concern about the broth's Qi resonance that would have sounded, six years ago, like an eccentric affectation and now sounded, entirely sincerely, like a legitimate culinary question. Xiao Dong had attended two workshops through the Cultivation Research Collective in the past year, entirely serious about it, for reasons that had nothing to do with cultivation as a discipline and everything to do with soup quality.

"The instructor kept trying to explain the deeper philosophical implications," Xiao Dong said, setting a bowl down in front of him. "I kept asking whether it would make the stock clearer. She found this very frustrating. I found it very productive. The stock is, in fact, clearer."

Lin Mushi laughed, and ate, the specific ordinary pleasure of a bowl of noodles made by a friend who had never once needed the extraordinary explained to him in order to simply enjoy a good result.

On the walk back, unhurried, he passed the old 7-Eleven's location — a bakery now, bright and unfamiliar, nothing left of the fluorescent-lit convenience store that had once, at 3:17 in the morning, held the whole beginning of everything he now was. He stopped a moment, the way he sometimes still did, and let himself simply look.

He thought, standing there, of an old woman who had once come through that door most mornings, slow and particular, whom he had held it open for more times than he could easily count, back when holding a door was the largest kindness his life had room for. She was gone now — had been gone some years, quietly, the way people were sometimes quietly gone from a life grown too large and too fast to notice every ordinary departure it should have paused for.

He had not been there for her ending. He understood, standing at the edge of what was now a bakery, that this was one of the costs the last six years had genuinely asked of him, and that he did not intend to pretend otherwise simply because so much else had gone so well.

She had been one small, ordinary anchor to the world he'd started in, among the many he'd carried forward instead. He held her memory a moment, gently, without dwelling in it longer than the moment actually asked for, and then continued walking, the bakery's warm bread smell following him a little way down the street before the ordinary afternoon carried it off entirely.

He reached the Strata's outer gate a while later, unhurried, entirely himself, and went inside to see what the rest of the day still held.

He thought, walking through the gate, of how many small, ordinary anchors a single life actually required in order to remain recognizably itself — not the five formal Anchors the Colony Link depended on, though those mattered more than almost anything else, but the countless smaller, quieter ones: a friend who cared more about broth clarity than cosmic philosophy, an old woman who had once accepted a held door without ever knowing what strangeness stood behind the boy holding it for her, a bakery now standing where a convenience store once had, indifferent to and untouched by everything that had begun there.

He did not think the ordinary anchors mattered less than the extraordinary ones. He had come, across six years, to suspect they mattered considerably more — that the whole vast architecture of Anchors and Colony Links and dissolution zone volunteers existed, in the end, in service of exactly this kind of afternoon: a bowl of noodles, a held door remembered, a walk home through a city that had never once stopped being, underneath everything it had also become, simply his.
