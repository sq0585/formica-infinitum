# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter Three Hundred and Eight — He Notices She's Counting

He caught her doing it three days later — not the pause itself, but the counting, a small, almost imperceptible motion of her fingers against the tabletop that he'd registered a dozen times before without ever once understanding what it meant.

They were eating breakfast, an ordinary morning, when Amber-0 surfaced a minor flag about workshop district power distribution and he felt his attention pull toward it for perhaps four seconds before returning. In that brief window, he saw her fingers tap once against the table's edge, quick and small, the gesture so practiced it barely registered as deliberate.

He set his chopsticks down.

"You're still counting."

She went still, caught in a way that made something in his chest tighten uncomfortably. "I told you I was counting. I didn't say I'd stopped."

"I thought — after the conversation, I thought you'd stop. Once I knew, once I was trying."

"You are trying," she said carefully. "I can see that you're trying. It's better than it was two weeks ago. But it's not gone, and I don't think pretending I've stopped noticing helps either of us."

He sat with that, the specific discomfort of being caught in something he'd genuinely believed he'd already addressed settling into him slowly.

"How many today?"

"Two, so far. It's early." She said it without cruelty, just fact, the same flat honesty she'd used weeks ago when she first raised it. "I'm not trying to make you feel like you're failing at this. I want to be clear about that. I'm trying to be honest instead of quietly resentful, because I've watched what quiet resentment does to people, and I'd rather tell you the truth even when the truth isn't fully resolved yet."

---

He turned the observation over for the rest of the morning, working through the laboratory's routine maintenance with only half his attention actually present, the other half caught on the specific, uncomfortable shape of what she'd said.

He found her again that evening, deliberately, choosing his words with more care than he usually needed to.

"I want to say something honestly," he said, "and I want you to hear the honesty in it rather than the discomfort, because I think the honesty matters more."

She waited, giving him the space to find it.

"Sometimes I'm not sure if I'm talking to you or to a process," he said. "That's not what I want. It's just — accurate, about what happens in those four or five seconds. Part of me is here, with you, and part of me is somewhere else entirely, managing a network I built specifically so I could be more present for the people I actually care about, and instead it's doing exactly the opposite of that, at least some of the time."

Something in her expression shifted — not relief exactly, but the particular loosening that came from finally hearing a thing named as clearly as she'd been naming it herself.

"That's honest," she said quietly. "Thank you for saying it that plainly instead of a softer version."

"I don't have a solution yet," he admitted. "I don't know if there is a clean one. The Colony Link isn't something I can partially turn off during dinner. It's not built that way."

"I know. I'm not asking you to turn it off." She reached for her tea, holding it without drinking, the gesture more about having something to hold than about the tea itself. "I think I'm asking for something smaller than a solution. I think I'm asking to keep being told, honestly, when it's happening — rather than either of us pretending it isn't, or me being the only one keeping score."

"I can do that."

"Can you? Actually?" She met his eyes directly, the question carrying real weight rather than doubt for its own sake. "Because I don't want a promise you make because it's the right thing to say tonight. I want to know if it's something you can actually sustain, given everything else you're carrying."

He considered the question with the seriousness it deserved, rather than reaching for the reassuring answer.

"I don't know if I can sustain it perfectly," he said finally. "I think I can sustain trying, and telling you honestly when trying isn't working. That's the actual truth, rather than the comfortable version of it."

Something in her posture eased at that — not fully, not completely, but enough that he understood the honesty itself had done real work, even without resolving the underlying problem.

"That's enough," she said. "For now. That's enough."

They finished the evening quietly, no further pauses that either of them counted, though he suspected, watching her hands rest still against the table for the rest of the night, that the counting itself hadn't fully stopped — only paused, the same way his own attention paused, waiting for whatever came next in a problem neither of them had yet found the shape of a real solution to.
