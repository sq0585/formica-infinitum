# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter Three Hundred and Sixty-One — Institutional Response to the Paper

Dr. Shen Yue arrived at the laboratory later than usual, her composure carrying a tightness Lin Mushi recognized immediately as something more personal than the paper's broader scientific reception had previously produced.

"University meeting," she said, before he'd even asked, settling into her station with more force than the gesture required. "The administration would like me to issue a clarifying statement."

"Clarifying what, exactly?"

"That's the interesting part. They can't quite articulate it precisely, because the paper itself is technically unimpeachable. The methodology holds. The data holds. Nothing in the published work contains an error they can point to and demand corrected." She pulled up her notes from the meeting, her tone carrying a specific, controlled frustration. "What they actually want is for the paper to feel less consequential than it currently does. They used the word 'measured' four times in a twenty-minute conversation, as though repetition alone might retroactively make my findings smaller."

"What did you tell them?"

"That I'd consider their feedback carefully, and that any clarifying statement would need to remain scientifically accurate, which meant it likely wouldn't say what they were hoping it would say." Something dry entered her voice. "They didn't love that answer. They also didn't have any actual grounds to force the issue. The paper passed peer review. My data is sound. Discomfort isn't a valid basis for retraction, and we both know it."

---

He sat with the situation, understanding, with growing clarity, that this represented something distinct from the paper's broader scientific reception — not a question of whether the findings held up to scrutiny, but a considerably more personal, institutional pressure now settling onto Dr. Shen Yue specifically.

"How worried should I be for you?" he asked directly.

She considered the question seriously rather than deflecting it. "Not urgently. The university can't force a retraction over discomfort alone, and my tenure position gives me more protection than an early-career researcher would have in the same situation. But I won't pretend this is nothing. There's a specific, uncomfortable weight to being the person whose careful, correct work is making an institution nervous, even when the institution has no legitimate grounds to act on that nervousness."

"Is there anything I can do?"

"Not directly, I don't think. This is precisely the kind of personal cost I warned you about, months ago, when I first showed you the draft. I told you then that this wasn't neutral for either of us. I think this is what that cost actually looks like, in its early, manageable form." She met his eyes with her usual steady directness. "I'd rather carry this honestly than have you feel responsible for protecting me from something I chose to walk into with my eyes open."

---

He raised the development with the review preparation group that same week, wanting the case they were building to account honestly for exactly this kind of consequence.

"This is the first genuinely personal cost any of us has faced from the broader disclosure process," he said. "I think it's worth including in what we're preparing for the review — not as evidence of danger, but as evidence that we're navigating real institutional friction honestly, rather than pretending the path forward is costless."

*I agree this is worth documenting,* Meen-4 signaled. *I would also note something relevant to Dr. Shen Yue's specific situation. The Collective's own governance framework places considerable value on how a civilization treats individuals who advance genuinely difficult, disruptive truths. If this world's institutions ultimately protect her rather than punish her, that outcome itself becomes meaningful evidence — proof that even under real pressure, the systems here hold to something resembling integrity.*

Lin Mushi found himself sitting with that framing for a long while afterward, understanding, with a specific, sobering clarity, that Dr. Shen Yue's quiet, personal institutional struggle had just become, without her ever intending it, one more thread woven into the considerably larger case an entire civilization's future might eventually depend on.

He wrote about it that evening, careful and attentive.

> *Dr. Shen Yue is facing real institutional pressure now — not retraction, not danger, but the specific discomfort of an administration that would prefer her correct, careful work simply hadn't happened. She's handling it with the same steady rigor she brings to everything. I think how this resolves matters more than any of us initially realized.*
