# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter One Hundred and Eighty-One — Wei Canglan's Sister

Mei Lan's message arrived through Chen Yufei's network on a Tuesday, three days after the Northern Peak's ultimatum, and it landed with a different kind of weight than anything else in the past several weeks.

She had not gone through her brother to reach them. She had found Chen Yufei's network independently, which Chen Yufei noted with quiet respect — a seventeen-year-old, navigating informal practitioner channels she had no formal standing in, to deliver a message she'd clearly decided needed to be delivered directly.

*I'm Wei Canglan's sister. I don't want anything from you, and I'm not writing to make anyone feel guilty. I just want you to know the actual situation, because I don't think anyone tells you the actual situation when it's not convenient.*

*My parents are renting a single room now. One room, the three of us before my brother left, now just the two of them. My mother got a job at a textile shop — her first job in twenty years, because she stopped working when she married my father and the Court's stipend covered everything. She's fifty-three. Her hands hurt by the end of every shift. She doesn't complain about it, which somehow makes it worse to watch.*

*My father doesn't talk about my brother. Not in anger — he just doesn't bring him up, which I think is its own kind of pain that doesn't have a name.*

*I'm not writing to punish anyone or make you feel bad enough to do something about it. I just think when people make decisions that affect other people, the other people deserve to at least be seen, not just exist as an abstract 'cost' in someone's calculation. So: this is what the cost actually looks like. Not a number. My mother's hands. My father's silence. A single room.*

Lin Mushi read this standing in his kitchen, and read it again sitting down.

He did not respond immediately. He thought about Chen Yufei's earlier counsel — let Wei Canglan come to him on his own terms, don't reach out preemptively in a way that risks making him feel like a charity case. But this wasn't from Wei Canglan. This was from someone outside that careful arrangement, someone who had made her own deliberate choice to be seen and to make her family seen, without asking for anything specific in return.

He thought about what an appropriate response actually looked like — not performance, not an apology that centered his own guilt over her family's actual situation, not a defense of the decision that had led here.

He wrote back, simply: *Thank you for telling me. You're right that I should know this, not as an abstraction. I'm not going to pretend I have an easy way to fix it. I want to ask, with full understanding that you don't owe me an answer: would your family accept help, if it were offered in a way that didn't require anyone to perform gratitude or explain themselves to anyone?*

Mei Lan's reply came two days later, briefer: *I don't know. I'll think about it. Thank you for not arguing with me.*

He sat with this.

He thought about quiet, structural help — not a public gesture, not something that would put Wei Canglan in the position of having to acknowledge or explain a benefactor's intervention to a family already navigating enough difficult silence. He thought about the noodle money his own parents sent him, the specific dignity of receiving help that didn't require performance.

He made a decision.

He transferred money to the family's account directly — anonymously, through a channel Chen Yufei helped him route so it couldn't be easily traced back, enough to cover several months of the rent gap between what the single room cost and what his mother's new wages could fully cover.

He did not tell Wei Canglan.

He told Chen Yufei, because he needed her judgment on whether the anonymity was actually kind or actually just convenient for his own conscience.

"It's kind," she said, after thinking about it carefully. "As long as you understand that if he ever finds out, he might feel exactly the complicated mixture of gratitude and resentment that gifts given without permission tend to produce. You're taking that risk knowingly, which is different from being naive about it."

"I understand," he said. "I think the alternative — asking his permission, which would require him to either accept charity from someone he's already in a complicated relationship with, or refuse help his family needs out of pride — is worse."

Chen Yufei nodded slowly.

"I think you're right," she said. "I also think you should be ready for the day it comes out anyway. These things usually do."

She was right about that too, eventually.

But that day was still some weeks away.
