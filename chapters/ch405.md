# FORMICA INFINITUM

## Chapter 405 — The Pelagic Archive Request

Drafting the request took three days, considerably longer than Lin Mushi had expected for a single formal document, though Meen-4 had warned him, from the start, that a request touching the Archive's classified contents would require a different register entirely than anything he'd submitted before.

"This is not the Opening Submission," she reminded him, on the second day, when he'd grown visibly frustrated with a paragraph he'd already rewritten four times. "That document argued for your civilization's continued existence. This one is arguing for access to something the Collective has specifically, deliberately gated. Different audience. Different standard of justification."

"Then help me get the standard right."

They built it, in the end, around a single central claim, stated as plainly as he could manage without either overselling its certainty or underselling its importance. The Archive, if it held the complete documentation of Veth-Karun's dissolution that Collective records suggested it did, was essential to finishing an analysis he had already demonstrated, through months of careful work, could meaningfully advance Void Lattice coherence restoration theory. He was not requesting access out of curiosity. He was requesting it because the review itself had validated the value of the work he intended to continue, and because reaching Formless Scale — a threshold he had not yet crossed, and did not intend to rush toward — safely and responsibly would require an understanding of Veth-Karun's failure more complete than the clinical fragments he currently possessed.

He did not ask for unrestricted access. He requested a scoped review, specific sections relevant to the dissolution's coherence mechanics, with whatever additional oversight the Collective judged necessary attached to the grant.

"Reasonable requests are harder to refuse outright," Meen-4 said, reading the final draft. "Not impossible. But harder. You are giving them a request narrow enough that denying it entirely requires them to explain, specifically, what about even a scoped review concerns them. That is a more difficult position for an institution to hold than simply saying no to something broad and open-ended."

He submitted it through the Witness Node on the morning of the fourth day, and the acknowledgment arrived within hours — faster than he expected, though he had, by now, learned not to read speed of acknowledgment as any indication of the eventual answer.

*Request received. Under formal review. Timeline: sixty days, standard for requests of this classification.*

Sixty days.

He read the number twice, feeling something settle into a shape he was slowly getting better at accepting — not the frantic uncertainty of the review itself, but a quieter, more patient kind of waiting, the kind that came from having already survived something considerably harder and understanding, now, that not every closed door required the same urgency to force open.

"Sixty days is not unreasonable," Meen-4 said, watching him process the timeline. "For context, requests of comparable classification have, historically, taken considerably longer. I would treat this as a genuinely open question rather than a foregone conclusion in either direction."

"I can wait sixty days," he said, and found, saying it, that he meant it more easily than he would have expected a few months earlier. Three years of accumulated patience — patience learned the hard way, through node stabilizations and stubborn notation symbols and years spent building something slowly instead of forcing it — had taught him something about the difference between urgency that served a purpose and urgency that simply exhausted him without changing the outcome.

He filed the request's acknowledgment in the notebook, alongside the growing list of things the review's conclusion had opened rather than closed — the quarterly reporting rhythm just beginning, the Stellar Scale curriculum he hadn't yet fully explored, the off-world installation still sitting, patient and unexplored, at the edge of the solar system.

"One door at a time," he said, more to himself than to Meen-4.

"That has always been your approach," she said. "I see no reason for the Archive to become the exception."

He closed the notebook, and turned his attention, for the moment, back toward the ordinary work still waiting for him — Circuit diagnostics, node monitoring, the daily maintenance of a civilization that had, against considerable odds, survived long enough to keep needing exactly this kind of unglamorous, patient care.
