# FORMICA INFINITUM
## Chapter Eighty-Five — Chen Yufei's History

Before midnight, between the tea house and the Strata, there was an hour. They spent it in a noodle shop two streets from the store — not Xiao Dong's place, a different one, with fluorescent lighting that had never aspired to atmosphere and broth that was better than the room deserved.

He had asked. She had been surprised, he thought, that he asked at all — surprised that the question was genuine rather than strategic. But he had been thinking about the seventh audio file since he wrote the notes about it: *the debt is specific, owed to specific people.* Chen Yufei was one of those people. Understanding what she was carrying was not simply interpersonally courteous. It was necessary.

So he asked how she came to be here.

---

She had discovered her cultivation ability at seventeen, which was later than most practitioners identified. The standard Iron Meridian pathway was family transmission — most members came through lineages — but she'd come through what the Court called *spontaneous emergence*, a term she used with the slight neutrality of someone who'd had years to decide what they thought about it.

"Spontaneous emergence," he said.

"I kept feeling — disturbances. In rooms. In people." She turned her noodle bowl around. "At first I thought I was becoming anxious. Then I thought I was becoming psychic, which I was horrified by, because I was in my second year of an engineering degree and had absolutely no interest in being psychic." She paused. "Then a practitioner in my social circle noticed me noticing things I shouldn't be able to notice, and made a discreet introduction to the Court."

He ate his noodles. He thought about how he had explained his own emerging perception to himself across forty-seven notebooks, and how different her entry into the same experience had been — social, witnessed, someone with context arriving to name it.

"What did the Court tell you it was?" he asked.

"Meridian sensitivity. Natural attunement to the Qi channels in the environment." She looked at the table. "Which is true, in a sense. The mechanism they described—"

"Was wrong."

"Was wrong," she agreed. "But it worked. Training under the Iron Meridian framework produced real results. I progressed quickly." A pause. "Quickly enough that some senior practitioners were uncomfortable about it."

"Because?"

"Because quick progress without lineage is suspicious in a hierarchical system. It implies either cheating or a hidden connection. Neither of which was true, but suspicion doesn't require truth."

He thought about how this would have felt at twenty-nine — nine years in, skill progressing faster than the institution was comfortable with, no lineage to explain it, no politics sophisticated enough to manage it. He was only beginning to understand the human cultivation world's organizational dynamics, and he was already finding them exhausting from the outside.

"Is that why you're operating independently?" he asked.

"Partially." She ate for a moment. "I requested independent operational status three years ago. The Court agreed, conditionally — my node-monitoring work was valuable enough that they wanted it continued. I got independence in exchange for continuing the assignment."

"The Wuhou node."

"Yes." She looked up. "Which I then watched die on my watch, because a twenty-two-year-old who reads physics papers in convenience stores was unknowingly drawing from it at night."

"I'm sorry about the node," he said.

"I know." She said it without resentment. She had said it before, in different words. "The thing is, the node was going to need attention regardless. The network in this area has been under-maintained for forty years. The Iron Meridian array above the mah-jong hall takes more than it puts back. The death of the Wuhou node was either going to happen this year or in three years." A pause. "I told my superiors it was geological. I think I knew it wasn't."

He looked at her.

"I was looking for an excuse to investigate further," she said. "The anomaly gave me one." She turned the bowl again. "I didn't expect the investigation to find what it found."

"What did you expect to find?"

She thought about this seriously, the way she thought about most things. "A senior practitioner from a sect I didn't know. Someone with lineage I couldn't trace. Something explainable in terms of the world I already understood." A pause. "Not a person who treats an alien notation system like a code-breaking problem and ends up in the basement of history."

He ate the rest of his noodles.

"The engineering degree," he said. "You didn't finish it either."

"I was four months from graduating." Something crossed her face that wasn't quite resignation. "The cultivation world is not compatible with a conventional professional schedule."

He thought about the night shifts, and the notebooks under the counter, and the three years of physics papers he'd read by phone-light while restocking the drinks case.

"No," he said. "It isn't."

They paid. They walked to the store in the December night, the city going about its late-evening business around them. He could feel the Strata below the pavement, its steady Qi field independent of the surface's busyness.

She said, walking: "What made you leave university?"

"The curriculum moved too slowly," he said. "And it was teaching me to operate machinery I didn't understand. I wanted to understand the machinery first."

She was quiet for a few steps.

"The machinery," she said. "You meant the universe."

"Yes."

She glanced at him. "And now?"

He considered the Strata ahead, the library he'd entered eleven times, the Keeper who had waited by the door with a quality of patient amusement, the Codex he could now read at nearly seventy percent comprehension.

"And now I'm starting to understand the machinery," he said.

They went inside.
